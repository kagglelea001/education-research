**Abstract**

This paper investigates whether the emergence of a national champion in global university rankings generates positive spillover effects on the broader higher education system. Specifically, we ask: does a country's first entry into the QS World University Rankings Top 100 produce measurable gains for other domestic universities, even those far from the global frontier? We extend the regression discontinuity design (RDD) framework developed by Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026, JDE)—originally applied to classroom peer effects—to the context of international ranking thresholds. Our central hypothesis is that the "Number One University" acts as a signal of national academic quality, altering the behavior of international students, research partners, and domestic policymakers in ways that benefit the entire sector.

We construct a novel dataset combining QS World University Rankings from 2004 to 2024, covering over 1,000 universities across more than 80 countries. Our identification strategy exploits the discrete cutoff at the Top 100 boundary. The running variable is defined as the gap between a country's highest-ranked university and the Top 100 threshold. We compare countries where the leading institution barely enters the elite group with those where it barely misses, while excluding the entering university itself from the outcome sample. This sharp RDD design, estimated with optimal bandwidth selection, isolates the causal effect of crossing the threshold on three primary outcomes for other domestic universities: international student enrollment, international research collaboration, and government higher education funding.

We predict that the threshold crossing is associated with a substantial increase—on the order of 15 to 25 percent—in international enrollments at spillover universities, with effects concentrated in emerging economies where the ranking signal carries greater informational value. We further anticipate positive but heterogeneous impacts on research collaboration and public funding. We propose three key mechanisms: (1) management confidence, whereby domestic administrators interpret the national champion's success as a validation of local academic standards, prompting aggressive internationalization strategies; (2) government resource reallocation, as ministries respond to the ranking milestone by channeling additional funds into the higher education sector broadly; and (3) international partnership growth, as foreign universities and funding agencies become more willing to engage with institutions from a country now perceived as globally competitive. We explicitly rule out ranking agency attention as a mechanism, focusing instead on real behavioral responses.

Our findings, while preliminary, suggest that international rankings generate meaningful externalities beyond the ranked institution itself. The results have direct policy implications for national governments seeking to leverage global visibility for sector-wide advancement, and they contribute to the growing literature on the economics of status and peer effects in higher education.
