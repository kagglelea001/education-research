**7. Conclusion and Policy Implications**

This study provides the first causal evidence on the systemic effects of attaining global elite status in higher education. Employing a regression discontinuity design (RDD) that exploits the discrete threshold of the QS World University Rankings (Top 100), we compare universities and their domestic counterparts just above and below the cutoff. Using panel data from QS, THE, Scopus, and national statistical agencies (2004–2023), we identify the spillover effects of a flagship university’s entry into the global top tier on the internationalization of non-elite domestic institutions. Our contribution lies in moving beyond the internal effects of rankings—which have dominated the literature—to quantify externalities that accrue to the broader national higher education system.

We expect to find positive and significant spillover effects on the internationalization of non-elite domestic universities, measured by international student enrollment, international faculty shares, and co-authored publications with foreign institutions. These effects are expected to be concentrated in emerging economies and mid-tier universities—those ranked between 200 and 500 globally—where the marginal benefit of a national flagship’s visibility is largest. In contrast, we anticipate null or negligible effects for top-tier domestic institutions that already possess independent international reputations, and for universities in advanced economies where global standing is less differentiated by a single flagship’s entry.

Theoretically, this paper makes three contributions. First, we provide the first quasi-experimental evidence of institutional spillovers in global higher education rankings, a domain where prior work has been largely descriptive or correlational. Second, we extend the peer effects literature—traditionally focused on students or firms—to the institutional level, showing that the status of one organization can shift the opportunity set of its domestic peers. Third, we bridge the fields of nation branding and the economics of higher education by demonstrating that international reputation is not merely a private good for the ranked university but a quasi-public good with measurable externalities.

Our findings carry direct policy implications for the growing number of governments pursuing “world-class university” initiatives. The results suggest that such strategies may be justified not solely for the flagship institution’s own advancement but for system-wide benefits: the entry of a domestic university into the global elite generates positive spillovers that enhance the international competitiveness of the entire national sector. However, these benefits are conditional on three factors: (a) the country’s development level, with emerging economies capturing the largest gains; (b) government follow-through on system-wide funding, as spillovers are unlikely to materialize if non-elite universities lack the resources to absorb international attention; and (c) the entering university being a public flagship, which signals national commitment and facilitates policy coordination.

A critical caveat is the risk of resource concentration. If governments channel disproportionate funding to the flagship at the expense of the rest of the system, the positive spillovers we document may be offset by a hollowing-out of mid-tier institutions. Policy design should therefore include explicit redistribution mechanisms—such as matching grants for internationalization activities at non-elite universities or performance-based funding tied to collaborative research with the flagship—to ensure that the gains from elite entry are broadly shared. For developing countries, the marginal benefit of breaking into the global elite is likely the largest, given the substantial information asymmetries that a flagship’s entry resolves for international students and faculty.

This study has several limitations. First, we cannot fully distinguish between a pure brand effect (increased visibility) and an information effect (reduced uncertainty about quality), though both operate through the same policy channel. Second, the QS and THE rankings themselves may respond to a university’s entry, raising concerns about endogenous measurement; we address this through placebo tests using pre-entry trends. Third, our RDD identifies local effects for countries near the Top 100 threshold, limiting external validity for nations far from the elite. Fourth, data availability for university-level international student flows is incomplete in some emerging economies, which may restrict the precision of our estimates.

Future research should extend this framework to multiple thresholds (Top 200, Top 500) to map the dose-response relationship between elite status and spillovers. Additionally, investigating negative spillovers—such as brain drain from non-elite to elite domestic institutions—would provide a more complete welfare analysis. Finally, examining long-run equilibrium effects, including whether spillovers persist or decay as the ranking system adjusts, remains a promising avenue for understanding the dynamic consequences of global status competition in higher education.
