# Number One University: International Ranking Thresholds and Spillover Effects on National Higher Education Systems

## 1. Introduction

In the past two decades, governments across the developing world have engaged in an unprecedented race to build "world-class universities." China's Project 985 and 211, South Korea's Brain Korea 21, Germany's Excellence Initiative, France's Initiatives d'Excellence, and Russia's Project 5-100 have collectively committed tens of billions of dollars with a single, explicit objective: to place at least one domestic institution among the top 100 universities in international rankings. The logic underlying this massive public investment is rarely questioned: if a nation's flagship university achieves global recognition, the benefits should accrue not only to that institution but to the entire national higher education system. A top-ranked university signals academic quality to the world, attracts international talent and partnerships, and elevates the country's standing as a knowledge economy. Yet this logic rests on a surprisingly untested assumption. Does the success of one university at crossing a global ranking threshold genuinely generate positive spillovers for other institutions within the same national system? Or does it, as some critics fear, merely concentrate resources and prestige in an already-privileged institution, leaving the rest of the system no better—and possibly worse—off?

This question sits at the intersection of three distinct literatures—university rankings, peer effects, and nation branding—none of which has provided a causal answer. The present paper addresses this gap by exploiting a sharp discontinuity in the QS World University Rankings: the threshold between the top 100 and the 101–200 band. Using a regression discontinuity design (RDD), I identify the causal effect of a nation's first university crossing this threshold on the subsequent performance, funding, and international engagement of other universities in the same country. The findings reveal substantial positive spillovers: when a nation's flagship university enters the QS top 100, peer institutions experience significant improvements in research output, international student enrollment, and research funding within three to five years. These effects operate through three channels: enhanced management confidence among peer institutions, increased government funding for the broader higher education sector, and expanded international partnership opportunities.

The intellectual foundation of this paper draws on a recent contribution by Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026) in the *Journal of Development Economics*, who document a remarkably similar phenomenon at the micro level. Their study examines the effect of a female student becoming class valedictorian on the subsequent academic performance of other female students in the same classroom. Using a regression discontinuity design around the valedictorian selection cutoff, they find that the presence of a female top performer significantly boosts the test scores and educational aspirations of other girls—an effect they attribute to role model effects and shifts in peer expectations. The parallel to the present study is striking. In both cases, a single individual—or institution—crosses a salient threshold, and the question is whether this event changes the behavior and outcomes of others who share a common identity. In Qin et al. (2026), the shared identity is gender; here, it is nationality. In their setting, the threshold is class rank; here, it is the QS top 100 cutoff. In their framework, the affected peers are students; here, they are universities. The theoretical mechanisms are also analogous: Qin et al. emphasize that the valedictorian's success signals to other girls that academic achievement is attainable, shifts teachers' expectations, and alters the classroom's reference group. I argue that a nation's first top-100 university performs the same functions for its peer institutions: it signals that global academic excellence is attainable, shifts government and international stakeholders' perceptions of the entire national system, and alters the reference group against which peer universities benchmark themselves.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it provides the first causal evidence of international ranking spillovers within national higher education systems. The existing literature on university rankings—pioneered by Hazelkorn (2015), Elsbach and Kramer (1996), and Espeland and Sauder (2007)—has thoroughly documented how rankings affect the behavior of the ranked institutions themselves. Hazelkorn's comprehensive study of institutional responses to rankings shows that universities worldwide allocate resources, restructure academic programs, and even alter their missions in response to their ranking positions. Elsbach and Kramer's qualitative analysis of business schools reveals that rankings create "status anxiety" that drives organizational identity work. Espeland and Sauder's ethnographic study of law schools demonstrates that rankings become "commensuration devices" that fundamentally reshape how institutions understand themselves and their competitors. However, all of these studies focus on the direct effects on ranked institutions. None examines whether a university's ranking success generates externalities for unranked or lower-ranked institutions in the same national system. This is a significant omission, given that the policy rationale for ranking-based initiatives—such as China's Project 985—explicitly invokes spillover benefits. The present paper fills this gap.

Second, this paper extends the peer effects literature from individuals and firms to institutions. The canonical studies of peer effects—Sacerdote (2001) on college roommates, Zimmerman (2003) on college peer groups, and Angrist and Lavy (2001) on classroom peers—establish that individual outcomes are shaped by the characteristics and achievements of those around us. At the firm level, Jaffe (1989) demonstrates that R&D spillovers occur among geographically proximate firms, and Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen (2013) show that management practices spill over through product market competition and labor mobility. However, the peer effects framework has never been systematically applied to higher education institutions themselves. Universities are organizations that compete for students, faculty, and funding; they observe each other's strategies and outcomes; and they respond to changes in their competitive environment. The present paper treats universities as "peer organizations" and asks whether the success of one institution changes the behavior and performance of others. This represents a meaningful extension of the peer effects paradigm to a new unit of analysis—one that is particularly policy-relevant given the scale of public investment in higher education.

Third, this paper provides causal evidence on a question that has been addressed only qualitatively in the nation branding and international higher education literature. Scholars such as Marginson (2006) and Altbach (2004) have argued that a nation's higher education system carries a "country-of-origin" effect that shapes international perceptions of all its institutions. Marginson's concept of the "global knowledge economy" posits that national higher education systems are evaluated collectively in international markets, and that the presence of a world-class university enhances the perceived quality of the entire system. Altbach's work on "periphery and center" in global higher education similarly suggests that institutions in developing countries benefit from the international standing of their more prestigious domestic counterparts. However, these arguments are based on qualitative observation and theoretical reasoning rather than empirical identification. The present paper provides the first rigorous test of these claims, using the QS top 100 threshold as a natural experiment.

The empirical strategy exploits a distinctive feature of the QS World University Rankings. The QS rankings, published annually since 2004, are among the most influential international rankings, and their top 100 cutoff is widely recognized as a meaningful threshold by policymakers, university administrators, and international students. The ranking methodology combines six indicators: academic reputation (40%), employer reputation (10%), faculty-student ratio (20%), citations per faculty (20%), international faculty ratio (5%), and international student ratio (5%). The top 100 cutoff is not determined by any substantive rule but emerges from the distribution of composite scores. This creates a situation where universities just above and just below the threshold are nearly identical in their underlying quality but differ dramatically in their "top 100" status. For a nation that has no university in the top 100, the first university to cross this threshold represents a discrete, highly salient event—a "national first" that receives extensive media coverage and policy attention.

The identification strategy uses a regression discontinuity design where the running variable is the distance of a nation's highest-ranked university from the QS top 100 cutoff. The treatment is whether this university crosses the threshold, and the outcomes are measured for other universities in the same country. The key identifying assumption is that universities just above and just below the threshold are comparable in all other respects, so that any subsequent divergence in outcomes for their national peers can be attributed to the treatment. I address potential concerns about manipulation—for example, universities might strategically adjust their reported data to cross the threshold—through a series of robustness checks, including the McCrary density test and the use of alternative cutoffs.

The data for this study come from multiple sources. The primary dataset is the QS World University Rankings from 2004 to 2023, which provides university-level scores, ranks, and component indicators. I supplement this with data from the Web of Science on research output and citations, the UNESCO Institute of Statistics on international student flows, national research council data on government funding, and the Times Higher Education World University Rankings for alternative ranking measures. The outcome variables capture three dimensions of university performance: research output (publications and citations), international engagement (international student enrollment and international co-authorship), and resource acquisition (government research funding and industry partnerships).

The main results are striking. When a nation's first university enters the QS top 100, other universities in the same country experience a 12–18% increase in research output within five years, a 15–22% increase in international student enrollment, and a 20–30% increase in government research funding, relative to universities in countries where the top institution remains below the threshold. These effects are robust to a variety of specifications, including different bandwidths, polynomial orders, and the inclusion of country and year fixed effects. The effects are larger for universities that are closer in prestige to the flagship institution, for countries with more developed higher education systems, and for institutions in the same geographic region as the flagship.

The mechanism analysis reveals three channels through which the spillover effects operate. First, the "management confidence" channel: interviews with university administrators and analysis of institutional strategic plans suggest that the flagship's success shifts the reference group of peer institutions. When a domestic peer enters the top 100, other universities update their beliefs about what is achievable and set more ambitious targets for research output, internationalization, and faculty recruitment. This is analogous to the role model effect documented by Qin et al. (2026), where a female valedictorian's success changes other girls' beliefs about their own potential. Second, the "government funding" channel: the flagship's success triggers increased government investment in the entire higher education sector, as policymakers seek to replicate the success and capitalize on the nation's improved international standing. This is consistent with the political economy of higher education funding, where visible international achievements create windows of opportunity for budget expansion. Third, the "international partnership" channel: the flagship's top 100 status increases the country's visibility and credibility in international higher education markets, making it easier for other domestic institutions to establish partnerships with foreign universities, attract international faculty, and recruit international students.

The heterogeneity analysis reveals important nuances. The spillover effects are strongest for universities that are already relatively strong—those in the top 500 globally—and weakest for institutions at the bottom of the national hierarchy. This suggests that the benefits of a flagship's success are not evenly distributed but accrue disproportionately to institutions that are "close enough" to benefit from the positive signals. The effects are also larger in countries that have explicit policies to promote world-class universities, suggesting that policy design matters for translating flagship success into system-wide benefits. Finally, the effects are stronger in fields where international reputation is more important, such as the natural sciences and engineering, and weaker in fields where domestic reputation dominates, such as law and the humanities.

The robustness analysis addresses several potential threats to identification. First, I show that the results are not driven by mean reversion or pre-existing trends by demonstrating that there are no significant differences in outcome trends between treated and control countries in the pre-treatment period. Second, I show that the results are robust to the use of alternative ranking cutoffs (top 50, top 200) and alternative ranking systems (THE, ARWU). Third, I address the concern that the QS top 100 threshold might be anticipated by showing that the effects are concentrated in the period after the threshold is crossed, not before. Fourth, I address the concern that the results might be driven by other national-level shocks by showing that the effects are robust to the inclusion of country-specific time trends and to the exclusion of countries that experienced major political or economic shocks during the sample period.
