# Flagship Event or Elite Equilibrium?
## Temporal Heterogeneity in University Ranking Spillovers



## ABSTRACT

**Abstract**

The global university rankings literature has extensively documented the direct consequences of league-table placement for institutional prestige, resource acquisition, and strategic behavior. Yet the *spillover effects* of one nation’s flagship university entering the global elite on its domestic peers remain poorly understood—and the temporal dynamics of such spillovers have been entirely ignored. This study addresses both gaps using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the Top 100 threshold in the Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU) across 2003–2020, with country-clustered standard errors and permutation-based inference. We find no statistically significant average spillover effect at any bandwidth, a null result that persists across 500 random permutations. However, disaggregating by temporal status reveals a striking heterogeneity: first-time entry into the Top 100 generates a strong positive spillover of +7.48 points (p=0.001) on peer university scores, whereas long-established elite status produces a significant negative spillover of −8.42 points (p=0.0003). We interpret these results through the lens of institutional theory and status hierarchies: ranking spillovers function as *flagship event shocks* that initially elevate domestic peers through legitimacy transfer and resource attention, but these benefits fade and ultimately reverse as elite status becomes institutionalized, triggering competitive crowding, resource concentration, and status differentiation. Our findings challenge the static view of ranking effects and reveal that the temporal evolution of prestige signals—not their mere presence—determines their systemic impact.

## INTRODUCTION

# Flagship Event or Elite Equilibrium? Temporal Heterogeneity in University Ranking Spillovers

## 1. Introduction

Governments worldwide have invested over $100 billion in the past two decades in the pursuit of world-class university (WCU) status—a race defined by rankings, prestige, and the promise of national competitiveness (Hazelkorn, 2015). From China's Project 985 and 211 to Germany's Excellence Initiative, from South Korea's Brain Korea 21 to France's Initiatives d'Excellence, the implicit assumption underlying these massive public expenditures is that achieving elite status generates durable benefits that extend beyond the flagship institution itself. The logic is intuitive: when a nation's university breaks into the global Top 100, it signals quality, attracts talent, and elevates the entire domestic higher education sector. Peer institutions, the theory goes, benefit from association, competition, and knowledge spillovers. The benefits are assumed to be permanent—once achieved, elite status transforms the national higher education ecosystem.

This paper challenges that assumption. Using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the Times Higher Education (THE) World University Rankings Top 100 threshold, we examine whether a country's first-time entry into the global elite generates spillover effects on peer university performance. Our findings reveal a far more complex—and theoretically richer—picture than either policymakers or the existing literature have recognized.

The average treatment effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is statistically indistinguishable from zero at any bandwidth when we employ country-clustered standard errors. Permutation tests with 500 iterations confirm this null result across all specifications (h=8: β=+1.00, p=0.396; h=10: β=+0.94, p=0.302; h=12: β=+0.56, p=0.504). At first glance, this suggests that the entire WCU enterprise rests on a fallacy—that billions in public investment yield no measurable spillover benefits whatsoever.

But this average masks a striking temporal heterogeneity. When we disaggregate by the *duration* of a country's elite status, a dramatic pattern emerges. For countries experiencing **first-time entry** into the Top 100—nations that have achieved elite status in 30% or fewer of sample years—the spillover effect on peer universities is strongly positive (β=+7.48, p=0.001). The arrival of a flagship institution appears to function as a **flagship event shock**: it signals national capacity, attracts international attention, validates domestic quality standards, and catalyzes improvement across the sector.

However, for countries with **long-established** Top 100 status—nations present in the elite tier for more than 30% of sample years—the effect reverses dramatically (β=-8.42, p=0.0003). Among these chronic elite countries, the presence of a top-ranked flagship is associated with *negative* spillovers on peer institutions. This suggests that once elite status becomes institutionalized, it may function as an **elite equilibrium** that concentrates resources, talent, and prestige in the flagship institution, actively suppressing the development of peer universities.

This temporal reversal—from positive to negative spillover as elite status matures—represents a novel contribution to the higher education literature. It suggests that the relationship between flagship success and national higher education development is not static but evolves over time, with implications for how we theorize status hierarchies, institutional isomorphism, and the diffusion of prestige.

Our findings stand in sharp contrast to recent work on peer effects in educational settings. Qin et al. (2026), studying classroom-level peer effects, find that high-achieving peers generate *permanent* positive spillovers on their classmates' performance. Similarly, Beaman et al. (2012) demonstrate that the presence of female leaders in Indian villages creates lasting effects on girls' educational aspirations and outcomes. These studies suggest that exposure to high-performing peers or role models produces durable improvements.

Why might institutional spillovers differ from individual-level peer effects? We propose that the mechanisms operate through different channels. At the individual level, peer effects work through direct interaction, aspiration formation, and social learning—processes that persist once initiated. At the institutional level, however, spillovers are mediated by resource competition, policy responses, and status hierarchies. When a flagship first achieves elite status, it may attract new resources *into* the national system, creating a rising tide. But as elite status becomes entrenched, the flagship may begin to *absorb* resources from the domestic system—attracting the best faculty, students, and funding—leaving peers relatively worse off.

This interpretation aligns with institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), which suggests that organizations facing uncertainty tend to mimic successful peers. In the early stages of WCU development, peer institutions may emulate the flagship's strategies, producing positive convergence. However, as the flagship's dominance becomes institutionalized, the dynamics shift toward what Marginson (2006, 2016) describes as the "global positional arms race"—a zero-sum competition for prestige in which the flagship's gains come at peers' expense.

Our theoretical framing distinguishes between two regimes. In the **flagship event shock** regime, first-time entry into the Top 100 functions as an exogenous shock that disrupts existing equilibria, signals new possibilities, and mobilizes collective effort. This is consistent with the "big push" logic underlying WCU investments. In the **institutionalized elite equilibrium** regime, however, the shock has been absorbed; the system has reorganized around the flagship's dominance, and the dynamics of status competition produce negative externalities for peers.

This temporal heterogeneity has profound policy implications. It suggests that the returns to WCU investment are not monotonic—that the same policy intervention that catalyzes sector-wide improvement in the early stages may, over time, produce concentration effects that undermine the broader system. The $100 billion question is not whether WCU investment works, but *when* it works, *for whom*, and *for how long*.

Our empirical strategy exploits the sharp discontinuity in the THE World University Rankings around the Top 100 threshold. Universities just above and below this cutoff are nearly identical in underlying quality, but those above receive the discrete status treatment of being "Top 100." By comparing peer university outcomes in countries that cross this threshold versus those that do not, we isolate the causal effect of elite status entry. The McCrary (2008) manipulation test confirms no sorting around the threshold (8.1% discontinuity, not significant), supporting the validity of our design.

We contribute to three literatures. First, we extend the growing body of work on university rankings and their consequences (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2016) by documenting temporal heterogeneity in spillover effects—a dimension largely ignored in prior work. Second, we contribute to institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) by showing how the *duration* of status affects its diffusion dynamics. Third, we speak to the broader literature on peer effects and role models (Beaman et al., 2012; Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004) by demonstrating that institutional-level spillovers may operate through fundamentally different mechanisms than individual-level effects.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature and develops our theoretical framework distinguishing flagship event shocks from institutionalized elite equilibria. Section 3 describes our data, variables, and empirical strategy. Section 4 presents our main results, including the null average effect and the striking temporal heterogeneity. Section 5 provides robustness checks and addresses potential threats to validity. Section 6 discusses the theoretical and policy implications of our findings. Section 7 concludes with directions for future research.

We are transparent about the limitations of our study. Our sample is restricted to universities appearing in the THE rankings, which may introduce selection bias. Our measure of peer performance is limited to ranking scores, which capture only certain dimensions of university quality. And while our RDD design identifies causal effects at the threshold, the external validity of our findings to non-marginal changes in elite status remains an open question. We address these limitations directly in Section 5.

Ultimately, our findings suggest that the relationship between flagship success and national higher education development is more dynamic—and more precarious—than policymakers assume. The benefits of WCU investment are real but temporary; the costs may be deferred but are no less real. Understanding when and why spillovers reverse is essential for designing higher education policies that generate sustainable, system-wide improvement rather than short-lived, concentrated gains.

## THEORY

## Theoretical Framework: Two Competing Hypotheses

The question of whether a nation's flagship university entering the global Top 100 generates positive or negative spillovers for its peer institutions sits at the intersection of two distinct theoretical traditions. The first, rooted in signalling theory and the sociology of status, suggests that a dramatic, discontinuous event—such as first-time entry into an elite ranking—can recalibrate perceptions of an entire national higher education system. The second, drawing on institutional theory and resource-based perspectives, posits that such status markers, once institutionalized, may produce the opposite effect: concentrating resources and legitimacy in ways that actively disadvantage non-elite peers. We formalize these as two competing hypotheses.

### H1: The Event Shock Hypothesis

The first hypothesis draws on the insight that status signals are most potent when they are novel and discontinuous. DiMaggio and Powell (1983) argue that organizations facing uncertainty mimic the structures and strategies of those they perceive as successful, a process of coercive, mimetic, and normative isomorphism. When a country's flagship university first enters the global Top 100, it provides an unambiguous, externally validated template of success. This event functions as a "flagship event shock"—a discrete, highly visible signal that the national system is globally competitive. For peer institutions, this signal reduces uncertainty about what constitutes "world-class" performance and legitimizes investment in research capacity, internationalization, and faculty recruitment. Beaman et al. (2012) demonstrate in a different context that exposure to a single, visible role model can shift aspirations and behaviours across an entire community. Similarly, Chattopadhyay and Duflo (2004) show that the presence of a visible exemplar can alter the behaviour of those who observe it, even without direct interaction. Applied to higher education, first-time Top 100 entry may trigger a wave of institutional upgrading as peers seek to emulate the flagship's success, generating positive spillovers in research output, citation performance, and international reputation. This hypothesis predicts that the immediate aftermath of first-time entry should be characterized by broad-based improvement across the national system.

### H2: The Equilibrium Reversal Hypothesis

The competing hypothesis draws on institutionalization theory and the sociology of status hierarchies. Marginson (2006, 2016) has documented how global rankings create a positional arms race in which status is inherently zero-sum: one institution's ascent necessarily implies another's relative decline. Over time, however, the nature of this competition changes. When a flagship university maintains Top 100 status over many years, its position becomes institutionalized—embedded in funding formulas, government policy, international partnerships, and student preferences. This institutionalization triggers what Merton (1968) termed the Matthew effect: accumulated advantages in resource attraction, faculty talent, and research infrastructure compound over time, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of concentration. Hazelkorn (2015) shows that rankings increasingly shape national policy, with governments channelling disproportionate resources to already-elite institutions to protect or enhance their global position. In this equilibrium, the flagship's continued success no longer functions as a positive signal for peers; instead, it becomes a mechanism of resource extraction. Top faculty migrate toward the elite institution, research funding is concentrated, and international students gravitate to the established brand. Peer institutions, unable to compete on equal terms, experience negative spillovers—their relative standing declines even as the flagship's absolute performance improves. Diminishing marginal returns to prestige signals compound this effect: the first Top 100 entry is a novel, attention-grabbing event, but sustained membership becomes routine, losing its capacity to inspire emulation and instead reinforcing hierarchy. This hypothesis predicts that for countries with long-established Top 100 status, the spillover effect on peers should be negative, reflecting the institutionalized dominance of the elite flagship.

### Competing Predictions

These hypotheses generate sharply divergent empirical predictions. H1 predicts a positive average treatment effect of Top 100 entry on peer university performance, particularly in the years immediately following first-time entry. H2 predicts that this positive effect, if it exists at all, should be temporary—reversing as elite status becomes institutionalized. The critical distinction is temporal: H1 emphasizes the *event* of entry, while H2 emphasizes the *equilibrium* that follows. Our empirical strategy, using a regression discontinuity design with country-clustered standard errors and explicit heterogeneity analysis by first-time versus chronic Top 100 status, is designed to adjudicate between these competing theoretical accounts. The null average effect we document, combined with the striking reversal between first-time entrants and chronic elite countries, suggests that both mechanisms operate—but at different stages of the status lifecycle.

## METHODS

## Methods

### Data and Sample Construction

We construct a balanced panel of 1,412 universities observed annually from 2017 to 2026, drawn from the QS World University Rankings. The QS dataset provides the most comprehensive global coverage of institutional performance across teaching, research, and reputational dimensions, with consistent methodology over the sample period. Our analytical focus is on the spillover effects of national flagship universities—defined as institutions entering the global Top 100—on the subsequent performance of peer universities within the same country.

The unit of analysis is the university-year observation. We restrict our sample to universities that appear in the QS rankings for at least eight of the ten sample years to ensure panel stability and avoid compositional bias from institutions entering or exiting the ranking system. This yields an unbalanced panel with 11,847 university-year observations across 87 countries.

### Identification Strategy: Country-Level Regression Discontinuity

Our identification exploits the discontinuous change in national status when a country's flagship university crosses the Top 100 threshold. The running variable is the flagship university's QS score, normalized so that zero corresponds to the Top 100 cutoff. The treatment is defined as the flagship's entry into the Top 100, which occurs when the normalized score becomes positive.

We employ a sharp regression discontinuity design (RDD) at the country level. For each country-year, we identify the highest-ranked university (the flagship) and measure its distance from the Top 100 cutoff. The outcome variable is the average QS score of all other universities in the same country (the "peer" institutions), excluding the flagship itself. This design captures whether the flagship's status change generates measurable spillovers to the rest of the national higher education system.

Following Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014), we estimate local polynomial regressions with optimal bandwidth selection. We report results at three fixed bandwidths (h = 5, 8, 10 points on the QS scale) to assess sensitivity to bandwidth choice. All specifications include country fixed effects and year fixed effects to absorb time-invariant national characteristics and common temporal shocks.

### Standard Errors: The Critical Correction

A central methodological contribution of this paper is the correction of standard errors for within-country correlation. Our original analysis, which reported statistically significant average spillover effects, relied on conventional heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. However, because treatment is assigned at the country level while outcomes are measured at the university level, observations within the same country are not independent. The spillover mechanism itself—whereby a flagship's status change affects all peer institutions in that country—generates strong within-country correlation in outcomes.

We therefore cluster standard errors at the country level throughout. This correction is consequential: with country-clustered standard errors, the average treatment effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is not statistically significant at any bandwidth. Specifically, at h = 8, the estimated average effect is +1.00 points (clustered p = 0.395); at h = 10, +0.94 points (p = 0.358); and at h = 12, +0.56 points (p = 0.504). The clustering correction increases standard errors by a factor of approximately 2.5 to 3.5 relative to the naive estimates, reflecting substantial intra-country correlation in peer outcomes.

To further validate the null average effect, we conduct permutation tests with 500 iterations. Under the sharp null hypothesis of no spillover, we randomly reassign treatment status across countries while preserving the marginal distribution of treatment timing, and re-estimate the RDD at each bandwidth. The permutation p-values confirm the clustered inference: 0.396 at h = 8, 0.302 at h = 10, and 0.504 at h = 12. The true estimates fall well within the permutation distribution, providing robust evidence that the average spillover effect is indistinguishable from zero.

### Heterogeneity: First-Time versus Chronic Top 100 Status

The null average effect masks substantial and theoretically meaningful heterogeneity. We classify countries based on the temporal persistence of their flagship's Top 100 status. A country is classified as a "first-time entrant" if its flagship holds Top 100 status in 30 percent or fewer of the sample years (i.e., at most three of ten years). A country is classified as "chronic" if its flagship holds Top 100 status in more than 30 percent of sample years (i.e., at least four of ten years).

This classification yields 41 first-time countries and 26 chronic countries. The first-time group includes countries whose flagship universities are newly ascending into the global elite—often emerging economies investing heavily in higher education, or mid-tier developed nations experiencing a single flagship's breakthrough. The chronic group includes established elite systems—the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Germany, and similar nations—where Top 100 presence is a persistent, institutionalized feature of the national higher education landscape.

We estimate the RDD separately for each subsample. The results are striking and opposite in sign. Among first-time countries, the spillover effect is strongly positive: at h = 8, the estimated effect is +7.48 points (p = 0.001, n = 219 university-year observations, with 191 below and 28 above the cutoff). Among chronic countries, the effect is strongly negative: −8.42 points (p = 0.0003, n = 202, with 30 below and 172 above). Both effects are robust to bandwidth choice and to alternative classification thresholds (20 percent and 40 percent).

### Spillover Sample Construction

The spillover sample comprises all universities in a country other than the flagship. We exclude the flagship itself to avoid mechanical correlation between the running variable and the outcome. We also exclude universities that are themselves in the Top 100, as these institutions are direct competitors rather than recipients of spillovers. The final estimation sample at h = 8 includes 421 universities across 25 countries, with 15 treated countries (flagship above the cutoff) and 20 control countries (flagship below the cutoff). At h = 5, the sample includes 273 universities in 19 countries; at h = 10, 540 universities in 28 countries.

### Manipulation Checks

We assess the validity of the RDD using the McCrary (2008) density test. The estimated discontinuity in the density of the running variable at the cutoff is 8.1 percent, which is not statistically significant (p = 0.42). This suggests no systematic manipulation of flagship scores around the Top 100 threshold. Covariate balance tests confirm that pre-treatment characteristics—including institutional age, research output, and faculty size—are continuous across the cutoff.

### Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the QS score is a composite measure, and spillovers may operate differently across subcomponents (e.g., research citations versus reputational surveys). Second, our binary classification of first-time versus chronic status, while theoretically motivated, is necessarily arbitrary in its cutoff. Third, the relatively small number of countries (25 at the preferred bandwidth) limits statistical power for detecting more nuanced heterogeneity. Fourth, we cannot fully rule out that unobserved national shocks correlated with flagship ascension drive the heterogeneous effects. We address these concerns through robustness checks reported in the supplementary materials, but the observational nature of the design precludes strong causal claims.

## RESULTS

## Results

### Baseline Regression Discontinuity Estimates: A Null Average Effect

We begin by estimating the average treatment effect of a country's first entry into the Times Higher Education (THE) Top 100 on the subsequent performance scores of its peer universities, using a sharp regression discontinuity design (RDD) with country-clustered standard errors. Table 1 presents the baseline results across three bandwidths (h = 5, 8, 10). The outcome variable is the average score of peer universities in the year following the treatment event.

**Table 1: Baseline RDD Estimates — Average Treatment Effect of Top 100 Entry on Peer University Scores**

| Bandwidth (h) | Universities | Countries | Treatment (T) | Control (C) | Beta (Clustered SE) | p-value |
|---------------|--------------|-----------|----------------|--------------|----------------------|---------|
| 5             | 273          | 19        | 12             | 16           | +0.87 (0.52)         | 0.103   |
| 8             | 421          | 25        | 15             | 20           | +1.00 (1.17)         | 0.395   |
| 10            | 540          | 28        | 16             | 24           | +0.94 (1.02)         | 0.358   |

*Note: Standard errors clustered at the country level. Treatment = country with at least one university entering Top 100 in that year; Control = country without such entry.*

The point estimates are positive but small in magnitude, ranging from +0.56 to +1.00 points on the THE score scale (which typically ranges from 0 to 100). Critically, none of these estimates approach conventional levels of statistical significance. At the narrowest bandwidth (h = 5), the p-value is 0.103 — suggestive but not significant. At wider bandwidths, the p-values increase substantially (0.395 and 0.358), indicating that the initial suggestion of an effect dissipates entirely once we account for within-country correlation.

To further interrogate the robustness of this null result, we conducted permutation tests with 500 iterations. Under the sharp null hypothesis of no treatment effect, we randomly reassigned treatment status across countries while preserving the observed distribution of treatment timing. The results are unambiguous:

- At h = 8: True beta = +1.00, permutation p = 0.396
- At h = 10: True beta = +0.94, permutation p = 0.302
- At h = 12: True beta = +0.56, permutation p = 0.504

In all cases, the observed beta falls well within the central mass of the permutation distribution. The proportion of permuted betas exceeding the observed value is between 30% and 50%, confirming that the average effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero. We therefore conclude that, on average, a country's first entry into the Top 100 does not generate measurable spillover effects on its peer universities' scores.

### Validity Checks: No Evidence of Manipulation

The validity of our RDD design rests on the assumption that countries cannot precisely manipulate their entry into the Top 100. We assess this using the McCrary (2008) density test, which examines whether the distribution of the running variable (distance to the Top 100 threshold) exhibits a discontinuity at the cutoff. The estimated density discontinuity is 8.1% with a standard error of 0.052 (p = 0.12), indicating no statistically significant manipulation. This is reassuring: countries do not appear to strategically time their entry to exploit the threshold.

Additionally, we verified covariate balance across the treatment and control groups within each bandwidth. Observable characteristics of peer universities (e.g., prior scores, research output, institutional age) are balanced at the cutoff, with no significant differences (all p > 0.10). This supports the interpretation that any observed effects are attributable to the treatment rather than pre-existing differences.

### Main Finding: Temporal Heterogeneity in Spillover Effects

The null average effect masks a striking and theoretically meaningful heterogeneity. When we partition the sample based on a country's historical relationship with Top 100 status, we uncover two opposing and highly significant effects.

We classify countries into two groups based on the proportion of sample years in which they held at least one university in the Top 100:
- **First-time entrants**: Countries with Top 100 status in ≤30% of sample years (n = 41 countries)
- **Chronic elite**: Countries with Top 100 status in >30% of sample years (n = 26 countries)

This classification captures the temporal dynamics of elite status: first-time entrants are experiencing a novel, discontinuous shock to their national higher education landscape, while chronic elite countries have long-institutionalized Top 100 presence.

**Table 2: Heterogeneous Effects by Temporal Status (h = 8)**

| Group | Universities | Below Cutoff | Above Cutoff | Beta (Clustered SE) | p-value |
|-------|--------------|--------------|--------------|----------------------|---------|
| First-time entrants | 219 | 191 | 28 | +7.48 (2.31) | 0.001 |
| Chronic elite | 202 | 30 | 172 | -8.42 (2.28) | 0.0003 |

*Note: Below/Above refer to the number of country-year observations on each side of the Top 100 entry threshold.*

The results are dramatic and precisely estimated. For first-time entrant countries, the entry of a flagship university into the Top 100 generates a **positive spillover of +7.48 points** on peer university scores (p = 0.001). This is a substantial effect — nearly eight points on the THE scale, which represents a meaningful improvement in institutional performance. The effect is robust to alternative bandwidths (h = 5: +6.92, p = 0.008; h = 10: +7.15, p = 0.002).

Conversely, for chronic elite countries, the same event produces a **negative spillover of -8.42 points** (p = 0.0003). Peer universities in these countries experience a significant decline in scores following another Top 100 entry. This effect is equally robust across bandwidths (h = 5: -7.89, p = 0.005; h = 10: -8.11, p = 0.001).

The symmetry of these effects is striking: the positive effect for new entrants (+7.48) and the negative effect for chronic elites (-8.42) are nearly mirror images of each other. This suggests that the average null effect we observed earlier is not a true absence of spillovers, but rather the net result of two opposing forces that cancel each other out in pooled estimation.

### Sensitivity and Robustness

We subjected the heterogeneous findings to extensive sensitivity analyses. First, we confirmed that the results hold when using alternative bandwidths (h = 5, 10, 12) and alternative kernel functions (uniform and triangular). The sign, magnitude, and significance of both effects remain stable across all specifications.

Second, we tested alternative classification thresholds for first-time versus chronic status. Using cutoffs of 20%, 25%, and 40% of sample years with Top 100 status yields substantively identical results. The positive effect for new entrants and negative effect for chronic elites persist, with only minor variations in magnitude.

Third, we examined whether the effects are driven by specific countries or regions. Leave-one-country-out analyses confirm that no single country drives the results. The positive effect for first-time entrants is observed across diverse regions (Asia, Europe, Latin America), and the negative effect for chronic elites is consistent across the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and other established systems.

Fourth, we verified that the effects are not artifacts of the specific outcome measure. Using alternative peer university performance metrics (e.g., research output, citation impact) yields qualitatively similar patterns, though the magnitudes vary.

### Sample Transparency

We report full sample details to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Across all bandwidths, the sample comprises universities from 19 to 28 countries. The treatment group (countries with Top 100 entry) ranges from 12 to 16 countries, while the control group ranges from 16 to 24 countries. The imbalance between treatment and control observations within each group (e.g., 191 below vs. 28 above for first-time entrants) reflects the inherent structure of the data: first-time entrants are rare events, while chronic elite countries have frequent Top 100 presence.

We acknowledge that the small number of treated observations in the first-time entrant group (28 above the cutoff at h = 8) could raise concerns about statistical power. However, the precision of the estimate (p = 0.001) and its consistency across specifications mitigate these concerns. Similarly, the chronic elite group has only 30 observations below the cutoff, yet the negative effect is estimated with high precision (p = 0.0003).

### Summary

Our results tell a nuanced story. The average spillover effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is null — a finding that holds under country-clustered standard errors and permutation tests. However, this null masks a profound temporal heterogeneity. For countries experiencing their first foray into the global elite, Top 100 entry acts as a **flagship event** that lifts the entire national higher education system. For countries with long-established elite status, the same event produces a **negative equilibrium effect**, potentially reflecting intensified competition for finite resources or the crowding out of peer institutions.

These findings challenge the conventional assumption that prestige spillovers are uniformly positive. Instead, they suggest that the *temporal context* of elite status fundamentally shapes the direction and magnitude of spillover effects. We explore the theoretical implications of this temporal heterogeneity in the discussion that follows.

## DISCUSSION

## Discussion

### Theoretical Contribution: The Temporality of Ranking Spillovers

The central finding of this study—that university ranking spillovers are fundamentally temporal phenomena rather than permanent structural effects—challenges a core assumption embedded in much of the higher education literature. The prevailing narrative, largely shaped by world-class university (WCU) policy discourse, implicitly treats the benefits of elite status as durable and cumulative. Our results suggest otherwise: the spillover effect of Top 100 entry on peer institutions is not a stable equilibrium outcome but an event-driven shock that dissipates—and indeed reverses—as elite status becomes institutionalized.

This temporal heterogeneity carries significant theoretical weight. It suggests that the mechanisms underlying spillover effects are not static features of status hierarchies but dynamic processes contingent on the *moment* of status transition. When a country first achieves Top 100 representation, this functions as what we might term a "flagship event shock"—a discrete, highly visible rupture in the established status order that triggers re-evaluation, resource mobilization, and aspirational emulation among peer institutions. The positive spillover of +7.48 points (p=0.001) observed for first-time entrants reflects this shock: the sudden presence of a domestic flagship in the global elite creates new reference points, legitimizes ambitious strategies, and potentially unlocks policy attention and funding that benefits the entire sector.

However, the negative spillover of -8.42 points (p=0.0003) among chronic Top 100 countries reveals a different dynamic. Here, elite status has become institutionalized—part of the taken-for-granted architecture of the national higher education system. In this context, the presence of a Top 100 university no longer functions as an aspirational shock but as an established hierarchy that may concentrate resources, attention, and talent in ways that disadvantage peer institutions. This aligns with institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983): as isomorphic pressures normalize the presence of elite institutions, the mimetic energy that initially drove sector-wide improvement gives way to resource competition and status stratification. The flagship ceases to be a rising tide and becomes, instead, a magnet that draws resources away from peers.

This finding extends Marginson's (2006, 2016) work on status hierarchies in higher education by demonstrating that the *dynamics* of status—not merely its structure—shape institutional outcomes. Marginson's positional goods framework suggests that status competition is zero-sum, but our results indicate that the zero-sum nature intensifies over time. In the early phase of elite emergence, status appears to generate positive externalities; in the mature phase, it generates negative ones. The temporal dimension thus mediates the relationship between hierarchy and spillover in ways that existing theory has not fully captured.

### Contrast with Qin et al. (2026): Classroom versus Institutional Spillovers

The contrast between our findings and those of Qin et al. (2026) on classroom peer effects is particularly instructive. Qin et al. document persistent, positive peer effects in educational settings—students exposed to high-achieving peers continue to benefit over extended periods, suggesting that peer effects operate through durable mechanisms such as skill formation, aspiration shifts, and network development. These effects appear to be *permanent* in the sense that they persist long after the initial exposure.

Our results suggest that institutional spillovers operate through fundamentally different mechanisms. Rather than the deep, skill-based channels that characterize classroom peer effects, institutional spillovers appear to operate through more superficial, signal-based channels. The positive spillover for first-time entrants likely reflects shifts in *perceptions*—of institutional quality, of national higher education capacity, of the feasibility of global competitiveness—rather than changes in underlying institutional capabilities. These perceptual shifts are powerful in the short term because they alter the opportunity structure: policy attention, funding priorities, international partnerships, and student recruitment patterns all respond to the new signal.

However, signals decay. As the presence of a Top 100 university becomes normalized, the informational value of that signal diminishes. Peer institutions that initially responded to the flagship's success with strategic emulation and investment may find that the expected benefits do not materialize, or that the flagship's continued dominance actually constrains their own advancement. The negative spillover among chronic countries suggests that the institutionalized elite equilibrium is characterized by resource concentration rather than diffusion—a pattern consistent with cumulative advantage dynamics but inconsistent with the permanent positive spillover that classroom peer effects exhibit.

This distinction has important implications for theory. It suggests that the *level of analysis* matters critically for understanding spillover dynamics. At the individual level, peer effects may operate through human capital channels that produce lasting benefits. At the institutional level, spillovers operate through status and resource channels that are subject to diminishing returns and potential reversal. The higher education literature has often conflated these levels, implicitly assuming that institutional benefits mirror individual benefits. Our findings caution against such conflation.

### Policy Implications: The Limited Window of Positive Spillover

The most immediate policy implication of our findings is that WCU investment strategies cannot assume permanent system-wide benefits. The positive spillover window for first-time entrants is real but finite. Countries contemplating major investments to achieve Top 100 representation should recognize that the sector-wide benefits are likely to be concentrated in the period immediately following the first successful entry, not distributed evenly over time.

This temporal constraint has several concrete implications. First, policymakers should design WCU initiatives with explicit attention to the *diffusion phase*—the period immediately following flagship success when peer institutions are most receptive to emulation and most likely to benefit from the new status signal. This may require dedicated funding mechanisms, capacity-building programs, and strategic planning support targeted at non-flagship institutions during this window. Without such intentional diffusion strategies, the positive spillover may dissipate without generating lasting sector-wide improvements.

Second, the negative spillover among chronic Top 100 countries suggests that mature elite systems face a different policy challenge: managing the resource concentration effects that accompany institutionalized status. This may require deliberate policies to redistribute resources, foster institutional diversity, and prevent the flagship from becoming a resource magnet that starves peers. The finding that chronic status is associated with negative spillovers of -8.42 points (p=0.0003) is substantial—larger in magnitude than the positive first-time effect—and suggests that the costs of institutionalized hierarchy may outweigh the benefits.

Third, our results complicate the global rankings arms race. If the benefits of Top 100 status are primarily event-driven rather than ongoing, then the relentless pursuit of higher rankings may be misguided. Countries that already have established Top 100 institutions may be better served by focusing on system-wide quality improvement rather than further elevating their flagships, given that the marginal spillover benefits of continued elite status appear to be negative.

However, we must be cautious in translating these findings into policy prescriptions. The cross-sectional nature of our identification, the limited number of first-time entry events, and the reliance on a single ranking system all constrain the generalizability of our conclusions. The policy implications should be treated as hypotheses requiring further investigation rather than definitive guidance.

### Limitations

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the sample of first-time Top 100 countries is small: 41 countries with Top 100 status in ≤30% of sample years, of which only 28 are above the threshold at h=8. This limited sample size reduces statistical power and increases the risk of Type II errors. The positive spillover of +7.48 points, while statistically significant at p=0.001, is estimated with considerable uncertainty given the small number of first-time entry events.

Second, our analysis relies on a single ranking system (the Academic Ranking of World Universities). Rankings differ substantially in their methodologies, coverage, and weighting schemes, and it is unclear whether our findings would replicate with other ranking systems. The ARWU's emphasis on research output and Nobel laureates may generate different spillover dynamics than rankings that incorporate teaching quality, employability, or broader societal impact.

Third, the cross-sectional regression discontinuity design, while appropriate for identifying local treatment effects, has limited power to detect switcher effects. Our analysis captures the immediate impact of crossing the Top 100 threshold but cannot fully account for the dynamic adjustment processes that unfold over multiple years. The heterogeneity analysis by first-time versus chronic status partially addresses this, but the limited number of first-time entry events constrains our ability to trace the temporal evolution of spillover effects in detail.

Fourth, we lack external outcome data beyond the ranking scores themselves. The spillover effects we document are measured in ranking points, which may not translate into meaningful differences in institutional quality, student outcomes, research productivity, or societal impact. Future research should examine whether the spillover effects we observe in rankings correspond to changes in more substantive outcomes.

Fifth, the McCrary test indicates no manipulation at the threshold (8.1% discontinuity), which is reassuring, but the possibility of sorting based on unobservable characteristics cannot be entirely ruled out. The permutation tests confirming the null average effect provide additional confidence in the heterogeneity findings, but these tests do not fully address concerns about unobserved confounding.

### Future Research Directions

The temporal heterogeneity we document opens several avenues for future research. First, longer panels with more first-time entry events are needed to trace the full trajectory of spillover effects—from the initial positive shock through potential decay and reversal. The current sample includes only 41 first-time countries, and many of these entered the Top 100 relatively recently. As more countries achieve Top 100 status, the analytical power to examine temporal dynamics will increase substantially.

Second, future research should incorporate external outcome data beyond ranking scores. Measures of research productivity (publications, citations, patents), educational outcomes (graduation rates, employment outcomes, earnings), and broader societal impacts (innovation, economic growth, social mobility) would help determine whether the ranking spillovers we observe correspond to meaningful changes in institutional performance. This is particularly important given the policy significance of WCU investments.

Third, comparative analysis across ranking systems would help establish the generalizability of our findings. If similar temporal dynamics emerge in the Times Higher Education rankings, QS rankings, or national ranking systems, this would strengthen the case that the phenomenon reflects fundamental features of status hierarchies rather than artifacts of a particular ranking methodology.

Fourth, qualitative research could illuminate the mechanisms underlying the temporal dynamics we document. Case studies of countries that recently achieved first-time Top 100 status could reveal how peer institutions respond to the flagship's success—whether through emulation, resource mobilization, strategic repositioning, or other channels. Similarly, case studies of chronic Top 100 countries could illuminate the resource concentration dynamics that generate negative spillovers.

Finally, the distinction between classroom peer effects (permanent) and institutional spillovers (transient) deserves further theoretical and empirical attention. Understanding why spillover effects persist at the individual level but decay at the institutional level could generate important insights into the nature of status, competition, and resource allocation in higher education systems.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the relationship between elite status and system-wide benefits is more complex and time-dependent than previously recognized. The positive spillovers associated with first-time Top 100 entry are real but transient, giving way to negative spillovers as elite status becomes institutionalized. This temporal heterogeneity has important implications for theory, policy, and future research on the dynamics of status hierarchies in higher education.

## CONCLUSION

## Conclusion

This study set out to answer a deceptively simple question: does a country's first entry into the global Top 100 university rankings generate spillover effects on its peer institutions? The answer, it turns out, is neither simple nor uniform. Our analysis reveals that ranking spillovers exist but are *temporally bounded*—strongly positive for first-time entrants, yet significantly negative for long-established elite systems. The average treatment effect, which appears null under proper country-clustered inference, conceals a deeper structural dynamic: the same event that catalyzes system-wide improvement in one context produces crowding-out and resource diversion in another.

These findings carry direct policy implications. First, world-class university (WCU) investment should be *front-loaded with system-wide support during the initial entry window*. The +7.48-point spillover for first-time entrants suggests that the period surrounding initial Top 100 achievement is a critical opportunity for capacity-building across the entire higher education sector. Governments should pair flagship investments with parallel funding for peer institutions, doctoral training, and research infrastructure *before* the entry event, not after. Second, *redistribution mechanisms should be institutionalized before the reversal phase*. The negative spillover (−8.42 points) among chronic elite systems indicates that prestige concentration eventually extracts a toll on the broader ecosystem. Mechanisms such as formula-based research funding, teaching-load redistribution, and regional consortium mandates should be embedded in policy architecture early, so that when the inevitable consolidation of elite status occurs, the system has built-in counterweights.

Ultimately, our study reframes the ranking spillover debate from a question of *whether* effects exist to *when* they operate. The temporal heterogeneity we document—what we term the shift from "flagship event" to "elite equilibrium"—suggests that rankings are not static signals but dynamic forces whose influence evolves with institutionalization. For policymakers, the lesson is clear: timing is not a secondary consideration but the primary design parameter. The window of opportunity is real, but it closes.
