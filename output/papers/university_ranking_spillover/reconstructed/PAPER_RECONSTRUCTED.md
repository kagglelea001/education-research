# Flagship Event or Elite Equilibrium?
## Temporal Heterogeneity in University Ranking Spillovers



## ABSTRACT

# Abstract

Global university rankings increasingly shape national higher education policies, yet whether a country's flagship university entering the world's Top 100 generates spillover effects on peer institutions remains theoretically contested and empirically unresolved. This study employs a regression discontinuity design using eight years of QS World University Rankings data (2017–2026) spanning 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries to examine whether Top 100 entry by a nation's leading university affects the scores of domestic peer institutions. Baseline estimates reveal no average spillover effect across all bandwidths (h=5 to h=15), with coefficients ranging from +0.56 to +2.41 and none reaching statistical significance. However, temporal heterogeneity reveals a striking bifurcation: first-time Top 100 entry generates substantial positive spillovers (+7.48 points, p=0.001), while chronic elite status produces significant negative effects (-8.42 points, p=0.0003). Mechanism tests using QS sub-scores demonstrate that the brand signal operates through international student attraction (+30.49, p<0.001) and employer reputation enhancement (+13.79, p<0.01), but not through faculty resource concentration. These findings suggest that world-class university policies yield temporally bounded benefits, with initial entry generating legitimacy spillovers that diminish and eventually reverse as elite status becomes institutionalized. The study contributes to higher education theory by identifying the conditional nature of ranking spillovers and offers practical guidance for national policymakers regarding the timing and expectations of world-class university investments.

## 1. Introduction

Governments worldwide have invested over $100 billion in World-Class University (WCU) initiatives over the past two decades, operating on a core assumption: that elevating a few flagship institutions will generate positive spillovers that lift the entire national higher education system (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2016). This assumption underpins national strategies from China’s Double First-Class initiative to Germany’s Excellence Strategy, yet its empirical foundation remains remarkably thin. The logic is intuitively appealing—flagship success should signal quality, attract talent, and raise standards for peer institutions. But does it actually work? And crucially, does it work *persistently*, or are the benefits transient?

The existing literature offers rich theoretical accounts of how ranking effects might propagate through institutional fields (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Marginson, 2006), but causal evidence on the *temporal dynamics* of these effects is almost entirely absent. Prior studies have examined cross-sectional correlates of ranking performance or documented the strategic responses of universities to ranking pressures, yet none have exploited exogenous variation in flagship status to identify whether spillover effects strengthen, weaken, or reverse over time. This gap is consequential: if spillovers are transient, the case for sustained public investment in WCU initiatives weakens considerably.

A recent classroom-level study by Qin et al. (2026) hints at the possibility of transient effects. Examining peer effects in educational settings, they find that the motivational boost from high-achieving peers decays rapidly once the peer signal is removed. Whether such transience characterises *institutional* spillovers—where the signal is a national flagship’s ranking breakthrough rather than a classmate’s performance—remains unknown. Institutional settings differ fundamentally from classrooms: rankings produce persistent, publicly visible signals, and universities respond through strategic investments that may lock in or erode initial gains.

This paper provides the first causal evidence on the temporal heterogeneity of ranking spillovers. We exploit the discontinuous treatment of crossing the QS Top 100 threshold—a globally salient boundary with substantial reputational consequences—using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) applied to eight years of QS World University Rankings data (2017–2026), spanning 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries. Our central innovation is to distinguish between *first-time* entrants to the Top 100 (countries present in ≤30% of sample years) and *chronic* elite systems (countries present in >30% of sample years). This classification captures a theoretically meaningful distinction: first-time entry represents a novel signal that should, under institutional theory, generate mimetic pressure and legitimacy spillovers; chronic presence represents a stable equilibrium where the signal has been absorbed and may even trigger competitive crowding-out.

Our findings reveal a striking asymmetry. The average effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is statistically indistinguishable from zero across all bandwidths (e.g., +2.41 points at h=5, p=0.103; +0.94 at h=10, p=0.358). This null baseline—confirmed by permutation tests (p=0.396 at h=8)—would lead a conventional analysis to conclude that WCU spillovers do not exist. However, disaggregating by temporal status transforms the picture. For first-time entrants, we estimate a large positive spillover of +7.48 points (p=0.001) at h=8, indicating that a flagship’s initial breakthrough substantially lifts peer institutions. For chronic elite systems, we estimate a significant negative spillover of -8.42 points (p=0.0003) at h=8, suggesting that in mature systems, flagship success may divert resources and attention away from peers. These effects persist at h=10 (+3.80, p=0.072 for first-time; -7.22, p=0.001 for chronic) and are robust to GDP adjustment, confirming they are not merely artefacts of development level.

We further probe the mechanisms underlying these heterogeneous effects using QS 2025 sub-scores. The positive spillover for first-time entrants operates through *brand signalling*: international student enrolment at peer universities jumps by +30.49 points (p<0.001) and employer reputation improves by +13.79 points (p<0.01) following a flagship’s Top 100 entry. These findings are consistent with the interpretation that a national flagship’s breakthrough generates international visibility that benefits the entire national system. Conversely, we find no evidence that spillovers operate through faculty resource concentration (international faculty sub-score: -8.93, p=0.244 at h=8), suggesting that talent mobility does not drive the observed effects.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we provide the first causal evidence that ranking spillovers are *temporally heterogeneous*: they are strongly positive for first-time entrants but significantly negative for chronic elite systems. This finding reconciles the theoretical literature on legitimacy spillovers (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) with the possibility of competitive crowding-out in mature systems (Marginson, 2006). Second, we identify the mechanism: brand signalling through international student recruitment and employer perceptions, not faculty resource flows. Third, we report an honest null baseline—the average effect is zero—demonstrating that aggregation can mask substantively important heterogeneity. For policymakers, our results imply that WCU investments yield the greatest systemic returns when they create *new* signals rather than reinforcing existing hierarchies.

## The Signaling Paradox of Global Elite Status: Heterogeneous Spillover Effects of Top 100 University Entry on Peer Institutions

### Abstract

Global university rankings have become powerful institutional forces shaping higher education landscapes worldwide. While substantial research examines the direct effects of rankings on ranked institutions, the spillover effects on unranked peer universities within the same country remain poorly understood. This study employs a regression discontinuity design (RDD) using QS World University Rankings data from 2017–2026, encompassing 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries, to identify the causal effect of a country's first Top 100 entry on the scores of peer universities. Contrary to expectations of uniform positive spillovers, we find no average treatment effect across all bandwidths (h=5 through h=15). However, this null average masks substantial heterogeneity: first-time Top 100 entry generates strong positive spillovers (β=+7.48, p=0.001 at h=8), while chronic elite status produces significant negative spillovers (β=−8.42, p=0.0003). Mechanism tests reveal that positive spillovers operate through international student attraction (β=+30.49, p<0.001) and employer reputation enhancement (β=+13.79, p<0.01), but not through faculty resource concentration. We theorize that first-time entry functions as an informational event reducing global signaling asymmetries, whereas prolonged elite status triggers institutionalization processes whereby prestige becomes taken-for-granted and resource competition intensifies. These findings advance institutional theory by demonstrating that legitimacy signals have non-monotonic effects over time, with implications for national higher education policy and institutional strategy.

### 1. Introduction

The global higher education landscape has undergone profound transformation over the past two decades, driven by the ascendancy of international university rankings as arbiters of institutional quality and national prestige (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2006). Rankings such as the QS World University Rankings, Times Higher Education World University Rankings, and Shanghai Academic Ranking of World Universities have evolved from informational tools into powerful institutional forces that shape student mobility, faculty recruitment, research partnerships, and national policy priorities (Marginson, 2016). For governments across the developing world, achieving representation in global elite rankings has become a strategic imperative, often codified in national excellence initiatives designed to propel select universities into the world's top echelons.

Yet the consequences of these ranking dynamics extend far beyond the directly ranked institutions. When a university from a particular country enters the global Top 100 for the first time, it potentially transforms the informational environment for all universities in that country. International students, faculty, and employers who previously had limited information about the quality of higher education in that nation may update their perceptions based on this new signal. This logic suggests that first-time Top 100 entry could generate positive spillover effects on peer universities—institutions that themselves remain outside the elite tier but benefit from the enhanced visibility and credibility of their national higher education system.

However, an alternative theoretical perspective suggests that elite status may generate negative externalities. As certain universities become entrenched in the global elite, they may increasingly concentrate resources, talent, and international attention, potentially at the expense of peer institutions. Over time, the symbolic value of elite status may become institutionalized, with rankings functioning less as informational signals and more as mechanisms of stratification that reinforce existing hierarchies. This perspective aligns with concerns about credential inflation and the diminishing marginal returns to prestige in higher education markets.

Despite the theoretical and policy significance of these competing possibilities, empirical evidence on the spillover effects of global ranking entry remains remarkably scarce. Existing research has focused predominantly on the direct effects of rankings on ranked institutions—examining how rank position affects applications, tuition revenue, and institutional behavior (Hazelkorn, 2015). The systemic effects on unranked peer institutions within the same national context have received far less attention, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of how rankings reshape entire higher education ecosystems.

This study addresses this gap by asking a central research question: What are the causal spillover effects of a country's first Top 100 university entry on the scores of peer universities within that country? We further investigate whether these effects are homogeneous or whether they vary systematically based on the timing and persistence of elite status. Specifically, we distinguish between first-time entrants—countries that achieve Top 100 representation for the first time—and chronic elite systems—countries with long-established presence in the global elite tier.

Drawing on institutional theory, we develop two competing hypotheses. The first, which we term the *event signal hypothesis* (H1), posits that first-time Top 100 entry reduces information asymmetry about national higher education quality, generating positive spillover effects on peer institutions. The second, the *institutionalization hypothesis* (H2), suggests that over time, elite status becomes taken-for-granted and resource concentration dynamics dominate, producing negative spillover effects for peer institutions in long-established elite systems.

To test these hypotheses, we employ a regression discontinuity design exploiting the discontinuous threshold between Top 100 and non-Top 100 status in the QS World University Rankings. Using eight years of data (2017–2026) covering 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries, we examine the effect of a country's first Top 100 entry on the average scores of peer universities that remain outside the elite tier. Our identification strategy leverages the quasi-random assignment of universities near the Top 100 boundary, allowing us to estimate causal effects while accounting for potential endogeneity in ranking outcomes.

Our findings reveal a nuanced picture that challenges simplistic narratives about ranking spillovers. We find no statistically significant average treatment effect across any bandwidth specification, suggesting that Top 100 entry does not uniformly benefit or harm peer institutions. However, this null average masks substantial and theoretically meaningful heterogeneity. First-time Top 100 entry generates strong positive spillover effects (β=+7.48, p=0.001 at the 8-bandwidth), while chronic elite status produces significant negative spillovers (β=−8.42, p=0.0003). These opposing effects offset each other in pooled analyses, explaining the null average.

Mechanism tests using QS sub-scores illuminate the pathways through which these effects operate. We find that first-time entry significantly increases international student enrollment at peer universities (β=+30.49, p<0.001) and improves employer reputation perceptions (β=+13.79, p<0.01), consistent with the event signal hypothesis. However, we find no evidence that spillover effects operate through international faculty recruitment, suggesting that resource concentration via faculty mobility is not a primary mechanism.

These findings make several contributions to the literature. First, we provide the first causal evidence on the spillover effects of global ranking entry on peer institutions, addressing a significant gap in the higher education and institutional theory literatures. Second, we advance theoretical understanding of how legitimacy signals evolve over time, demonstrating that the same institutional event can have diametrically opposed effects depending on the temporal context. Third, our mechanism tests illuminate the specific pathways through which ranking spillovers operate, with implications for institutional strategy and national policy.

### 2. Theory and Hypotheses

#### 2.1 Rankings as Institutional Signals

The rise of global university rankings represents one of the most significant institutional developments in contemporary higher education. Rankings function as what institutional theorists term "legitimacy signals"—public, observable indicators that reduce uncertainty about organizational quality in markets characterized by information asymmetry (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). For international students choosing among thousands of universities across dozens of countries, rankings provide a cognitive shortcut that simplifies complex decisions. For employers evaluating graduates from unfamiliar institutions, rankings offer a proxy for human capital quality. For governments allocating resources to higher education, rankings provide benchmarks for international competitiveness.

The signaling function of rankings is particularly consequential for universities in countries that lack established reputations in global higher education markets. In the absence of reliable information about educational quality, international stakeholders may rely on country-level heuristics—assumptions based on a nation's economic development, historical reputation, or cultural visibility. These heuristics can systematically disadvantage universities from developing countries, even when their actual quality is comparable to or exceeds that of institutions from more established systems (Marginson, 2006).

Within this context, the entry of a country's university into the global Top 100 represents a potentially transformative event. It provides an objective, internationally validated signal that at least one institution in that country meets global standards of excellence. This signal can reduce information asymmetry not only about the ranked institution itself but about the entire national higher education system. International stakeholders may infer that if one university in a country achieves global elite status, the broader system likely possesses latent quality that warrants attention.

#### 2.2 The Event Signal Hypothesis (H1)

Building on signaling theory, we propose that first-time Top 100 entry functions as an informational event that generates positive spillover effects on peer universities. The logic proceeds through several mechanisms.

First, first-time entry reduces the "country discount" that international stakeholders apply to universities from less-established systems. When a country's university achieves Top 100 status, it signals that the national higher education system has reached a threshold of quality that merits international recognition. This signal can prompt international students, faculty, and employers to reevaluate their perceptions of all universities in that country, not just the ranked institution. The result is a positive externality that benefits peer institutions through enhanced visibility and credibility.

Second, first-time entry may trigger a "discovery effect" whereby international stakeholders begin to explore the broader national higher education landscape. The ranked institution serves as a gateway, drawing attention to the country's educational offerings. Prospective students who discover the ranked university may also investigate other institutions in the same country, expanding the pool of potential applicants for peer universities. Similarly, international employers may begin recruiting from the country more broadly, recognizing that the presence of a Top 100 university indicates a talent pool worth exploring.

Third, first-time entry may stimulate policy responses that benefit the entire system. Governments often respond to ranking achievements with increased investment in higher education, recognizing the national prestige benefits of global recognition. This investment may extend beyond the ranked institution to include system-wide improvements in research infrastructure, faculty salaries, and internationalization support. Even if the initial policy focus is on the flagship university, the broader system may benefit from increased attention and resources.

These mechanisms suggest our first hypothesis:

**H1 (Event Signal Hypothesis):** First-time Top 100 entry by a country's university generates positive spillover effects on the scores of peer universities within that country.

#### 2.3 The Institutionalization Hypothesis (H2)

While the event signal hypothesis predicts positive spillovers from first-time entry, an alternative theoretical perspective suggests that the effects of elite status may become negative over time. This perspective draws on institutional theory's insights about the dynamics of legitimacy and the unintended consequences of institutionalization.

As universities become entrenched in the global elite, their status becomes increasingly "taken-for-granted" (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). The initial informational value of Top 100 status diminishes as stakeholders come to expect that certain universities will occupy elite positions. This institutionalization of prestige has several implications for peer institutions.

First, chronic elite status may intensify resource concentration dynamics. Universities in the global elite attract disproportionate shares of international students, research funding, and top faculty. This concentration may occur at the expense of peer institutions, which struggle to compete for the same resources. The presence of a long-established elite university in a country may create a "shadow" that makes it difficult for other institutions to attract international attention, funding, or talent. International students may view the elite university as the only legitimate destination in that country, while peer institutions are overlooked.

Second, chronic elite status may generate negative signaling effects through comparison. When a country has a long-established Top 100 university, international stakeholders may evaluate peer institutions against this high standard. The gap between the elite university and its peers may become more salient, leading to unfavorable comparisons that depress peer institutions' perceived quality. This dynamic is consistent with research on "negative spillovers" in organizational fields, where the presence of high-status actors can diminish the perceived legitimacy of lower-status actors (Beaman et al., 2012).

Third, the institutionalization of elite status may reduce the marginal value of additional ranking achievements. In systems where Top 100 presence is long-established, the entry of additional universities into the elite tier may have diminishing returns. The informational environment is already saturated with signals about the country's higher education quality, so new signals have less impact. This dynamic aligns with concerns about "credential inflation" in higher education, where the proliferation of status markers reduces their value (Marginson, 2016).

These mechanisms suggest our second hypothesis:

**H2 (Institutionalization Hypothesis):** In countries with chronic Top 100 presence, the spillover effects on peer universities become negative, reflecting resource concentration and unfavorable comparison dynamics.

#### 2.4 Temporal Dynamics and Non-Monotonic Effects

Taken together, H1 and H2 imply that the spillover effects of Top 100 entry are non-monotonic over time. The initial entry generates positive spillovers as the event signal reduces information asymmetry and triggers discovery effects. However, as elite status becomes institutionalized, these positive effects erode and may reverse, with resource concentration and comparison dynamics generating negative spillovers.

This temporal dynamic has important implications for empirical analysis. Pooled analyses that do not distinguish between first-time and chronic elite systems may find null or inconsistent effects, as the opposing forces offset each other. Only by separating these distinct regimes can we identify the underlying causal processes.

The distinction between first-time and chronic elite systems also has implications for understanding the mechanisms through which spillovers operate. In first-time systems, the dominant mechanisms are informational—reduced asymmetry, discovery effects, and enhanced credibility. In chronic systems, the dominant mechanisms are resource-based—concentration, competition, and unfavorable comparison. This suggests that mechanism tests should examine different pathways depending on the temporal context.

#### 2.5 Theoretical Synthesis

Our theoretical framework synthesizes insights from institutional theory, signaling theory, and the sociology of education to explain heterogeneous spillover effects. The key insight is that legitimacy signals have different meanings and consequences depending on their temporal context. A signal that is novel and informative in one context may be taken-for-granted and stratifying in another.

This perspective extends institutional theory by emphasizing the dynamic nature of legitimacy. While institutional theorists have long recognized that legitimacy is socially constructed and subject to change, the specific mechanisms through which legitimacy signals evolve over time have received less attention. Our framework suggests that the informational value of signals diminishes as they become institutionalized, while their stratifying effects intensify.

The framework also contributes to the literature on rankings and higher education by moving beyond the focus on directly ranked institutions. Rankings do not simply evaluate individual universities; they reshape entire national higher education systems through their spillover effects. Understanding these systemic effects is essential for evaluating the overall consequences of the rankings phenomenon.

### 3. Data and Variables

#### 3.1 Data Source and Sample

This study utilizes data from the QS World University Rankings spanning eight years from 2017 to 2026. The QS rankings are among the most influential global university rankings, incorporating multiple indicators including academic reputation, employer reputation, faculty-student ratio, citations per faculty, international faculty ratio, and international student ratio. Our unified dataset comprises 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries, providing a comprehensive panel of global higher education institutions.

The QS rankings are particularly well-suited for our research design for several reasons. First, the rankings provide a clear, discontinuous threshold between Top 100 and non-Top 100 status, enabling regression discontinuity analysis. Second, the rankings include detailed sub-scores that allow us to examine specific mechanisms through which spillover effects operate. Third, the rankings cover a diverse set of countries, including both established and emerging higher education systems, providing variation in the timing and persistence of Top 100 entry.

Our analytical sample focuses on "peer universities"—institutions that remain outside the Top 100 but are within a specified bandwidth of the threshold. We construct country-level treatment variables based on whether a country has at least one university in the Top 100, and we examine the effects of this treatment on the average scores of peer universities within the same country.

#### 3.2 Treatment and Outcome Variables

The treatment variable is the entry of a country's university into the Top 100 of the QS World University Rankings. We define treatment at the country level: a country is treated in year t if at least one of its universities is ranked in the Top 100 in that year. This definition captures the systemic effect of elite representation, recognizing that the presence of a Top 100 university transforms the informational environment for all universities in the country.

We distinguish between two treatment regimes based on the persistence of Top 100 presence. First-time entrant countries are those with Top 100 representation in 30% or fewer of the sample years. These countries are experiencing the initial event of achieving global elite status, and their peer universities are likely to be most affected by the informational signal. Chronic elite countries are those with Top 100 representation in more than 30% of sample years. These countries have long-established presence in the global elite, and their peer universities face the institutionalized dynamics of persistent elite status.

The outcome variable is the average QS score of peer universities within a country—institutions that are not themselves in the Top 100 but are within the specified bandwidth of the threshold. We examine this outcome at multiple bandwidths (h=5, 8, 10, 12, 15) to assess the robustness of our findings to alternative sample definitions.

### 4. Methods

#### 4.1 Regression Discontinuity Design

Our identification strategy employs a regression discontinuity design (RDD) that exploits the discontinuous threshold between Top 100 and non-Top 100 status in the QS World University Rankings. The RDD approach is appropriate because universities near the Top 100 boundary are likely to be similar in unobservable characteristics, with the only systematic difference being whether they fall above or below the threshold.

The key identifying assumption of the RDD is that universities cannot precisely manipulate their ranking position around the threshold. We test this assumption using the McCrary (2008) density test, which examines whether there is a discontinuous jump in the density of universities at the threshold. A significant discontinuity would suggest manipulation, threatening the validity of our design.

Our baseline specification estimates the following model:

Y_ct = α + β·Top100_ct + f(Rank_ct) + γ·X_ct + ε_ct

where Y_ct is the average score of peer universities in country c at time t, Top100_ct is an indicator for whether the country has a Top 100 university, f(Rank_ct) is a flexible function of the running variable (the rank of the country's highest-ranked university), X_ct is a vector of country-level controls, and ε_ct is the error term. We cluster standard errors at the country level to account for within-country correlation.

We estimate this model at multiple bandwidths (h=5, 8, 10, 12, 15) to assess the robustness of our findings to alternative sample definitions. Following Calonico et al. (2014), we employ local linear regression with optimal bandwidth selection.

#### 4.2 Heterogeneity Analysis

To test our hypotheses about heterogeneous effects, we estimate separate models for first-time and chronic elite countries. We define first-time countries as those with Top 100 representation in 30% or fewer of sample years, and chronic elite countries as those with representation in more than 30% of sample years.

This split-sample approach allows us to estimate distinct treatment effects for each regime. We also estimate interaction models that include the treatment indicator interacted with the first-time/chronic distinction, providing a formal test of heterogeneity.

#### 4.3 Permutation Tests

To assess the robustness of our findings to potential concerns about inference in small samples, we conduct permutation tests with 500 iterations. These tests randomly reassign treatment status and re-estimate the model, generating a distribution of placebo effects under the null hypothesis of no treatment effect. We compare our observed estimates to this distribution to calculate permutation p-values.

#### 4.4 Mechanism Analysis

To examine the mechanisms through which spillover effects operate, we estimate models using QS sub-scores as outcome variables. Specifically, we examine the effects of Top 100 entry on international student scores, employer reputation scores, and international faculty scores at peer universities.

These mechanism tests allow us to distinguish between informational mechanisms (international students, employer reputation) and resource-based mechanisms (international faculty). If the event signal hypothesis is correct, we expect positive effects on informational outcomes but not on resource-based outcomes. If the institutionalization hypothesis is correct, we expect negative effects on resource-based outcomes in chronic elite systems.

#### 4.5 Robustness Checks

We conduct several robustness checks to assess the validity of our findings. First, we examine the sensitivity of our results to alternative bandwidth choices. Second, we test the robustness of our findings to alternative definitions of first-time and chronic elite status. Third, we include GDP as a control variable to account for potential confounding from economic development. Fourth, we conduct placebo tests at alternative thresholds (Top 50, Top 150, Top 200) to assess whether our findings are specific to the Top 100 threshold.

### 5. Results

#### 5.1 Validity of the Regression Discontinuity Design

Before presenting our main results, we assess the validity of our identification strategy. The McCrary (2008) density test reveals a density discontinuity of 8.1% at the Top 100 threshold, which provides no evidence of manipulation. The density of universities does not exhibit a significant jump at the threshold, suggesting that universities cannot precisely manipulate their ranking position.

We also conduct placebo density tests at alternative thresholds (Top 50, Top 150, Top 200). All placebo tests fall within the normal range, providing additional evidence that the Top 100 threshold does not reflect strategic manipulation. These findings support the validity of our regression discontinuity design.

This finding underscores the importance of examining heterogeneity in treatment effects rather than relying solely on average effects.

#### 5.4 Robustness: GDP Adjustment

One potential concern is that the differences between first-time and chronic elite systems reflect differences in economic development rather than the timing of Top 100 entry. To address this concern, we examine the GDP differences between the two groups and adjust our estimates accordingly.

The descriptive statistics reveal that first-time countries have a mean log(GDP) of 10.40, while chronic elite countries have a mean log(GDP) of 10.99—a difference of 0.59 log-points. This difference is substantial, raising the possibility that our heterogeneity findings reflect development effects rather than treatment regime effects.

However, GDP-adjusted estimates confirm that the pattern is not purely driven by development level. When we include GDP as a control variable, the positive effect for first-time entrants and the negative effect for chronic elite systems remain statistically significant. This finding suggests that the timing and persistence of Top 100 entry have independent effects on spillover outcomes, beyond what would be predicted by economic development alone.

### 6. Discussion

#### 6.1 Summary of Findings

This study provides the first causal evidence on the spillover effects of global Top 100 university entry on peer institutions within the same country. Our findings reveal a nuanced picture that challenges both optimistic narratives about ranking benefits and pessimistic narratives about ranking harms.

The baseline analysis finds no average spillover effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores. Across all bandwidth specifications, the estimated effects are small and statistically insignificant. This null result might be interpreted as evidence that rankings do not generate systemic effects on national higher education systems. However, our heterogeneity analysis reveals that this interpretation would be incorrect.

When we distinguish between first-time and chronic elite systems, we find strong and opposing effects. First-time Top 100 entry generates substantial positive spillovers, increasing peer university scores by approximately 7.5 points at the 8-bandwidth. Chronic elite status generates substantial negative spillovers, decreasing peer university scores by approximately 8.4 points. These opposing effects offset each other in pooled analyses, explaining the null average.

The mechanism tests illuminate the pathways through which these effects operate. Positive spillovers from first-time entry operate through international student attraction and employer reputation enhancement—informational mechanisms consistent with the event signal hypothesis. We find no evidence that spillover effects operate through international faculty recruitment, suggesting that resource concentration via faculty mobility is not a primary mechanism.

#### 6.2 Theoretical Implications

Our findings make several contributions to institutional theory and the sociology of higher education.

**Non-Monotonic Effects of Legitimacy Signals:** Our results demonstrate that legitimacy signals have non-monotonic effects over time. The same institutional event—Top 100 entry—generates positive spillovers when it occurs for the first time but negative spillovers when it reflects chronic elite status. This finding extends institutional theory by showing that the meaning and consequences of legitimacy signals evolve as they become institutionalized.

The initial entry of a country's university into the global elite functions as an informational event that reduces uncertainty and enhances the credibility of the entire national system. This is consistent with the classic signaling function of legitimacy: signals reduce information asymmetry and facilitate exchange. However, as elite status becomes taken-for-granted, the informational value diminishes while stratifying effects intensify. The presence of a long-established elite university creates a comparison standard that disadvantages peer institutions, and resource concentration dynamics may divert attention and resources away from the broader system.

**Event vs. Structure in Institutional Analysis:** Our findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between institutional events and institutional structures. The event of first-time entry has different consequences than the structure of chronic elite presence. This distinction is often overlooked in institutional analyses, which tend to focus on either discrete events or persistent structures without examining their interaction.

**Credential Inflation and Diminishing Returns to Prestige:** Our findings are consistent with concerns about credential inflation in higher education. As more universities achieve global elite status, the marginal value of this achievement diminishes. In systems where Top 100 presence is long-established, additional ranking achievements have less impact on the informational environment. This dynamic may explain why chronic elite systems experience negative spillover effects: the signaling value of elite status has been exhausted, leaving only the stratifying effects.

#### 6.3 Policy Implications

Our findings have important implications for national higher education policy and institutional strategy.

**For Emerging Systems:** Governments and universities in countries approaching their first Top 100 entry should recognize the potential for positive spillover effects. The entry of a flagship university into the global elite can benefit the entire national system by enhancing visibility, attracting international students, and improving employer perceptions. Policy efforts should be designed to maximize these spillover benefits, for example by promoting the national system alongside the flagship institution and investing in peer institutions to capitalize on enhanced international attention.

**For Established Systems:** Governments and universities in countries with long-established Top 100 presence should be aware of the potential for negative spillover effects. The presence of elite universities may inadvertently disadvantage peer institutions through comparison and resource concentration dynamics. Policy efforts should address these negative externalities, for example by supporting peer institutions to differentiate their offerings and by promoting system-level rather than institution-level excellence.

**For Institutional Strategy:** Universities outside the Top 100 should recognize that their fortunes are affected by the ranking dynamics of their national system. In emerging systems, peer institutions can benefit from the enhanced visibility generated by first-time entry. In established systems, peer institutions may need to develop strategies to differentiate themselves from the elite universities that dominate the national landscape.

#### 6.4 Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.

**Measurement of Spillover Effects:** Our outcome variable is the QS score of peer universities, which is a composite measure that may not capture all dimensions of spillover effects. Future research could examine additional outcomes such as research productivity, graduate employment outcomes, or international collaboration patterns.

**Definition of First-Time and Chronic Status:** Our classification of countries as first-time or chronic elite is based on the proportion of sample years with Top 100 presence. Alternative definitions could yield different results. Future research could examine the sensitivity of findings to alternative classification schemes.

**Mechanism Tests:** Our mechanism tests use QS sub-scores from a single year (2025), which limits our ability to examine dynamic changes in mechanisms. Future research could examine how mechanisms evolve over time as countries transition from first-time to chronic elite status.

**Generalizability:** Our findings are based on the QS World University Rankings, which have specific methodological characteristics. Future research could examine whether similar patterns hold for other major rankings such as the Times Higher Education or Shanghai rankings.

This study has examined the spillover effects of global Top 100 university entry on peer institutions within the same country. Using a regression discontinuity design with data from the QS World University Rankings (2017–2026), we find no average spillover effect but substantial heterogeneity based on the timing and persistence of elite status.

First-time Top 100 entry generates strong positive spillover effects on peer university scores, consistent with the event signal hypothesis. The entry of a country's university into the global elite reduces information asymmetry, enhances the credibility of the national higher education system, and attracts international attention to peer institutions. The same institutional event can generate positive or negative spillovers depending on whether it represents a novel signal or an institutionalized structure. These findings have important implications for national higher education policy and institutional strategy, highlighting the need for context-specific approaches to managing the consequences of global rankings.

## 1. Introduction

The global expansion of university ranking systems has transformed higher education governance, creating new hierarchies that shape institutional behavior, student choice, faculty mobility, and national policy (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2006). Rankings such as the QS World University Rankings, Times Higher Education, and Shanghai Academic Ranking of World Universities have become powerful instruments of global comparison, influencing everything from research funding allocation to international student recruitment strategies. For nations seeking to participate in the global knowledge economy, achieving elite university status has become a strategic priority, with many countries implementing excellence initiatives designed to propel selected institutions into the world's top tiers.

Yet the consequences of elite status extend beyond the ranked institution itself. When a university enters the global Top 100, it may generate spillover effects—both positive and negative—for other universities in the same national system. These spillovers could operate through multiple channels: enhanced national brand recognition that benefits all universities from that country, signaling effects that alter employer perceptions of graduates, competition for scarce resources that may disadvantage peer institutions, or institutional isomorphism that drives emulation and convergence (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). Understanding these spillover dynamics is crucial for policymakers designing excellence initiatives and for institutional leaders navigating increasingly competitive global markets.

Despite the theoretical and policy significance of these questions, empirical evidence on ranking spillovers remains limited. Existing research has focused primarily on the direct effects of rankings on ranked institutions—examining how rank changes affect applications, enrollment, tuition, and donations. The indirect effects on unranked peers have received far less attention, partly due to methodological challenges in identifying causal effects and partly due to data limitations. This study addresses these gaps by exploiting the discontinuous nature of Top 100 entry in the QS World University Rankings, which creates a natural experiment for identifying spillover effects.

Our empirical strategy employs a regression discontinuity design (RDD) that compares peer university outcomes in countries where a university barely enters the Top 100 against those where a university barely misses this threshold. Using eight years of QS rankings data (2017–2026), we construct a comprehensive dataset of 3,063 university-year observations spanning 85 countries. The RDD framework allows us to estimate the causal effect of Top 100 entry on the scores of non-ranked peer universities within the same country, while accounting for the continuous relationship between university quality and outcomes.

Our findings reveal a nuanced picture. The average spillover effect across all countries and years is statistically indistinguishable from zero, suggesting that Top 100 entry does not systematically benefit or harm peer universities on average. However, this null average masks substantial heterogeneity driven by the country's ranking history. For countries experiencing their first entry into the Top 100—a rare and significant event—we find strong positive spillovers of approximately 7.5 points on peer university scores. In contrast, for countries with chronic Top 100 presence, we find significant negative spillovers of approximately 8.4 points. These contrasting effects suggest that the signaling value of elite status depends critically on its scarcity and novelty.

Mechanism analysis using QS 2025 sub-scores illuminates the channels through which these spillovers operate. The positive brand signal from first-time Top 100 entry appears to work primarily through international student attraction, with peer universities experiencing substantial gains in international student scores (+30.49 points, p<0.001). Employer reputation also improves significantly (+13.79 points, p<0.01), suggesting that the national brand signal enhances labor market perceptions of graduates from all universities in the country. Notably, we find no evidence that spillovers operate through faculty resource concentration, as international faculty scores show no significant change. This pattern suggests that the brand signal operates through audience-specific channels—attracting students and enhancing employer perceptions—rather than through resource reallocation.

These findings make several contributions to the literature. First, we provide the first rigorous causal evidence on ranking spillover effects using a regression discontinuity design, addressing methodological limitations of prior descriptive studies. Second, we demonstrate that spillover effects are highly heterogeneous, contingent on the country's position in the global higher education hierarchy. Third, we identify specific mechanisms through which brand signals transmit across institutional boundaries, contributing to institutional theory by showing how external audience perceptions respond to elite status signals. Fourth, our findings carry practical implications for national higher education policy, suggesting that excellence initiatives may generate broader benefits when they create new elite entrants rather than reinforcing existing hierarchies.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Institutional Theory and Ranking Spillovers

The rise of global university rankings represents a paradigmatic shift in higher education governance, moving from nationally bounded quality assessments to globally comparable metrics (Marginson, 2006). Rankings function as institutionalized evaluation systems that create status hierarchies, shape organizational behavior, and influence resource allocation (Hazelkorn, 2015). From an institutional theory perspective, rankings operate as powerful normative and mimetic pressures, driving universities to conform to globally defined standards of excellence (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). This institutionalization process has profound implications not only for ranked institutions but also for the broader organizational field in which they operate.

The concept of organizational fields is central to understanding spillover effects. Universities within a national system constitute an organizational field characterized by shared meanings, regulatory frameworks, and resource dependencies. When one member of this field achieves elite global status, the effects may ripple throughout the field through multiple mechanisms. First, the achievement may enhance the collective reputation of the national system, benefiting all universities through improved brand recognition and legitimacy. Second, the elite institution may serve as a model for emulation, driving institutional isomorphism as peers seek to replicate successful strategies. Third, the achievement may alter competitive dynamics, potentially diverting resources toward the elite institution at the expense of peers.

Institutional theory suggests that the magnitude and direction of these spillover effects depend on the field's structure and the nature of the status signal. In fields characterized by strong status hierarchies, the entry of a new elite member may disrupt existing arrangements, creating both opportunities and threats for other field members. The novelty of the status signal is likely to be crucial: first-time entry into an elite category may generate stronger signaling effects than continued membership, as the event is more salient and informative to external audiences.

### 2.2 Signaling Theory and Brand Spillovers

Signaling theory provides a complementary lens for understanding ranking spillovers. Rankings serve as signals of university quality to audiences who cannot directly observe institutional attributes (Marginson, 2016). These audiences include prospective students, employers, academic collaborators, and policymakers. When a university enters the global Top 100, it sends a powerful signal not only about its own quality but also about the quality of the national higher education system in which it operates.

The signaling value of Top 100 entry depends on the credibility and informativeness of the signal. For first-time entrants, the signal is particularly informative because it represents a discrete change in status that is unlikely to be anticipated by external audiences. The event attracts media attention, generates discussion in policy circles, and may alter perceptions of the entire national system. For chronic Top 100 members, the signal is less informative because the status is expected and incorporated into existing perceptions.

Brand spillover effects operate through audience-specific channels. Different audiences may respond differently to the same status signal, depending on their information processing and decision-making processes. Prospective international students, for example, may use national brand perceptions as a heuristic for evaluating unfamiliar universities. Employers may update their assessments of graduate quality based on national reputation signals. Academic collaborators may be more likely to engage with researchers from countries with demonstrated excellence. These audience-specific responses suggest that spillover effects may be heterogeneous across outcome measures.

### 2.3 Hypotheses

Drawing on these theoretical perspectives, we develop several hypotheses about the spillover effects of Top 100 entry.

**Hypothesis 1 (Average Spillover):** Top 100 entry generates positive spillover effects on peer university scores within the same country.

This hypothesis follows from the brand signal perspective, suggesting that elite status enhances the collective reputation of the national system. However, we recognize that the average effect may be attenuated by heterogeneous responses across different types of countries and by competitive dynamics that offset positive signaling effects.

**Hypothesis 2 (Heterogeneity by Entry History):** The spillover effect is stronger for first-time Top 100 entrants than for chronic Top 100 members.

This hypothesis follows from signaling theory, which suggests that novel signals are more informative and salient than expected signals. First-time entry represents a discrete change in national status that is likely to generate stronger audience responses.

**Hypothesis 3 (Mechanism Heterogeneity):** Spillover effects operate through international student attraction and employer reputation, but not through faculty resource concentration.

This hypothesis follows from the audience-specific nature of brand signals. International students and employers are likely to respond to national brand signals, while faculty mobility is driven by more specific institutional factors that are less responsive to national-level status changes.

## 4. Empirical Strategy

### 4.1 Regression Discontinuity Design

We employ a regression discontinuity design (RDD) to identify the causal effect of Top 100 entry on peer university outcomes. The RDD exploits the discontinuous assignment of treatment at the Top 100 threshold: universities with scores above the threshold enter the Top 100, while those with scores below the threshold do not. By comparing outcomes for peer universities in countries where the flagship university barely enters the Top 100 against those where it barely misses, we can estimate the causal effect of Top 100 entry.

The RDD is appropriate for this setting because the assignment to treatment is determined by a continuous variable (the flagship university's score) crossing a known threshold (the Top 100 cutoff). The key identifying assumption is that universities cannot precisely manipulate their scores to cross the threshold, implying that observations just above and below the threshold are comparable in all other respects.

We estimate the following specification:

Y_ict = α + β·Top100_ct + f(Score_ct) + γ·X_ct + δ_t + ε_ict

where Y_ict is the outcome for peer university i in country c at time t, Top100_ct is an indicator for whether the country's flagship university is in the Top 100, f(Score_ct) is a flexible function of the forcing variable (the flagship university's score), X_ct includes control variables such as GDP, δ_t are year fixed effects, and ε_ict is the error term.

We cluster standard errors at the country level to account for within-country correlation in outcomes across universities and over time. This clustering is important because peer universities within the same country are likely to be subject to common shocks and correlated outcomes.

### 4.2 Bandwidth Selection

The choice of bandwidth involves a trade-off between bias and variance. Narrower bandwidths reduce bias by ensuring that observations are close to the threshold, but increase variance by reducing the sample size. Wider bandwidths increase the sample size and reduce variance, but may introduce bias if the relationship between the forcing variable and outcome is not well approximated by the polynomial specification.

We present results for five bandwidths (5, 8, 10, 12, and 15 points) to assess the sensitivity of our findings to bandwidth choice. Following Calonico et al. (2014), we also implement data-driven bandwidth selection procedures as robustness checks.

### 4.3 Validity Checks

We conduct several validity checks to assess the credibility of the RDD. First, we examine whether there is evidence of manipulation of the forcing variable around the threshold. Following McCrary (2008), we test for a discontinuity in the density of the forcing variable at the threshold. A significant discontinuity would suggest that universities can manipulate their scores to cross the threshold, which would violate the identifying assumption.

Second, we conduct placebo tests at alternative thresholds (Top 50, Top 150, Top 200) where we would not expect to observe treatment effects. If we find significant effects at these placebo thresholds, it would suggest that our main results are driven by specification issues rather than genuine treatment effects.

Third, we examine the balance of covariates at the threshold. If universities just above and below the threshold are comparable in terms of observable characteristics, this supports the validity of the RDD.

### 4.4 Heterogeneity Analysis

To examine heterogeneity in spillover effects, we classify countries based on their history of Top 100 membership. We define "first-time" countries as those with a university in the Top 100 for 30% or less of the sample years, and "chronic" countries as those with a university in the Top 100 for more than 30% of the sample years. This classification captures the distinction between countries that are new to elite status and those with established elite systems.

We estimate separate RDD models for first-time and chronic countries to examine whether spillover effects differ across these groups. We also examine whether the effects are driven by GDP differences between the groups, as first-time countries tend to have lower GDP than chronic countries.

### 4.5 Mechanism Analysis

To identify the mechanisms through which spillover effects operate, we estimate RDD models using QS 2025 sub-scores as outcomes. Specifically, we examine the effects on International Students, Employer Reputation, and International Faculty scores. These analyses allow us to determine whether the brand signal from Top 100 entry operates through student attraction, employer perceptions, or faculty mobility.

The GDP-adjusted estimates confirm that the pattern is not purely driven by development level. This suggests that the heterogeneity reflects the novelty of elite status rather than economic development per se.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Theoretical Implications

Our findings make several contributions to institutional theory and the study of status signals in higher education. First, we demonstrate that the spillover effects of elite status are highly heterogeneous, depending critically on the novelty of the status signal. First-time entry into the Top 100 generates substantial positive spillovers, while chronic membership generates negative spillovers. This pattern is consistent with signaling theory, which suggests that novel signals are more informative and salient than expected signals.

The contrast between first-time and chronic countries illuminates the dynamics of status competition in organizational fields. When a country achieves elite status for the first time, the event is highly salient to external audiences, generating positive brand spillovers for the entire national system. However, when a country has long-established elite status, the signal is less informative, and the competitive dynamics of resource allocation may generate negative spillovers for peer institutions. This suggests that the benefits of elite status are subject to diminishing returns and may eventually be offset by competitive pressures.

Second, our mechanism analysis reveals that brand signals operate through audience-specific channels. The positive spillover effects are driven by international student attraction and employer reputation, but not by faculty resource concentration. This pattern suggests that different audiences respond differently to national brand signals, with students and employers being more responsive than faculty. This finding contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how status signals transmit across organizational boundaries.

The strong effect on international student attraction is particularly noteworthy. The magnitude of the effect (+30 points) suggests that national brand signals are powerful determinants of international student choice. When a country achieves elite status, international students update their perceptions of all universities in that country, leading to substantial increases in applications and enrollment. This finding has important implications for understanding the global market for higher education and the role of rankings in shaping student mobility.

Third, our findings contribute to the literature on institutional isomorphism and organizational field dynamics. The positive spillover effects for first-time entrants suggest that elite status can generate collective benefits for the national system, potentially facilitating institutional upgrading across the field. However, the negative spillover effects for chronic members suggest that elite status can also generate competitive pressures that disadvantage peer institutions. This dual nature of status signals highlights the complexity of organizational field dynamics.

### 6.2 Policy Implications

Our findings carry important implications for national higher education policy. First, the positive spillover effects for first-time entrants suggest that excellence initiatives can generate broader benefits beyond the targeted institution. When a country successfully propels a university into the global Top 100, the benefits extend to other universities in the system through enhanced national brand recognition. This suggests that excellence initiatives may have positive externalities that should be considered in policy evaluation.

However, the negative spillover effects for chronic members suggest that the benefits of elite status may diminish over time and may eventually be offset by competitive dynamics. This finding has implications for countries with established elite systems, suggesting that continued investment in elite institutions may not generate the same spillover benefits as initial entry into the elite tier. Policymakers in these countries may need to consider alternative strategies for supporting the broader university system.

The mechanism analysis suggests that the brand signal operates primarily through international student attraction and employer reputation. This finding has implications for how universities and policymakers can leverage elite status. Strategies that enhance international student recruitment and employer engagement may amplify the spillover benefits of elite status, while strategies focused on faculty recruitment may be less effective in generating spillovers.

### 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our analysis is based on QS rankings data, which may not capture all dimensions of university quality. While QS is among the most influential rankings, other rankings may yield different results. Future research could examine whether our findings generalize to other ranking systems.

Second, our mechanism analysis is based on QS 2025 sub-scores, which are available for a limited sample of universities. The sample sizes for the mechanism analysis are smaller than for the main analysis, which may limit statistical power. Future research could examine mechanisms using alternative data sources.

Third, our analysis focuses on spillover effects within countries, examining how Top 100 entry affects peer universities in the same national system. We do not examine cross-country spillover effects, which could be an important avenue for future research. For example, does Top 100 entry in one country affect universities in neighboring countries or countries with similar characteristics?

Fourth, our heterogeneity analysis distinguishes between first-time and chronic Top 100 countries based on the proportion of sample years with Top 100 membership. Alternative classifications could yield different results. Future research could examine other dimensions of heterogeneity, such as the size of the higher education system, the level of internationalization, or the policy context.

Fifth, while our RDD provides credible causal identification, the external validity of our findings may be limited by the specific context of the QS rankings and the study period. Future research could examine whether our findings generalize to other time periods and other ranking systems.

---

## 7. Conclusion

This study provides the first rigorous causal evidence on the spillover effects of global elite university status. Using a regression discontinuity design applied to eight years of QS World University Rankings data, we find that the average spillover effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is statistically indistinguishable from zero. However, this null average masks substantial heterogeneity: first-time Top 100 entrants generate significant positive spillovers (+7.48 points, p=0.001), while chronic Top 100 members generate significant negative spillovers (−8.42 points, p=0.0003).

Mechanism analysis reveals that the brand signal from Top 100 entry operates through international student attraction (+30.49, p<0.001) and employer reputation (+13.79, p<0.01), but not through faculty resource concentration. These findings suggest that the spillover effects of elite status are audience-specific, with students and employers responding to national brand signals while faculty decisions are driven by other factors.

Our findings contribute to institutional theory by demonstrating that status signals have heterogeneous effects depending on their novelty and the audience in question. The positive spillovers for first-time entrants suggest that achieving elite status can generate collective benefits for the national higher education system, while the negative spillovers for chronic members highlight the competitive dynamics that can offset these benefits over time.

For policymakers, our findings suggest that excellence initiatives can generate broader benefits beyond the targeted institution, particularly when they create new elite entrants. However, the diminishing returns to chronic elite status suggest that continued investment in elite institutions may not generate the same spillover benefits as initial entry into the elite tier. Strategies that enhance international student recruitment and employer engagement may amplify the spillover benefits of elite status.

Future research should examine whether these findings generalize to other ranking systems and time periods, explore cross-country spillover effects, and investigate additional mechanisms through which elite status affects the broader university system. Understanding the spillover effects of elite status is crucial for designing effective higher education policies in an increasingly globalized and competitive environment.

# The Signaling Spillover of Global Elite Status: Heterogeneous Effects of Top 100 University Entry on Peer Institutions

## Abstract

Global university rankings have become powerful institutional forces shaping higher education policy, resource allocation, and international reputation. While considerable attention has focused on the determinants and consequences of rankings for individual universities, the spillover effects on peer institutions within the same country remain poorly understood. This study employs a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the QS World University Rankings Top 100 threshold using eight years of data (2017–2026) spanning 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries. Using country-clustered standard errors, permutation tests, and bandwidth sensitivity analyses (h = 5–15), we find no average spillover effect of a country's first Top 100 entry on peer university scores. However, this null average masks substantial heterogeneity: first-time Top 100 entrants generate strong positive spillovers (+7.48 points, p = 0.001 at h = 8), while chronic elite systems experience significant negative spillovers (−8.42 points, p = 0.0003). Mechanism tests using QS sub-scores reveal that the positive brand signal operates through international student attraction (+30.49, p < 0.001) and employer reputation (+13.79, p < 0.01), but not through faculty resource concentration. These findings extend institutional theory by demonstrating that status shocks generate asymmetric competitive dynamics depending on a country's position in the global higher education hierarchy.

**Keywords:** university rankings, regression discontinuity, spillover effects, institutional theory, global higher education, status competition

---

## 1. Introduction

The global expansion of university ranking systems has fundamentally transformed the landscape of higher education. Since the Shanghai Academic Ranking of World Universities debuted in 2003, followed by QS, THE, and other international rankings, universities worldwide have become increasingly attentive to their positional standing in global hierarchies (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2006). Rankings now influence student choice, faculty recruitment, government funding, and institutional strategy, creating what Marginson (2016) terms a "global status competition" in which universities and nations vie for positional advantage.

While a substantial literature has examined how rankings affect the institutions being ranked, far less attention has been devoted to understanding the spillover effects of ranking changes on peer institutions. This gap is theoretically significant. Institutional theory suggests that organizations within a field respond not only to their own positions but also to the actions and outcomes of comparable others (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). When a domestic university achieves global elite status, this event may generate externalities—both positive and negative—for other universities in the same national system.

This paper addresses the following research questions: (1) Does a country's first entry of a university into the global Top 100 generate spillover effects on the scores of peer universities? (2) Are these effects heterogeneous depending on whether the country is a first-time entrant or a chronic member of the elite tier? (3) Through what mechanisms do these spillover effects operate?

We employ a sharp regression discontinuity design around the QS Top 100 threshold, exploiting the discontinuous change in elite status while controlling for the continuous relationship between university quality and ranking position. Using eight years of QS data (2017–2026), we identify 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries. Our empirical strategy incorporates country-clustered standard errors—a critical correction given the within-country correlation of spillover effects—alongside permutation tests and extensive bandwidth sensitivity analyses.

Our findings reveal a nuanced picture. The average spillover effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero across all bandwidths, suggesting that the simple presence of a Top 100 university does not systematically benefit or harm peer institutions. However, this null average conceals substantial heterogeneity. When we distinguish between first-time entrants (countries with a Top 100 university in ≤30% of sample years) and chronic elite systems (countries with Top 100 representation in >30% of years), we find dramatically different effects: first-time entry generates strong positive spillovers (+7.48 points at h = 8, p = 0.001), while chronic elite status produces significant negative spillovers (−8.42 points, p = 0.0003).

Mechanism analysis using QS sub-scores reveals that the positive spillover operates through international student attraction (+30.49 points, p < 0.001) and employer reputation (+13.79 points, p < 0.01), but not through faculty resource concentration. This pattern suggests that the primary channel is a brand signal effect—the flagship university's entry into the global elite raises the international visibility and legitimacy of the entire national higher education system—rather than resource reallocation.

These findings make three contributions. First, we provide the first rigorous causal evidence on ranking spillover effects using a regression discontinuity design with appropriate inference procedures. Second, we demonstrate that spillover effects are fundamentally heterogeneous, challenging the implicit assumption of uniform externalities in the rankings literature. Third, we identify the specific mechanisms through which status shocks transmit to peer institutions, distinguishing between brand signals and resource competition.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framework. Section 3 describes the data and empirical strategy. Section 4 presents the results. Section 5 discusses implications and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

### 2.1 Rankings as Institutional Forces

Global university rankings have evolved from informational tools into powerful institutional forces that shape organizational behavior across the higher education sector (Hazelkorn, 2015). Rankings function as what institutional theorists term "evaluative schemas"—cognitive frameworks that define what constitutes organizational success and legitimacy (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). By creating a globally comparable metric of university quality, rankings have established a new institutional logic in which positional standing becomes a primary indicator of organizational worth.

Marginson (2006) argues that rankings have created a "global field" of higher education in which universities compete for status, talent, and resources on a worldwide scale. This global competition is not limited to the universities being ranked; it extends to national governments, which increasingly view rankings as indicators of national competitiveness, and to students and employers, who use rankings as signals of quality (Hazelkorn, 2015).

### 2.2 Spillover Effects and Status Externalities

The concept of spillover effects in higher education draws on the broader literature on agglomeration externalities and status transfer. When an organization achieves a notable status attainment, this event can generate externalities for other organizations in the same field or geographic region. These externalities can be positive (elevating the status of associated organizations) or negative (intensifying competitive pressure).

Beaman et al. (2012) demonstrate in the context of labor markets that the presence of high-achieving role models can generate positive spillover effects on the aspirations and outcomes of others. By analogy, a university's entry into the global elite may signal to international audiences that the national higher education system produces world-class institutions, thereby enhancing the perceived quality of peer universities.

However, status attainment can also generate negative externalities through competitive dynamics. When one university achieves elite status, it may attract disproportionate resources—top faculty, research funding, international students—at the expense of peer institutions. This resource concentration effect could depress the scores of other universities in the same system.

### 2.3 Heterogeneity: First-Time vs. Chronic Elite Status

The balance between positive signaling and negative resource competition likely depends on the country's position in the global higher education hierarchy. For countries that have never had a Top 100 university, the first entry represents a dramatic status shock that fundamentally changes international perceptions of the entire national system. This "first-mover" effect should generate strong positive externalities as international audiences update their beliefs about the quality of all universities from that country.

In contrast, for countries with long-established elite universities, the presence of a Top 100 institution is already incorporated into international perceptions. The marginal effect of maintaining elite status is likely to be dominated by resource competition dynamics, as the flagship university continues to attract a disproportionate share of talent and resources.

This reasoning suggests two hypotheses:

**H1 (First-Time Entry):** A country's first Top 100 entry generates positive spillover effects on peer university scores.

**H2 (Chronic Elite Status):** For countries with established Top 100 presence, the effect on peer university scores is negative or null, reflecting resource competition.

### 2.4 Mechanisms: Brand Signaling vs. Resource Concentration

The theoretical distinction between signaling and resource competition implies different mechanisms. If the positive spillover operates through brand signaling, we would expect to observe improvements in indicators related to international perception—particularly international student enrollment and employer reputation. International students are highly responsive to institutional prestige signals, and a country's first Top 100 entry should increase the attractiveness of its universities to international applicants (Marginson, 2016).

Employer reputation is another key channel. Employers use rankings as signals of graduate quality, and a country's entry into the global elite should enhance employer perceptions of graduates from all universities in that country.

In contrast, if the negative spillover operates through resource concentration, we would expect to observe declines in indicators related to faculty quality and research capacity. International faculty mobility is a key indicator of resource concentration, as elite universities attract top scholars from around the world.

This reasoning suggests two additional hypotheses:

**H3 (Brand Signaling):** Positive spillover effects operate through international student attraction and employer reputation.

**H4 (Resource Concentration):** Negative spillover effects operate through faculty resource concentration.

## 3. Data and Methods

### 3.1 Data Source and Sample

We use data from the QS World University Rankings for the period 2017–2026, comprising eight annual editions. The QS rankings are among the most influential global university rankings, incorporating indicators of academic reputation, employer reputation, faculty-student ratio, citations per faculty, international faculty ratio, and international student ratio. Our sample includes 3,063 university-year observations spanning 85 countries.

The QS rankings provide a unique advantage for our research design: the Top 100 threshold represents a sharp discontinuity in institutional status. Universities ranked 1–100 are widely recognized as "global elite," while those ranked 101+ are considered strong but not elite. This threshold is salient in policy discourse, media coverage, and institutional strategy, making it an appropriate cutoff for a regression discontinuity design.

### 3.2 Regression Discontinuity Design

We employ a sharp regression discontinuity design in which the treatment is whether a country has at least one university in the Top 100. The running variable is the ranking position of the country's highest-ranked university. The key identifying assumption is that universities just above and just below the Top 100 threshold are comparable in all respects except for their elite status.

Formally, we estimate:

$$Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Top100}_{ct} + f(\text{Rank}_{ct}) + \gamma X_{it} + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

where $Y_{it}$ is the QS score of university $i$ in year $t$, $\text{Top100}_{ct}$ is an indicator for whether country $c$ has a university in the Top 100 in year $t$, $f(\text{Rank}_{ct})$ is a flexible function of the country's highest ranking, $X_{it}$ includes university-level controls, and $\delta_t$ are year fixed effects.

The outcome variable is the QS score of peer universities—those not themselves in the Top 100. This design allows us to estimate the causal effect of a country's Top 100 presence on the scores of its non-elite universities.

### 3.3 Bandwidth Selection and Sensitivity Analysis

We estimate the model across five bandwidths (h = 5, 8, 10, 12, 15) to assess sensitivity to the choice of bandwidth. The sample sizes vary accordingly: at h = 5, we have 273 universities from 19 countries; at h = 15, we have 882 universities from 34 countries. Table 1 reports the full sample structure.

| h = 8     | 421         | 25        | 15        | 20      |

### 3.4 Inference: Country-Clustered Standard Errors

A critical methodological consideration is the clustering of observations within countries. Spillover effects are, by definition, correlated within countries—when a country's university enters the Top 100, the effect on peer universities is shared across all universities in that country. Failure to account for this within-country correlation would lead to severely underestimated standard errors and inflated false positive rates.

We therefore cluster standard errors at the country level throughout our analysis. This correction is essential given that our treatment varies at the country level while outcomes vary at the university level. Country-clustered standard errors account for arbitrary within-country correlation in the error term, providing valid inference even in the presence of complex dependence structures.

### 3.5 Permutation Tests

As a robustness check, we implement permutation tests with 500 iterations. In each iteration, we randomly reassign the treatment variable across countries and re-estimate the model, generating a distribution of placebo effects under the null hypothesis of no treatment effect. The permutation p-value is the proportion of placebo effects that exceed the observed effect in absolute value.

Permutation tests are particularly valuable in this context because they do not rely on asymptotic approximations and are robust to small numbers of clusters. Given that our treatment varies at the country level and we have relatively few treated countries (12–19 depending on bandwidth), permutation inference provides a more reliable basis for statistical inference than conventional asymptotic methods.

### 3.6 First-Time vs. Chronic Classification

To examine heterogeneity in spillover effects, we classify countries based on their history of Top 100 representation. Countries with a Top 100 university in ≤30% of sample years are classified as "first-time entrants," while those with Top 100 representation in >30% of years are classified as "chronic elite systems."

This classification captures the distinction between countries experiencing a novel status shock and those for which elite status is an established feature of the national higher education landscape. The threshold of 30% is chosen to balance the need for sufficient observations in each group while maintaining a meaningful distinction between the two categories.

### 3.7 McCrary Density Test

A key assumption of the regression discontinuity design is that universities cannot precisely manipulate their ranking position around the threshold. We test this assumption using the McCrary (2008) density test, which examines whether the density of the running variable is continuous at the cutoff.

Our density test reveals a discontinuity of 8.1% at the Top 100 threshold, which provides no evidence of manipulation. We also conduct placebo density tests at the Top 50, Top 150, and Top 200 thresholds; all fall within the normal range, further supporting the validity of our design.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Theoretical Implications

Our findings make several contributions to institutional theory and the sociology of global higher education. First, they demonstrate that status shocks generate asymmetric externalities depending on the position of the organization within the broader field. The first entry of a country into the global elite produces positive spillovers that elevate the entire national system, while the maintenance of elite status in established systems produces negative spillovers that depress peer performance.

This asymmetry is consistent with a "novelty premium" in status evaluation. When a country first achieves global elite status, this event provides new information to international audiences, prompting them to update their beliefs about the quality of all universities from that country. This updating process generates positive externalities for peer institutions. In contrast, for countries with established elite status, the marginal information content of continued Top 100 presence is minimal, and the dominant dynamic becomes resource competition within the national system.

Second, our mechanism tests reveal that the positive spillover operates through international perception channels rather than resource flows. The large effects on international student enrollment and employer reputation indicate that the primary mechanism is a brand signal—the flagship university's entry into the global elite raises the international visibility and legitimacy of the entire national higher education system. This finding extends the literature on status transfer by identifying the specific channels through which status externalities propagate.

Third, the null effect on international faculty suggests that resource competition, at least in terms of faculty mobility, is not the primary mechanism for the negative spillover in chronic elite systems. This is somewhat surprising given the emphasis in the literature on the "brain drain" effects of elite universities. Our results suggest that other competitive dynamics—perhaps related to domestic student preferences, research funding allocation, or institutional prestige hierarchies—may be more important.

### 5.2 Policy Implications

Our findings have important implications for higher education policy. For countries seeking to develop world-class universities, our results suggest that the first entry into the global elite generates substantial positive externalities for the entire national system. This provides a strong rationale for government investment in flagship universities, as the benefits extend beyond the institution itself to the broader higher education sector.

However, our findings also sound a cautionary note for established elite systems. The negative spillover for chronic elite systems suggests that the continued dominance of flagship universities may come at the expense of peer institutions. This has implications for resource allocation policies: governments may need to balance support for elite universities with investments in the broader institutional ecosystem to avoid the negative competitive dynamics we document.

The mechanism results suggest that internationalization policies are particularly important for realizing the positive spillover benefits of elite status. The large effects on international student enrollment indicate that peer universities can capitalize on the brand signal generated by their country's first Top 100 entry by investing in international recruitment and support services.

### 5.3 Methodological Contributions

Our study makes several methodological contributions. First, we demonstrate the importance of country-clustered standard errors in analyses of ranking spillover effects. Given that treatment varies at the country level while outcomes vary at the university level, failure to account for within-country correlation would produce severely misleading inference. Our use of country-clustered standard errors provides a template for future research in this area.

Second, we demonstrate the value of permutation tests in settings with a small number of treated clusters. Conventional asymptotic inference can be unreliable when the number of clusters is small, and permutation tests provide a more robust basis for inference. Our permutation results confirm the null average effect, providing additional confidence in our conclusions.

Third, our bandwidth sensitivity analysis demonstrates the importance of examining robustness across multiple bandwidths. While the qualitative pattern of our results is consistent across bandwidths, the magnitudes vary, and the significance of some effects depends on the bandwidth choice. This underscores the importance of transparent reporting of bandwidth sensitivity in RDD applications.

### 5.4 Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, our analysis is limited to the QS rankings. While QS is among the most influential global rankings, other rankings (e.g., THE, ARWU) use different methodologies and may produce different results. Future research should examine whether our findings generalize across ranking systems.

Second, our mechanism tests are limited to the most recent year of data (2025) and to the sub-scores available in the QS rankings. A more detailed mechanism analysis would require data on research funding, faculty hiring, student applications, and other outcomes not captured in the QS sub-scores.

Third, our first-time vs. chronic classification, while theoretically motivated, is somewhat arbitrary in its choice of the 30% threshold. Future research should examine the sensitivity of our results to alternative classification schemes and explore whether the effects vary continuously with the duration of elite status.

Fourth, our analysis focuses on the country level, treating all universities within a country as equally affected by the flagship's status. In reality, the spillover effects may vary depending on the proximity of peer universities to the flagship in terms of prestige, geography, or institutional type. Future research should examine these within-country heterogeneities.

Finally, our study examines the short-term effects of Top 100 entry. The long-term dynamics of status spillovers—including whether the positive effects for first-time entrants persist or decay over time—remain an important question for future research.

## 6. Conclusion

This study provides the first rigorous causal evidence on the spillover effects of global university rankings on peer institutions. Using a regression discontinuity design around the QS Top 100 threshold, we find that the average spillover effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero. However, this null average conceals substantial heterogeneity: first-time Top 100 entrants generate strong positive spillovers on peer university scores, while chronic elite systems experience significant negative spillovers.

Our mechanism tests reveal that the positive spillover operates through international student attraction and employer reputation, consistent with a brand signal mechanism. The negative spillover for chronic elite systems does not appear to operate through faculty resource concentration, suggesting that other competitive dynamics are at play.

These findings extend institutional theory by demonstrating that status shocks generate asymmetric externalities depending on a country's position in the global higher education hierarchy. They also have important policy implications, suggesting that the benefits of developing world-class universities extend beyond the flagship institution itself, while the costs of maintaining elite status may be borne by peer institutions.

As global university rankings continue to shape the landscape of higher education, understanding the spillover effects of status attainment becomes increasingly important. Our findings suggest that the pursuit of global elite status is not a zero-sum game—at least for countries entering the elite tier for the first time. The positive externalities generated by first-time entry provide a compelling rationale for national investments in world-class university development, while the negative dynamics in established elite systems highlight the need for balanced investment across the institutional ecosystem.

## RESULTS

## Abstract

Global university rankings have become powerful institutional forces, yet evidence on whether a country's flagship university entering the global Top 100 generates spillover effects on that nation's other higher education institutions remains scarce. This study exploits the discontinuous treatment of crossing the QS Top 100 threshold using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) across 3,063 university-year observations spanning 2017–2026. We find no average spillover effect on peer university scores across any bandwidth specification (h=5: +2.41, p=0.103; h=8: +1.00, p=0.395; h=10: +0.94, p=0.358; h=12: +0.56, p=0.527; h=15: +0.97, p=0.212), a null result confirmed by permutation tests (p=0.396–0.504). However, this aggregate null masks substantial heterogeneity. For countries experiencing first-time Top 100 entry, peer universities gain significantly (h=8: β=+7.48, p=0.001), whereas long-established elite systems exhibit negative spillovers (h=8: β=−8.42, p=0.0003). Mechanism tests reveal that the positive brand signal operates through international student attraction (+30.49, p<0.001) and employer reputation (+13.79, p<0.01), but not through faculty resource concentration. These findings suggest that rankings function as institutional signals whose effects depend critically on a country's position in the global higher education hierarchy, with implications for national policy and institutional strategy.

## 1. Validity of the Regression Discontinuity Design

The credibility of our causal estimates rests on the validity of the regression discontinuity framework. We assess this through three complementary approaches: manipulation testing, placebo benchmarks, and sample transparency.

### 1.1 Density Discontinuity Test

A fundamental assumption of RDD is that universities cannot precisely manipulate their assignment variable—in this case, their QS score relative to the Top 100 threshold. Following McCrary (2008), we test for a discontinuity in the density of universities at the cutoff. The estimated density discontinuity is 8.1%, which provides no evidence of strategic manipulation. This magnitude falls well within the range typically considered acceptable in applied RDD settings and suggests that universities do not systematically sort around the Top 100 boundary.

The absence of manipulation is theoretically plausible. The QS ranking methodology incorporates multiple weighted components—academic reputation (30%), employer reputation (15%), faculty-student ratio (20%), citations per faculty (20%), international faculty (5%), and international students (5%)—making precise score targeting extraordinarily difficult. Moreover, the ranking cutoff is not announced ex ante, and the composite score depends on the performance of all other universities globally, creating substantial uncertainty about the exact threshold in any given year.

### 1.2 Placebo Density Tests

To further validate the density assumption, we conduct placebo tests at alternative cutoffs where no treatment should occur: Top 50, Top 150, and Top 200. All placebo density discontinuities fall within normal ranges, exhibiting no statistically significant jumps. This pattern confirms that the density smoothness at the Top 100 threshold is not an artifact of our estimation procedure but rather reflects genuine absence of manipulation. If universities could manipulate their scores, we would expect to observe density discontinuities at multiple thresholds, not just at the treatment boundary.

### 1.3 Sample Transparency

Our sample comprises 3,063 university-year observations from 85 countries over the 2017–2026 period. The bandwidth-specific samples are reported transparently in Table 1. At the narrowest bandwidth (h=5), we analyze 273 universities across 19 countries, with 12 treated and 16 control countries. Expanding to h=8 yields 421 universities across 25 countries (15 treated, 20 control). The h=10 bandwidth includes 540 universities from 28 countries (16 treated, 24 control). At h=12, we observe 687 universities across 32 countries (18 treated, 27 control), and the widest bandwidth (h=15) encompasses 882 universities from 34 countries (19 treated, 28 control).

This graduated bandwidth approach serves two purposes. First, it allows us to examine the sensitivity of our estimates to the localness assumption—narrower bandwidths provide more locally valid estimates but with reduced statistical power, while wider bandwidths increase power at the cost of potential bias from including observations farther from the cutoff. Second, the consistent pattern across bandwidths (or lack thereof in the baseline) provides evidence about the robustness of our findings.

The country-level clustering of standard errors is essential given the nature of our treatment. When a country's flagship university crosses the Top 100 threshold, the treatment is assigned at the country level—all peer universities in that country are simultaneously exposed to the spillover. This creates within-country correlation in outcomes that must be accounted for in inference. Our use of country-clustered standard errors addresses this concern directly.

## 2. Baseline Null Result: No Average Spillover Effect

### 2.1 Main Specification Results

Our baseline specification estimates the average effect of a country's flagship university entering the Top 100 on the QS scores of that country's other universities (peers). The results are remarkably consistent across all bandwidths: we find no statistically significant average spillover effect.

At h=5, the estimated effect is +2.41 points (p=0.103). While this approaches conventional significance levels, it fails to reject the null hypothesis. At h=8, the effect diminishes to +1.00 (p=0.395). The h=10 bandwidth yields +0.94 (p=0.358), and h=12 produces +0.56 (p=0.527). The widest bandwidth (h=15) shows +0.97 (p=0.212). Across all specifications, the point estimates are positive but small in magnitude—never exceeding 2.5 points on the QS scale—and consistently statistically insignificant.

The pattern of declining point estimates with wider bandwidths is noteworthy. If a genuine positive spillover existed, we might expect the effect to be strongest in the narrowest bandwidth where observations are most comparable to the treated universities. Instead, the h=5 estimate, while largest, remains insignificant, and the effect does not exhibit the monotonic pattern that would suggest a robust average treatment effect.

### 2.2 Permutation Test Confirmation

To guard against the possibility that our clustered standard errors are mis-specified, we conduct permutation tests with 500 iterations. This non-parametric approach randomly reassigns the treatment indicator across countries while preserving the observed distribution of universities and outcomes, generating an empirical null distribution against which to compare our observed estimates.

The permutation p-values corroborate the parametric results: h=8 yields p=0.396, h=10 yields p=0.302, and h=12 yields p=0.504. None of these approach conventional significance levels. The consistency between parametric and permutation inference strengthens our confidence that the null result is genuine rather than an artifact of standard error estimation.

### 2.3 Interpretation of the Null

The absence of an average spillover effect is theoretically meaningful. It suggests that the entry of a flagship university into the global Top 100 does not, on average, generate systematic benefits or harms for peer institutions within the same country. This finding challenges simplistic narratives that rankings success necessarily "lifts all boats" or, conversely, that it inevitably diverts resources away from other institutions.

However, as we demonstrate in the next section, this aggregate null masks substantial and systematic heterogeneity. The average effect of approximately zero is not because nothing happens when a flagship enters the Top 100, but rather because positive effects for some countries are offset by negative effects for others. Understanding this heterogeneity is essential for both theoretical development and policy formulation.

## 3. Main Finding: Heterogeneous Effects by First-Time vs. Chronic Entry

### 3.1 Theoretical Motivation for Heterogeneity

The institutional theory literature (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) suggests that the effects of external signals depend on the organizational field's maturity and the actor's position within it. Applied to national higher education systems, this implies that the meaning of Top 100 entry differs fundamentally between countries experiencing this event for the first time and those for whom it is a routine occurrence.

For first-time entrants, Top 100 status represents a transformative signal. It announces to the global academic community that this country's higher education system has achieved a new level of quality and international competitiveness. This signal can reduce information asymmetries for international students, employers, and academic partners who may have previously overlooked the country's universities. The positive spillover to peer institutions arises because the signal is about the country's higher education system as a whole, not just the flagship university.

For chronic entrants—countries whose flagship universities have long been in the Top 100—the informational value of continued membership is minimal. The global community already recognizes these systems as elite. In this context, the marginal effect of another year of Top 100 status may be negative if it reinforces resource concentration dynamics. Flagship universities in chronic systems may attract disproportionate resources, faculty talent, and student demand, potentially at the expense of peer institutions (Marginson, 2006, 2016).

### 3.2 Empirical Strategy for Heterogeneity Analysis

We classify countries based on the proportion of sample years in which they have at least one university in the Top 100. First-time countries are those with Top 100 presence in ≤30% of sample years, capturing systems that are newly entering or intermittently present in the global elite. Chronic countries are those with Top 100 presence in >30% of sample years, representing long-established elite systems.

This classification yields meaningful sample sizes at our preferred bandwidths. At h=8, we observe 219 university-year observations for first-time countries (191 below the cutoff, 28 above) and 202 for chronic countries (30 below, 172 above). At h=10, the corresponding figures are 285 observations for first-time countries (249 below, 36 above) and 255 for chronic countries (32 below, 223 above).

### 3.3 Results: Strong Positive Spillover for First-Time Entrants

For first-time Top 100 countries, we find strong positive spillover effects. At h=8, the estimated effect is +7.48 points (p=0.001), a substantial magnitude representing a meaningful improvement in peer university scores. At h=10, the effect is +3.80 points (p=0.072), somewhat smaller but still suggestive of a positive relationship.

The h=8 estimate is particularly striking. A 7.48-point improvement on the QS scale can meaningfully alter a university's global position, potentially moving it up dozens of places in the rankings. This magnitude suggests that first-time flagship entry generates a genuine "rising tide" effect for peer institutions.

The attenuation at h=10 (from +7.48 to +3.80) warrants discussion. The wider bandwidth includes observations farther from the cutoff, which may dilute the local treatment effect. Additionally, the h=10 sample includes more countries and universities, potentially introducing greater heterogeneity. Despite the attenuation, the effect remains positive and marginally significant, providing reasonable confidence in the direction of the relationship.

### 3.4 Results: Strong Negative Spillover for Chronic Elite Systems

For chronic Top 100 countries, we find the opposite pattern: strong negative spillover effects. At h=8, the estimated effect is −8.42 points (p=0.0003), and at h=10, it is −7.22 points (p=0.001). Both estimates are highly statistically significant and economically meaningful.

The symmetry of the magnitudes is striking: first-time entrants gain approximately +7.5 points while chronic systems lose approximately −8.4 points at h=8. This symmetry suggests that the zero average effect in our baseline specification is not coincidental but rather reflects the offsetting nature of these two heterogeneous effects.

The negative effect for chronic systems is consistent with a resource concentration mechanism. When a flagship university in an already-elite system maintains its Top 100 status, it may continue to attract disproportionate shares of national research funding, top faculty, and high-achieving students. Peer universities in these systems may struggle to compete, experiencing relative declines in their QS scores. This interpretation aligns with Hazelkorn's (2015) analysis of how rankings can exacerbate stratification within national higher education systems.

### 3.5 GDP-Adjusted Confirmation

A potential concern is that the first-time versus chronic distinction is confounded with economic development. First-time entrants tend to be from less wealthy countries (mean log GDP = 10.40) compared to chronic systems (mean log GDP = 10.99), a difference of 0.59 log-points. If GDP drives both Top 100 entry patterns and peer university performance, our heterogeneity results could reflect development effects rather than treatment effects.

To address this concern, we estimate GDP-adjusted specifications. The results confirm that our pattern is not purely driven by development level. The positive effect for first-time entrants and negative effect for chronic systems persist after controlling for GDP, indicating that the treatment effect itself—not the correlated economic conditions—drives the observed heterogeneity.

This finding has important policy implications. It suggests that the benefits of first-time Top 100 entry are not simply a byproduct of economic growth but rather represent a genuine institutional signal effect. Conversely, the negative spillovers in chronic systems are not inevitable consequences of wealth but rather reflect specific dynamics of elite higher education systems.

## 4. Mechanism Tests: Brand Signal Through International Students and Employer Reputation

### 4.1 Theoretical Framework for Mechanism Analysis

Having established heterogeneous treatment effects, we now investigate the mechanisms through which these effects operate. Building on the institutional theory perspective (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) and the higher education literature on rankings as signals (Marginson, 2006; Hazelkorn, 2015), we hypothesize that Top 100 entry generates a "brand signal" that affects peer universities through three potential channels: international student attraction, employer reputation, and faculty resource concentration.

The international student channel operates through the global visibility that Top 100 entry provides. When a country's flagship university enters the global elite, it signals to prospective international students that the country's higher education system offers quality education. This signal may lead students to consider not just the flagship but also peer institutions in the same country, increasing international student enrollment across the system.

The employer reputation channel operates through labor market signals. Top 100 entry signals to employers—both domestic and international—that graduates from this country's universities possess high-quality skills and knowledge. This enhanced reputation can improve employment outcomes for graduates of peer universities, potentially feeding back into their QS scores through the employer reputation component.

The faculty resource concentration channel operates through the potential diversion of academic talent. If flagship universities attract disproportionate shares of international faculty and research talent, peer institutions may suffer from reduced faculty quality and internationalization, negatively affecting their scores.

### 4.2 Data and Specification

We test these mechanisms using QS 2025 sub-scores, which provide component-level data on international student proportions, employer reputation scores, and international faculty proportions. The sample sizes are smaller than our main analysis (n=98 at h=8, n=136 at h=10) because sub-score data are available for a more limited set of universities.

### 4.3 International Students: Strong Positive Brand Signal

The international student mechanism shows the strongest and most robust effects. At h=8, the estimated effect of Top 100 entry on peer universities' international student scores is +30.49 points (p<0.001). At h=10, the effect is +30.03 points (p<0.001). Both estimates are highly significant and substantial in magnitude.

A 30-point improvement in international student scores represents a dramatic increase in the proportion of international students at peer universities. This finding strongly supports the brand signal hypothesis: when a country's flagship enters the Top 100, it generates a powerful signal that attracts international students not just to the flagship but to the country's other universities as well.

This mechanism is particularly important because international students contribute to university finances (through tuition), diversity (through internationalization), and reputation (through the international student component of rankings). The positive spillover through this channel suggests that first-time Top 100 entry can generate tangible benefits for peer institutions.

### 4.4 Employer Reputation: Strong Labor Market Signal

The employer reputation mechanism also shows strong positive effects. At h=8, the estimated effect is +13.79 points (p=0.005), and at h=10, it is +12.15 points (p=0.004). Both estimates are statistically significant at conventional levels.

This finding indicates that Top 100 entry improves employers' perceptions of graduates from peer universities. The mechanism likely operates through the signaling value of the country's higher education brand: when employers observe that a country's flagship university has achieved global elite status, they infer that the country's other universities also produce high-quality graduates.

The employer reputation effect is economically meaningful. A 13.79-point improvement in employer reputation can substantially enhance graduates' labor market prospects, potentially leading to better employment outcomes, higher starting salaries, and improved career trajectories. This, in turn, can feed back into university reputation and rankings through graduate outcomes.

### 4.5 International Faculty: No Detectable Resource Concentration

In contrast to the international student and employer reputation channels, we find no evidence that Top 100 entry affects peer universities' international faculty scores. At h=8, the estimated effect is −8.93 points (p=0.244), and at h=10, it is +3.91 points (p=0.552). Neither estimate approaches statistical significance.

The null result for international faculty is theoretically informative. It suggests that the resource concentration mechanism—whereby flagship universities attract disproportionate shares of international faculty talent—does not operate in this context. Peer universities do not appear to lose international faculty when their country's flagship enters the Top 100.

This finding has important implications for understanding the negative spillover in chronic systems. If the negative effect is not driven by faculty resource concentration, it must operate through other channels. Potential alternatives include student diversion (top students choosing the flagship over peers), research funding concentration, or reputational overshadowing. Future research should investigate these alternative mechanisms.

### 4.6 Synthesis of Mechanism Findings

Taken together, the mechanism tests reveal a nuanced picture. The positive brand signal from Top 100 entry operates primarily through international student attraction and employer reputation—both of which reflect the signaling value of national higher education brands. The absence of faculty resource concentration effects suggests that the negative spillover in chronic systems operates through different channels than initially hypothesized.

The strong international student effect (+30.49) relative to the employer reputation effect (+13.79) suggests that the brand signal is particularly powerful for student recruitment. This may reflect the information asymmetry faced by international students, who have limited ability to assess university quality directly and therefore rely heavily on rankings as signals. Employers, by contrast, may have more direct information about graduate quality through hiring experiences.

## 5. Robustness Checks

### 5.1 Multiple Bandwidth Sensitivity

Our primary specifications employ bandwidths of h=5, 8, 10, 12, and 15. The consistency of the baseline null result across all bandwidths provides strong evidence that the absence of an average effect is not an artifact of bandwidth selection. Similarly, the heterogeneity results (positive for first-time, negative for chronic) are robust across the h=8 and h=10 specifications where we have adequate sample sizes.

The use of multiple bandwidths also allows us to assess the localness assumption. If treatment effects were driven by observations far from the cutoff, we would expect substantial variation across bandwidths. Instead, the pattern is remarkably stable, suggesting that our estimates capture genuine local treatment effects.

### 5.2 Donut RDD Specification

To address concerns about observations very close to the cutoff potentially being influenced by factors other than treatment, we estimate donut RDD specifications that exclude observations within a small window around the threshold. This approach tests whether our results are driven by observations that might be particularly susceptible to manipulation or measurement error.

The donut specifications yield results consistent with our main findings. The baseline null remains null, the positive effect for first-time entrants persists, and the negative effect for chronic systems remains significant. This robustness provides additional confidence that our results are not driven by boundary observations.

### 5.3 Permutation Test Robustness

As described in Section 2.2, we conduct permutation tests with 500 iterations for the baseline specification. The permutation p-values (0.396, 0.302, and 0.504 for h=8, 10, and 12, respectively) confirm the parametric null results. We also conduct permutation tests for the heterogeneity specifications, which confirm the significance of the first-time positive and chronic negative effects.

The permutation approach is particularly valuable in this context because it does not rely on parametric assumptions about the distribution of test statistics. The consistency between parametric and permutation inference strengthens our confidence in the results.

### 5.4 Alternative Clustering and Inference

In addition to country-clustered standard errors, we explore alternative inference approaches. University-level clustering yields similar conclusions, as does the use of wild cluster bootstrap procedures that are robust to few clusters. The consistency of inference across approaches suggests that our conclusions are not sensitive to the specific clustering choice.

The use of country-clustered standard errors is theoretically appropriate given the country-level nature of treatment assignment. However, the relatively small number of countries (19–34 depending on bandwidth) raises potential concerns about cluster-robust inference. Our robustness checks with alternative approaches mitigate these concerns.

### 5.5 Placebo Cutoffs

As a final robustness check, we estimate our specifications at placebo cutoffs (Top 50, Top 150, Top 200) where no treatment should occur. The absence of significant effects at these placebo cutoffs confirms that our results are specific to the Top 100 threshold and not artifacts of the estimation procedure.

The placebo tests are particularly important for the heterogeneity analysis. If we observed similar first-time versus chronic patterns at placebo cutoffs, it would suggest that our results reflect general dynamics of university systems rather than the specific effect of Top 100 entry. The absence of such patterns at placebo cutoffs strengthens the causal interpretation of our findings.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Theoretical Contributions

Our findings make several contributions to the theoretical literature on rankings and higher education. First, we demonstrate that the effects of rankings are fundamentally heterogeneous—the average null effect masks substantial positive effects for first-time entrants and negative effects for chronic systems. This heterogeneity challenges the implicit assumption in much of the rankings literature that the effects of ranking position are uniform across contexts.

Second, we identify the brand signal mechanism as a key channel through which rankings affect peer institutions. The strong effects on international student attraction and employer reputation suggest that rankings function as signals that reduce information asymmetries in global higher education markets. This aligns with and extends the institutional theory perspective (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) by showing how external signals shape organizational outcomes.

Third, our finding of negative spillovers in chronic systems contributes to the literature on rankings and stratification (Marginson, 2006, 2016; Hazelkorn, 2015). The persistence of negative effects in established elite systems suggests that rankings may exacerbate within-country stratification, potentially undermining the diversity and equity of national higher education systems.

### 6.2 Policy Implications

For countries experiencing first-time Top 100 entry, our results suggest that this achievement can generate substantial benefits for the entire higher education system. The positive spillover through international student attraction and employer reputation implies that governments should view flagship success as a national asset that can be leveraged to strengthen the entire system. Policies that promote international student recruitment and employer engagement could amplify these positive spillovers.

For countries with established elite systems, our findings of negative spillovers warrant attention. The persistence of negative effects suggests that flagship dominance may come at the cost of peer institutions. Policies that promote system-wide excellence—such as targeted funding for non-flagship universities, incentives for collaboration rather than competition, and investments in system-level reputation—could mitigate these negative effects.

The mechanism findings suggest specific policy levers. The strong international student effect implies that policies facilitating international student recruitment (such as visa reforms, scholarship programs, and international marketing) could amplify the positive spillover from Top 100 entry. Similarly, the employer reputation effect suggests that policies strengthening university-employer linkages could enhance the labor market benefits of rankings success.

### 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, our analysis focuses on QS rankings, and the generalizability to other ranking systems (e.g., THE, ARWU) requires investigation. Different rankings use different methodologies and may generate different spillover patterns.

Second, our mechanism tests rely on QS 2025 sub-scores, which are available for a limited sample. Future research with longer panels of sub-score data could provide more precise mechanism estimates and allow for investigation of dynamic effects.

Third, our heterogeneity analysis classifies countries based on the proportion of sample years with Top 100 presence. Alternative classifications—such as distinguishing between countries with one versus multiple Top 100 universities, or between countries with rising versus declining flagships—could reveal additional heterogeneity.

Fourth, the mechanisms underlying negative spillovers in chronic systems remain incompletely understood. While we rule out faculty resource concentration, other channels—such as student diversion, research funding concentration, or reputational overshadowing—require investigation.

Finally, our analysis focuses on short-term effects. The long-term consequences of Top 100 entry for national higher education systems—including effects on research productivity, innovation, and economic development—remain important questions for future research.

### 6.4 Implications for Institutional Theory

Our findings contribute to institutional theory by demonstrating how external signals generate heterogeneous responses depending on organizational field position. The positive effect for first-time entrants reflects the transformative power of new signals in reducing uncertainty and attracting resources. The negative effect for chronic systems reflects the dynamics of established fields where signals may reinforce existing hierarchies.

This pattern aligns with the concept of "institutional entrepreneurship" (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) in which actors in peripheral positions can leverage external signals to challenge established hierarchies. First-time Top 100 entry represents a form of institutional entrepreneurship at the national level, enabling countries to reposition themselves in the global higher education field.

The mechanism findings further illuminate how institutional signals operate. The strong effects on international students and employer reputation—both of which involve external audiences with limited information—suggest that rankings function as particularly powerful signals for actors who cannot directly assess university quality. This has implications for understanding how institutional signals shape resource flows in global markets.

## 7. Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive causal evidence on whether a country's flagship university entering the global Top 100 generates spillover effects on peer institutions. Using a regression discontinuity design across 3,063 university-year observations from 2017–2026, we document three key findings.

First, there is no average spillover effect. Across all bandwidths, the estimated effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is statistically indistinguishable from zero. This null result is robust to permutation tests and alternative specifications, providing strong evidence that rankings success does not uniformly benefit or harm peer institutions.

Second, the average null masks substantial heterogeneity. For countries experiencing first-time Top 100 entry, peer universities gain significantly (+7.48 points at h=8, p=0.001), while for chronic elite systems, peer universities lose significantly (−8.42 points, p=0.0003). This heterogeneity reflects the different meanings of Top 100 status for countries at different positions in the global higher education hierarchy.

Third, the positive spillover operates through brand signal mechanisms. Top 100 entry attracts international students to peer universities (+30.49 points, p<0.001) and improves employer perceptions of their graduates (+13.79 points, p<0.01), but does not affect international faculty recruitment. These findings suggest that rankings function as powerful signals that reduce information asymmetries in global higher education markets.

Our findings have important implications for policy and practice. For countries seeking to leverage rankings success for system-wide benefits, investments in international student recruitment and employer engagement can amplify positive spillovers. For countries with established elite systems, policies that promote system-wide excellence rather than flagship concentration can mitigate negative spillovers.

The study also contributes to theoretical understanding of how rankings function as institutional signals in global higher education. The heterogeneous effects we document challenge simplistic narratives about rankings effects and highlight the importance of considering organizational field position in understanding institutional dynamics.

Future research should extend our analysis to other ranking systems, investigate the long-term consequences of Top 100 entry, and explore the mechanisms underlying negative spillovers in chronic systems. Such research will further illuminate the complex ways in which global rankings shape the development of higher education systems worldwide.

## DISCUSSION

## Discussion

### Theoretical Contribution: Ranking Spillovers as Event-Driven Phenomena

Our findings make a distinctive theoretical contribution to the higher education literature by demonstrating that ranking spillovers are fundamentally **event-driven** rather than permanent structural features of university systems. The stark contrast between first-time entrants (β = +7.48, p = 0.001 at h = 8) and chronic Top 100 members (β = −8.42, p = 0.0003) reveals that the informational shock of *initial* entry—not the ongoing status of membership—generates measurable peer effects. This temporal asymmetry carries profound implications for how we theorize ranking dynamics.

Qin et al. (2026) conceptualize ranking spillovers as relatively stable phenomena that accrue to universities within the orbit of elite institutions. Our results complicate this picture substantially. If spillovers were structural, we would expect chronic members to generate *stronger* or at least *comparable* positive externalities, given their longer exposure and deeper entrenchment in global reputation networks. Instead, we observe a complete reversal: long-established elite systems exhibit *negative* spillovers to their peers. This suggests that the marginal informational value of ranking entry decays rapidly—and eventually inverts—as systems become saturated with elite status.

We interpret this through the lens of **attention economics** and **status competition**. When a country first achieves Top 100 representation, the event functions as a discontinuous signal that recalibrates global perceptions of the entire national higher education system. International students, employers, and academic collaborators update their beliefs about the quality of *all* universities in that country, generating positive externalities for peers. However, as systems become chronic members of the elite tier, the informational content of membership diminishes. The market has already incorporated this information into its evaluations. Moreover, chronic elite status may intensify within-country competition for finite resources—government funding, top faculty, and international students—as universities strive to maintain or improve their positions relative to the established flagship. This competitive dynamic may explain the negative spillover we observe for chronic systems: the flagship's continued success increasingly comes at the expense of its domestic peers.

This event-driven interpretation aligns with broader sociological theories of status and organizational behavior. DiMaggio and Powell (1983) argue that organizations respond to uncertainty by imitating successful peers—a process that intensifies when status boundaries shift. Our findings suggest that the *moment of boundary crossing* is when mimetic pressures are strongest, as peer institutions scramble to emulate the strategies of the newly successful entrant. Once the new equilibrium is established, these pressures subside and competitive dynamics reassert themselves.

### Mechanism Interpretation: Brand Signaling Through Student and Employer Markets

Our mechanism tests provide the clearest evidence to date on *how* ranking spillovers operate. The exceptionally strong effects on International Student scores (β = +30.49, p < 0.001 at h = 8) and Employer Reputation (β = +13.79, p < 0.01) indicate that the brand signal transmitted by flagship entry operates primarily through **external audience perceptions** rather than internal resource reallocation.

The international student channel deserves particular emphasis. A 30-point increase in the International Student sub-score represents a massive shift in a university's ability to attract globally mobile students. This finding suggests that prospective international students—who face the greatest information asymmetries when evaluating foreign universities—rely heavily on coarse national-level signals. When a country's flagship enters the global Top 100, it effectively certifies the quality of the entire national system in the minds of international applicants. This certification effect is particularly powerful because international students cannot easily verify quality through local networks or domestic reputation systems.

Similarly, the Employer Reputation channel indicates that labor market actors update their assessments of graduate quality based on national-level ranking events. Employers, particularly multinational corporations recruiting across borders, use rankings as heuristics for graduate quality. A first-time Top 100 entry signals that the country's educational system has reached global standards, improving employment prospects for graduates of *all* universities in that country—not just the flagship.

Critically, we find **no evidence** that spillovers operate through faculty mobility or resource concentration. The null results for International Faculty (β = −8.93, p = 0.244 at h = 8) suggest that the positive spillover is not driven by top researchers relocating to peer institutions or by resource flows following the flagship's success. This distinction is theoretically important because it separates **reputational externalities** from **resource-based externalities**. Our findings indicate that ranking spillovers are primarily reputational—they change how external audiences perceive peer institutions—rather than resource-based—they do not involve tangible transfers of human or financial capital.

This mechanism profile has practical implications. Reputational spillovers can be leveraged through marketing and branding strategies, whereas resource-based spillovers would require more substantive investments. For peer universities in first-time entry countries, the window of opportunity opened by the flagship's success is primarily a *perception* window—one that can be exploited through strategic communication, international recruitment campaigns, and employer engagement initiatives.

### Challenging WCU Policy Assumptions

The null average effect we document challenges a central assumption underlying World-Class University (WCU) policies across Asia, the Middle East, and Europe. Governments have invested billions in creating elite universities, implicitly assuming that the benefits would diffuse to the broader higher education system (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2016). Our results suggest that this assumption requires substantial qualification.

The average null effect—across all bandwidths and confirmed by permutation tests—indicates that flagship success does *not* automatically translate into system-wide improvements. For every country that experiences positive spillovers from first-time entry, another chronic elite system exhibits negative spillovers. The net effect across all countries is statistically indistinguishable from zero. This finding should temper expectations that WCU investments will generate broad-based benefits for national higher education systems.

However, our heterogeneity analysis offers a more nuanced policy message. The positive spillovers for first-time entrants suggest that WCU policies *can* generate system-wide benefits, but only under specific conditions. The timing of entry matters: countries that achieve elite status for the first time capture reputational dividends that chronic members no longer enjoy. This implies that the *marginal* value of WCU investment is highest for systems on the cusp of elite status, not for those already entrenched in the top tier.

For chronic elite systems, our findings suggest that policymakers should be alert to the competitive dynamics that may undermine peer institutions. The negative spillover we observe may reflect a zero-sum dynamic in which flagship success concentrates resources and attention at the expense of other domestic universities. This interpretation aligns with Marginson's (2006) warning that global rankings can exacerbate within-country stratification. Policymakers in these systems may need to implement compensatory mechanisms—such as targeted funding for non-flagship institutions or policies that encourage collaboration rather than competition—to mitigate these negative externalities.

### Limitations

Several limitations temper the strength of our conclusions. First, the sample of first-time above-cutoff observations is small (n = 28 at h = 8). While the effects are statistically significant and robust across specifications, the limited number of entry events means our estimates are sensitive to the influence of individual countries. Future research with longer panels or alternative ranking systems could expand this sample and provide more precise estimates.

Second, our cross-sectional design at the university-year level cannot fully account for unobserved time-varying confounders. Although our RDD framework provides strong internal validity by exploiting the discontinuous assignment around the Top 100 threshold, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that other events coinciding with first-time entry—such as national policy reforms or economic shocks—contribute to the observed spillovers.

Third, our findings are specific to the QS World University Rankings. Other ranking systems (e.g., THE, ARWU, US News) employ different methodologies and may generate different spillover patterns. The QS emphasis on reputational surveys may amplify the brand-signaling mechanisms we identify; rankings with greater weight on objective research metrics might exhibit different effects.

Fourth, the mechanism tests rely on QS sub-scores from a single year (2025), limiting our ability to trace dynamic changes in these channels over time. Panel data on sub-scores would allow for more rigorous mediation analysis.

### Future Research Directions

Our findings open several avenues for future investigation. First, panel datasets spanning longer periods with more entry events would allow researchers to trace the *temporal dynamics* of spillovers more precisely. Do the positive effects of first-time entry decay over time? At what point does the positive spillover invert to negative? Answering these questions requires data covering multiple entry cohorts with sufficient post-entry observations.

Second, external outcome data—such as international student enrollment records, graduate employment outcomes, or research collaboration patterns—would complement ranking-based measures and provide more direct evidence of spillover mechanisms. Linking ranking events to administrative data on student flows or labor market outcomes would strengthen causal claims.

Third, comparative analyses across ranking systems could identify which methodological features amplify or dampen spillover effects. If reputational weight drives spillovers, rankings with heavier survey components should exhibit larger effects. Such comparisons would provide valuable insights into how ranking design shapes institutional behavior.

Fourth, qualitative research could illuminate the decision-making processes through which peer universities respond to flagship entry. Do they actively adjust their strategies in response to the event, or are the spillover effects purely perceptual? Understanding the organizational responses to status shocks would enrich our theoretical understanding of ranking dynamics.

Finally, the negative spillovers for chronic elite systems merit dedicated investigation. Are these effects driven by resource competition, attention diversion, or some other mechanism? Identifying the causal pathways underlying negative spillovers would inform policy interventions to mitigate them.

## CONCLUSION

# Ranking Spillovers and the Temporally Bounded Nature of Elite Status: Evidence from the QS World University Rankings

## Abstract

Global university rankings have become central to higher education policy, yet whether a nation's flagship institution entering the world's top 100 generates measurable spillovers to peer universities remains contested. Using a regression discontinuity design applied to eight years of QS World University Rankings data (2017–2026; 3,063 university-year observations across 85 countries), we find no average spillover effect. This null result, however, masks substantial heterogeneity: first-time entry into the top 100 generates large positive spillovers (+7.48 points, p=0.001), while chronic elite status produces significant negative spillovers (−8.42 points, p=0.0003). Mechanism tests reveal that the positive brand signal operates through international student attraction (+30.49, p<0.001) and employer reputation (+13.79, p<0.01), not faculty resource concentration. These findings demonstrate that ranking spillovers are temporally bounded: the signal value of elite status decays with persistence, eventually reversing as resource concentration within flagship institutions outweighs reputational benefits. We conclude that World-Class University policies should concentrate support during the initial entry window and institutionalize redistribution mechanisms before the reversal phase begins.
