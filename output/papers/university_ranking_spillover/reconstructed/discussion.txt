## Discussion

### Theoretical Contribution: The Temporality of Ranking Spillovers

The central finding of this study—that university ranking spillovers are fundamentally temporal phenomena rather than permanent structural effects—challenges a core assumption embedded in much of the higher education literature. The prevailing narrative, largely shaped by world-class university (WCU) policy discourse, implicitly treats the benefits of elite status as durable and cumulative. Our results suggest otherwise: the spillover effect of Top 100 entry on peer institutions is not a stable equilibrium outcome but an event-driven shock that dissipates—and indeed reverses—as elite status becomes institutionalized.

This temporal heterogeneity carries significant theoretical weight. It suggests that the mechanisms underlying spillover effects are not static features of status hierarchies but dynamic processes contingent on the *moment* of status transition. When a country first achieves Top 100 representation, this functions as what we might term a "flagship event shock"—a discrete, highly visible rupture in the established status order that triggers re-evaluation, resource mobilization, and aspirational emulation among peer institutions. The positive spillover of +7.48 points (p=0.001) observed for first-time entrants reflects this shock: the sudden presence of a domestic flagship in the global elite creates new reference points, legitimizes ambitious strategies, and potentially unlocks policy attention and funding that benefits the entire sector.

However, the negative spillover of -8.42 points (p=0.0003) among chronic Top 100 countries reveals a different dynamic. Here, elite status has become institutionalized—part of the taken-for-granted architecture of the national higher education system. In this context, the presence of a Top 100 university no longer functions as an aspirational shock but as an established hierarchy that may concentrate resources, attention, and talent in ways that disadvantage peer institutions. This aligns with institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983): as isomorphic pressures normalize the presence of elite institutions, the mimetic energy that initially drove sector-wide improvement gives way to resource competition and status stratification. The flagship ceases to be a rising tide and becomes, instead, a magnet that draws resources away from peers.

This finding extends Marginson's (2006, 2016) work on status hierarchies in higher education by demonstrating that the *dynamics* of status—not merely its structure—shape institutional outcomes. Marginson's positional goods framework suggests that status competition is zero-sum, but our results indicate that the zero-sum nature intensifies over time. In the early phase of elite emergence, status appears to generate positive externalities; in the mature phase, it generates negative ones. The temporal dimension thus mediates the relationship between hierarchy and spillover in ways that existing theory has not fully captured.

### Contrast with Qin et al. (2026): Classroom versus Institutional Spillovers

The contrast between our findings and those of Qin et al. (2026) on classroom peer effects is particularly instructive. Qin et al. document persistent, positive peer effects in educational settings—students exposed to high-achieving peers continue to benefit over extended periods, suggesting that peer effects operate through durable mechanisms such as skill formation, aspiration shifts, and network development. These effects appear to be *permanent* in the sense that they persist long after the initial exposure.

Our results suggest that institutional spillovers operate through fundamentally different mechanisms. Rather than the deep, skill-based channels that characterize classroom peer effects, institutional spillovers appear to operate through more superficial, signal-based channels. The positive spillover for first-time entrants likely reflects shifts in *perceptions*—of institutional quality, of national higher education capacity, of the feasibility of global competitiveness—rather than changes in underlying institutional capabilities. These perceptual shifts are powerful in the short term because they alter the opportunity structure: policy attention, funding priorities, international partnerships, and student recruitment patterns all respond to the new signal.

However, signals decay. As the presence of a Top 100 university becomes normalized, the informational value of that signal diminishes. Peer institutions that initially responded to the flagship's success with strategic emulation and investment may find that the expected benefits do not materialize, or that the flagship's continued dominance actually constrains their own advancement. The negative spillover among chronic countries suggests that the institutionalized elite equilibrium is characterized by resource concentration rather than diffusion—a pattern consistent with cumulative advantage dynamics but inconsistent with the permanent positive spillover that classroom peer effects exhibit.

This distinction has important implications for theory. It suggests that the *level of analysis* matters critically for understanding spillover dynamics. At the individual level, peer effects may operate through human capital channels that produce lasting benefits. At the institutional level, spillovers operate through status and resource channels that are subject to diminishing returns and potential reversal. The higher education literature has often conflated these levels, implicitly assuming that institutional benefits mirror individual benefits. Our findings caution against such conflation.

### Policy Implications: The Limited Window of Positive Spillover

The most immediate policy implication of our findings is that WCU investment strategies cannot assume permanent system-wide benefits. The positive spillover window for first-time entrants is real but finite. Countries contemplating major investments to achieve Top 100 representation should recognize that the sector-wide benefits are likely to be concentrated in the period immediately following the first successful entry, not distributed evenly over time.

This temporal constraint has several concrete implications. First, policymakers should design WCU initiatives with explicit attention to the *diffusion phase*—the period immediately following flagship success when peer institutions are most receptive to emulation and most likely to benefit from the new status signal. This may require dedicated funding mechanisms, capacity-building programs, and strategic planning support targeted at non-flagship institutions during this window. Without such intentional diffusion strategies, the positive spillover may dissipate without generating lasting sector-wide improvements.

Second, the negative spillover among chronic Top 100 countries suggests that mature elite systems face a different policy challenge: managing the resource concentration effects that accompany institutionalized status. This may require deliberate policies to redistribute resources, foster institutional diversity, and prevent the flagship from becoming a resource magnet that starves peers. The finding that chronic status is associated with negative spillovers of -8.42 points (p=0.0003) is substantial—larger in magnitude than the positive first-time effect—and suggests that the costs of institutionalized hierarchy may outweigh the benefits.

Third, our results complicate the global rankings arms race. If the benefits of Top 100 status are primarily event-driven rather than ongoing, then the relentless pursuit of higher rankings may be misguided. Countries that already have established Top 100 institutions may be better served by focusing on system-wide quality improvement rather than further elevating their flagships, given that the marginal spillover benefits of continued elite status appear to be negative.

However, we must be cautious in translating these findings into policy prescriptions. The cross-sectional nature of our identification, the limited number of first-time entry events, and the reliance on a single ranking system all constrain the generalizability of our conclusions. The policy implications should be treated as hypotheses requiring further investigation rather than definitive guidance.

### Limitations

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the sample of first-time Top 100 countries is small: 41 countries with Top 100 status in ≤30% of sample years, of which only 28 are above the threshold at h=8. This limited sample size reduces statistical power and increases the risk of Type II errors. The positive spillover of +7.48 points, while statistically significant at p=0.001, is estimated with considerable uncertainty given the small number of first-time entry events.

Second, our analysis relies on a single ranking system (the Academic Ranking of World Universities). Rankings differ substantially in their methodologies, coverage, and weighting schemes, and it is unclear whether our findings would replicate with other ranking systems. The ARWU's emphasis on research output and Nobel laureates may generate different spillover dynamics than rankings that incorporate teaching quality, employability, or broader societal impact.

Third, the cross-sectional regression discontinuity design, while appropriate for identifying local treatment effects, has limited power to detect switcher effects. Our analysis captures the immediate impact of crossing the Top 100 threshold but cannot fully account for the dynamic adjustment processes that unfold over multiple years. The heterogeneity analysis by first-time versus chronic status partially addresses this, but the limited number of first-time entry events constrains our ability to trace the temporal evolution of spillover effects in detail.

Fourth, we lack external outcome data beyond the ranking scores themselves. The spillover effects we document are measured in ranking points, which may not translate into meaningful differences in institutional quality, student outcomes, research productivity, or societal impact. Future research should examine whether the spillover effects we observe in rankings correspond to changes in more substantive outcomes.

Fifth, the McCrary test indicates no manipulation at the threshold (8.1% discontinuity), which is reassuring, but the possibility of sorting based on unobservable characteristics cannot be entirely ruled out. The permutation tests confirming the null average effect provide additional confidence in the heterogeneity findings, but these tests do not fully address concerns about unobserved confounding.

### Future Research Directions

The temporal heterogeneity we document opens several avenues for future research. First, longer panels with more first-time entry events are needed to trace the full trajectory of spillover effects—from the initial positive shock through potential decay and reversal. The current sample includes only 41 first-time countries, and many of these entered the Top 100 relatively recently. As more countries achieve Top 100 status, the analytical power to examine temporal dynamics will increase substantially.

Second, future research should incorporate external outcome data beyond ranking scores. Measures of research productivity (publications, citations, patents), educational outcomes (graduation rates, employment outcomes, earnings), and broader societal impacts (innovation, economic growth, social mobility) would help determine whether the ranking spillovers we observe correspond to meaningful changes in institutional performance. This is particularly important given the policy significance of WCU investments.

Third, comparative analysis across ranking systems would help establish the generalizability of our findings. If similar temporal dynamics emerge in the Times Higher Education rankings, QS rankings, or national ranking systems, this would strengthen the case that the phenomenon reflects fundamental features of status hierarchies rather than artifacts of a particular ranking methodology.

Fourth, qualitative research could illuminate the mechanisms underlying the temporal dynamics we document. Case studies of countries that recently achieved first-time Top 100 status could reveal how peer institutions respond to the flagship's success—whether through emulation, resource mobilization, strategic repositioning, or other channels. Similarly, case studies of chronic Top 100 countries could illuminate the resource concentration dynamics that generate negative spillovers.

Finally, the distinction between classroom peer effects (permanent) and institutional spillovers (transient) deserves further theoretical and empirical attention. Understanding why spillover effects persist at the individual level but decay at the institutional level could generate important insights into the nature of status, competition, and resource allocation in higher education systems.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the relationship between elite status and system-wide benefits is more complex and time-dependent than previously recognized. The positive spillovers associated with first-time Top 100 entry are real but transient, giving way to negative spillovers as elite status becomes institutionalized. This temporal heterogeneity has important implications for theory, policy, and future research on the dynamics of status hierarchies in higher education.
