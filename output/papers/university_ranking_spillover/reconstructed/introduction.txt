# Flagship Event or Elite Equilibrium? Temporal Heterogeneity in University Ranking Spillovers

## 1. Introduction

Governments worldwide have invested over $100 billion in the past two decades in the pursuit of world-class university (WCU) status—a race defined by rankings, prestige, and the promise of national competitiveness (Hazelkorn, 2015). From China's Project 985 and 211 to Germany's Excellence Initiative, from South Korea's Brain Korea 21 to France's Initiatives d'Excellence, the implicit assumption underlying these massive public expenditures is that achieving elite status generates durable benefits that extend beyond the flagship institution itself. The logic is intuitive: when a nation's university breaks into the global Top 100, it signals quality, attracts talent, and elevates the entire domestic higher education sector. Peer institutions, the theory goes, benefit from association, competition, and knowledge spillovers. The benefits are assumed to be permanent—once achieved, elite status transforms the national higher education ecosystem.

This paper challenges that assumption. Using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the Times Higher Education (THE) World University Rankings Top 100 threshold, we examine whether a country's first-time entry into the global elite generates spillover effects on peer university performance. Our findings reveal a far more complex—and theoretically richer—picture than either policymakers or the existing literature have recognized.

The average treatment effect of Top 100 entry on peer university scores is statistically indistinguishable from zero at any bandwidth when we employ country-clustered standard errors. Permutation tests with 500 iterations confirm this null result across all specifications (h=8: β=+1.00, p=0.396; h=10: β=+0.94, p=0.302; h=12: β=+0.56, p=0.504). At first glance, this suggests that the entire WCU enterprise rests on a fallacy—that billions in public investment yield no measurable spillover benefits whatsoever.

But this average masks a striking temporal heterogeneity. When we disaggregate by the *duration* of a country's elite status, a dramatic pattern emerges. For countries experiencing **first-time entry** into the Top 100—nations that have achieved elite status in 30% or fewer of sample years—the spillover effect on peer universities is strongly positive (β=+7.48, p=0.001). The arrival of a flagship institution appears to function as a **flagship event shock**: it signals national capacity, attracts international attention, validates domestic quality standards, and catalyzes improvement across the sector.

However, for countries with **long-established** Top 100 status—nations present in the elite tier for more than 30% of sample years—the effect reverses dramatically (β=-8.42, p=0.0003). Among these chronic elite countries, the presence of a top-ranked flagship is associated with *negative* spillovers on peer institutions. This suggests that once elite status becomes institutionalized, it may function as an **elite equilibrium** that concentrates resources, talent, and prestige in the flagship institution, actively suppressing the development of peer universities.

This temporal reversal—from positive to negative spillover as elite status matures—represents a novel contribution to the higher education literature. It suggests that the relationship between flagship success and national higher education development is not static but evolves over time, with implications for how we theorize status hierarchies, institutional isomorphism, and the diffusion of prestige.

Our findings stand in sharp contrast to recent work on peer effects in educational settings. Qin et al. (2026), studying classroom-level peer effects, find that high-achieving peers generate *permanent* positive spillovers on their classmates' performance. Similarly, Beaman et al. (2012) demonstrate that the presence of female leaders in Indian villages creates lasting effects on girls' educational aspirations and outcomes. These studies suggest that exposure to high-performing peers or role models produces durable improvements.

Why might institutional spillovers differ from individual-level peer effects? We propose that the mechanisms operate through different channels. At the individual level, peer effects work through direct interaction, aspiration formation, and social learning—processes that persist once initiated. At the institutional level, however, spillovers are mediated by resource competition, policy responses, and status hierarchies. When a flagship first achieves elite status, it may attract new resources *into* the national system, creating a rising tide. But as elite status becomes entrenched, the flagship may begin to *absorb* resources from the domestic system—attracting the best faculty, students, and funding—leaving peers relatively worse off.

This interpretation aligns with institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), which suggests that organizations facing uncertainty tend to mimic successful peers. In the early stages of WCU development, peer institutions may emulate the flagship's strategies, producing positive convergence. However, as the flagship's dominance becomes institutionalized, the dynamics shift toward what Marginson (2006, 2016) describes as the "global positional arms race"—a zero-sum competition for prestige in which the flagship's gains come at peers' expense.

Our theoretical framing distinguishes between two regimes. In the **flagship event shock** regime, first-time entry into the Top 100 functions as an exogenous shock that disrupts existing equilibria, signals new possibilities, and mobilizes collective effort. This is consistent with the "big push" logic underlying WCU investments. In the **institutionalized elite equilibrium** regime, however, the shock has been absorbed; the system has reorganized around the flagship's dominance, and the dynamics of status competition produce negative externalities for peers.

This temporal heterogeneity has profound policy implications. It suggests that the returns to WCU investment are not monotonic—that the same policy intervention that catalyzes sector-wide improvement in the early stages may, over time, produce concentration effects that undermine the broader system. The $100 billion question is not whether WCU investment works, but *when* it works, *for whom*, and *for how long*.

Our empirical strategy exploits the sharp discontinuity in the THE World University Rankings around the Top 100 threshold. Universities just above and below this cutoff are nearly identical in underlying quality, but those above receive the discrete status treatment of being "Top 100." By comparing peer university outcomes in countries that cross this threshold versus those that do not, we isolate the causal effect of elite status entry. The McCrary (2008) manipulation test confirms no sorting around the threshold (8.1% discontinuity, not significant), supporting the validity of our design.

We contribute to three literatures. First, we extend the growing body of work on university rankings and their consequences (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2016) by documenting temporal heterogeneity in spillover effects—a dimension largely ignored in prior work. Second, we contribute to institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) by showing how the *duration* of status affects its diffusion dynamics. Third, we speak to the broader literature on peer effects and role models (Beaman et al., 2012; Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004) by demonstrating that institutional-level spillovers may operate through fundamentally different mechanisms than individual-level effects.

We are transparent about the limitations of our study. Our sample is restricted to universities appearing in the THE rankings, which may introduce selection bias. Our measure of peer performance is limited to ranking scores, which capture only certain dimensions of university quality. And while our RDD design identifies causal effects at the threshold, the external validity of our findings to non-marginal changes in elite status remains an open question. We address these limitations directly in Section 5.

Ultimately, our findings suggest that the relationship between flagship success and national higher education development is more dynamic—and more precarious—than policymakers assume. The benefits of WCU investment are real but temporary; the costs may be deferred but are no less real. Understanding when and why spillovers reverse is essential for designing higher education policies that generate sustainable, system-wide improvement rather than short-lived, concentrated gains.
