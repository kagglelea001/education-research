**Abstract**

The global university rankings literature has extensively documented the direct consequences of league-table placement for institutional prestige, resource acquisition, and strategic behavior. Yet the *spillover effects* of one nation’s flagship university entering the global elite on its domestic peers remain poorly understood—and the temporal dynamics of such spillovers have been entirely ignored. This study addresses both gaps using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the Top 100 threshold in the Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU) across 2003–2020, with country-clustered standard errors and permutation-based inference. We find no statistically significant average spillover effect at any bandwidth, a null result that persists across 500 random permutations. However, disaggregating by temporal status reveals a striking heterogeneity: first-time entry into the Top 100 generates a strong positive spillover of +7.48 points (p=0.001) on peer university scores, whereas long-established elite status produces a significant negative spillover of −8.42 points (p=0.0003). We interpret these results through the lens of institutional theory and status hierarchies: ranking spillovers function as *flagship event shocks* that initially elevate domestic peers through legitimacy transfer and resource attention, but these benefits fade and ultimately reverse as elite status becomes institutionalized, triggering competitive crowding, resource concentration, and status differentiation. Our findings challenge the static view of ranking effects and reveal that the temporal evolution of prestige signals—not their mere presence—determines their systemic impact.
