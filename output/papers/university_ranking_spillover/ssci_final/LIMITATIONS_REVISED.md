## Limitations

Several limitations warrant careful consideration, which we categorize into three domains: identification strategy, data and measurement, and external validity.

### Limitations in Identification and Empirical Design

First, our primary identification relies on a cross-sectional regression discontinuity design, which estimates the local average treatment effect (LATE) at the threshold. The interpretation of our results is therefore restricted to countries whose top-ranked university lies within close proximity of the Top 100 cutoff. Our findings do not directly generalize to countries where the flagship university substantially exceeds or falls short of this threshold. While we supplement the cross-sectional RDD with panel DID-RDD robustness checks, the number of switcher countries—those whose universities cross the QS Top 100 threshold during our observation window—is limited (16 out of 67), constraining statistical power for dynamic identification.

Second, the validity of our analysis rests on a single ranking system. THE World University Rankings fail the McCrary density test (53.5% discontinuity, indicating systematic sorting near the threshold) and are therefore excluded from causal analysis. ARWU exhibits no variation in treatment status, as virtually all countries in our sample already possess at least one Top 100 university, precluding RDD estimation. Consequently, our conclusions are derived exclusively from QS rankings, whose methodology heavily weights reputational surveys (40% academic reputation, 10% employer reputation). This emphasis on subjective perceptions may amplify signaling effects relative to more research-oriented ranking systems, raising the question of whether our findings are specific to the QS framework rather than generalizable to all global rankings.

Third, the RDD cannot fully eliminate concerns about anticipatory behavior and lagged effects. If governments or institutions foresee a realistic chance of crossing the Top 100 threshold, they may adjust resource allocation and institutional strategies years in advance. While the McCrary test (8.1%) rules out precise manipulation of the running variable, it cannot detect longer-term policy pre-adjustments that may confound the treatment effect.

### Limitations in Data and Measurement

Fourth, all outcome variables are QS sub-scores derived from institutional self-reported data and reputation surveys. These indicators are susceptible to two forms of measurement bias. On the one hand, universities may engage in strategic data reporting or cosmetic adjustments to improve their standing on specific metrics—the well-documented phenomenon of "gaming the rankings." On the other hand, the employer reputation and academic reputation indicators are inherently subjective, reflecting perceived rather than actual institutional quality. Observed improvements in these scores may reflect enhanced national branding rather than genuine improvements in educational or research capacity.

Fifth, the reliance on ranking-derived outcomes makes it difficult to disentangle two competing interpretations of our findings: (a) genuine improvement in the quality and attractiveness of national higher education systems, and (b) merely enhanced subjective reputation driven by the ranking signal itself. External validation using independent administrative data—such as UNESCO international student enrollment statistics or Scopus bibliometric records—would help distinguish between these mechanisms. The divergent citation results (+12.9 at narrow bandwidths, −29 to −39 at wider bandwidths) underscore the importance of triangulating our findings with objective, non-ranking-based outcome measures.

### Limitations in Mechanisms and External Validity

Sixth, our study identifies the presence and direction of spillover effects but cannot quantitatively decompose the relative contributions of distinct transmission channels. The four mechanisms hypothesized in our theoretical framework—government resource reallocation, faculty and talent mobility, international collaboration transfer, and mimetic isomorphism—remain empirically indistinguishable. We observe that spillover effects exist, but we lack the mediating variables required to determine whether resource concentration, signaling, or institutional imitation is the dominant channel. This represents a priority for future research incorporating institutional-level administrative data and text analysis of strategic plans.

Seventh, heterogeneity analyses by institutional type remain underexplored. Due to sample size constraints, we cannot determine whether the Janus-faced pattern of spillovers differs systematically across developed versus developing countries, public versus private universities, or research-intensive versus teaching-focused institutions. The 532 spillover universities identified in our QS 2025 cross-section provide a rich sample for such subgroup analyses, but within-bandwidth cell sizes become insufficient for meaningful statistical inference once further stratifications are imposed.

Finally, the cross-sectional nature of our primary design, combined with the lack of overlapping years between QS (2017–2026) and THE (2011–2016), prevents a multi-system panel analysis that would strengthen causal identification and address dynamic concerns about anticipatory policy adjustments. Future work exploiting natural experiments with overlapping ranking years and larger switcher samples would substantially improve the internal and external validity of these findings.

In summary, our study provides the first causal evidence of ranking spillovers but necessarily confronts important methodological trade-offs: the tension between internal validity (strong identification at the QS threshold) and external validity (generalizability beyond QS, beyond the cutoff region, and beyond self-reported outcomes). These limitations should be addressed in subsequent research as additional data sources and methodological advances become available.
