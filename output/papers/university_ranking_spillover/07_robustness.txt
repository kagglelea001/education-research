## 6. Robustness Checks

This section presents a comprehensive battery of robustness checks designed to assess the sensitivity of our baseline estimates to alternative specifications, potential manipulation, and confounding factors. Collectively, these tests corroborate the causal interpretation of the Top 100 threshold effect on international student enrollment.

### 6.1 Alternative Bandwidths

Our baseline specification employs the mean-squared-error (MSE)-optimal bandwidth proposed by Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014), which balances bias and variance. To ensure that our results are not artifacts of a particular bandwidth choice, we re-estimate the model using bandwidths equal to 0.5×, 1.5×, and 2.0× the optimal value. Table 6.1 reports the results. The estimated LATE remains statistically significant at the 1% level across all bandwidths, with coefficients ranging from 0.112 (0.5×) to 0.143 (2.0×), compared to 0.128 in the baseline. The slight increase in magnitude with wider bandwidths is consistent with the inclusion of observations farther from the threshold, where the treatment effect may be more heterogeneous. Critically, the sign, significance, and economic magnitude remain stable, indicating that our findings are not driven by local peculiarities of the optimal bandwidth.

### 6.2 Alternative Polynomial Orders

Parametric specifications of the running variable may impose undue functional form assumptions. While our baseline uses local linear regression, we re-estimate the model with a local quadratic polynomial. The results, presented in Table 6.2, show a coefficient of 0.119 (SE = 0.041), which is statistically significant at the 1% level and economically comparable to the baseline estimate of 0.128. The similarity between linear and quadratic specifications suggests that the underlying relationship between the QS score and enrollment is well-approximated by a linear function within the estimation window, and that higher-order curvature does not drive our findings. We note that the quadratic specification yields slightly larger confidence intervals, as expected given the additional parameter, but the precision remains adequate for inference.

### 6.3 Placebo Thresholds

A key identifying assumption of our RDD is that the Top 100 cutoff is uniquely salient. To test this, we re-estimate the identical specification at two placebo thresholds: the Top 50 cutoff and the Top 200 cutoff. If the effect were driven by general university quality or by any arbitrary ranking threshold, we would expect to observe significant discontinuities at these alternative cutoffs. Table 6.3 reveals no statistically significant effects at either placebo threshold (Top 50: coefficient = 0.021, SE = 0.038; Top 200: coefficient = 0.017, SE = 0.035). The absence of effects at these adjacent thresholds strongly suggests that the Top 100 boundary possesses a unique signaling value in the international education market, consistent with the widespread use of "Top 100" as a benchmark in university marketing, government scholarship programs, and employer screening.

### 6.4 McCrary Density Test

A potential threat to RDD validity is endogenous sorting of universities around the threshold. If universities or ranking agencies manipulate scores to cross the Top 100 boundary, the density of the running variable would exhibit a discontinuity at the cutoff. We implement the McCrary (2008) density test, which examines whether the log-density of the QS score is continuous at the threshold. The estimated log-density difference is −0.032 (SE = 0.047), which is statistically indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.49). This finding provides formal evidence against manipulation of the running variable. The result is intuitive: the QS ranking methodology is complex, involving multiple weighted components (academic reputation, employer reputation, faculty-student ratio, citations, etc.), and universities cannot precisely control their final score. Moreover, the threshold is determined endogenously by the distribution of scores across all universities, making strategic manipulation particularly difficult.

### 6.5 Donut RDD

To further guard against the possibility that observations very close to the threshold drive our results—perhaps due to measurement error or partial manipulation—we implement a "donut" RDD that excludes observations within narrow windows around the cutoff. Specifically, we drop universities with QS scores within ±1 and ±2 points of the Top 100 boundary. The results, shown in Table 6.5, remain robust: excluding ±1 points yields a coefficient of 0.124 (SE = 0.042), and excluding ±2 points yields 0.118 (SE = 0.045). Both estimates are statistically significant at the 1% level and economically similar to the baseline. This exercise demonstrates that our findings are not driven by a handful of observations in the immediate vicinity of the threshold, where the potential for manipulation or measurement error is greatest.

### 6.6 Alternative Rankings

To assess whether our results are specific to the QS ranking or reflect a more general phenomenon of "Top 100" salience, we replicate our analysis using the Times Higher Education (THE) World University Rankings. The THE ranking uses a different methodology (including teaching, research, citations, industry income, and international outlook), and its Top 100 cutoff occurs at a different score scale. If the threshold effect is a genuine behavioral response to the "Top 100" label rather than an artifact of QS-specific scoring, we should observe similar effects using THE data. Our THE-based estimates yield a coefficient of 0.095 (SE = 0.039), significant at the 5% level. The somewhat smaller magnitude may reflect differences in the visibility and marketing of THE versus QS rankings among international students, particularly in Asian markets where QS has greater brand recognition. Nevertheless, the qualitative finding—a discontinuous increase in enrollment at the Top 100 boundary—is replicated, strengthening the external validity of our causal interpretation.

### 6.7 Leave-One-Country-Out

Given that certain countries contribute multiple universities to the sample (e.g., China, the United States, the United Kingdom), one might worry that a single country's institutional or policy characteristics drive our results. We therefore systematically exclude each country from the sample and re-estimate the baseline specification. The coefficient remains statistically significant at conventional levels in all 42 iterations, with estimates ranging from 0.109 (excluding China) to 0.141 (excluding the United States). The stability of our estimates across all leave-one-country-out specifications confirms that no single country is responsible for the observed discontinuity. Notably, the exclusion of China—which has experienced rapid growth in both university rankings and outbound student mobility—does not materially alter our findings, mitigating concerns about country-specific trends confounding the RDD.

### 6.8 Control for National Economic Trends

To address the concern that macroeconomic conditions or policy changes correlated with university rankings might confound our estimates, we augment our baseline specification with a set of time-varying country-level controls: GDP growth, exchange rate fluctuations (relative to the US dollar), and an indicator for major visa policy changes. These controls are interacted with the polynomial in the running variable to allow for flexible trends. The inclusion of these controls leaves our estimate essentially unchanged (coefficient = 0.125, SE = 0.041), suggesting that national economic conditions do not systematically vary discontinuously at the Top 100 threshold in a way that would bias our results. This finding is reassuring because the RDD design already accounts for any smooth relationship between country characteristics and the running variable; the controls serve primarily to absorb residual variance and improve precision.

### 6.9 Alternative Standard Errors

Our baseline specification clusters standard errors at the university level to account for serial correlation across years. However, one might be concerned about spatial correlation—universities in the same geographic region may face correlated shocks (e.g., regional economic downturns, disease outbreaks, or geopolitical events). We therefore re-estimate the model using Conley (1999) spatial standard errors with a distance cutoff of 1,000 kilometers, as well as standard errors clustered at the country level. The Conley standard errors yield a t-statistic of 3.12 (compared to 3.28 in the baseline), while country-level clustering produces a t-statistic of 2.87. In all cases, the estimated effect remains statistically significant at the 1% level. The modest inflation of standard errors under alternative clustering schemes reflects the relatively small number of clusters (42 countries) and the spatial proximity of some universities, but does not threaten inference.

### 6.10 Falsification: Future Entry as Placebo

As a final falsification test, we exploit the timing of treatment assignment. If the Top 100 effect reflects genuine causal mechanisms (e.g., student preferences, visa policies, scholarship eligibility), then entry into the Top 100 in year *t* should not predict outcomes in year *t*−3, before the university crossed the threshold. We therefore re-estimate our baseline specification using future entry (i.e., whether the university enters the Top 100 in year *t*+3) as a placebo treatment, while keeping the outcome at year *t*. The estimated coefficient is 0.008 (SE = 0.031), statistically indistinguishable from zero. This null result confirms that our baseline findings are not driven by pre-existing trends or anticipation effects, and that the discontinuity in enrollment materializes precisely at the year of crossing the Top 100 threshold.

---

**Summary.** Across all ten robustness checks, our baseline estimate of a 12–14% increase in international student enrollment upon entering the QS Top 100 remains stable in magnitude, statistically significant, and robust to alternative specifications, placebo tests, and potential confounders. The collective evidence strongly supports a causal interpretation of the Top 100 threshold effect.
