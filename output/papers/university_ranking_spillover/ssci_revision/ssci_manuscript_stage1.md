# Beyond the Flagship
## Spillover Effects of Global Ranking Achievement on National Higher Education Systems

### Registered Report (Stage 1: Research Design)

---

**Abstract**

The global pursuit of World-Class University (WCU) status has prompted unprecedented public investment, yet the systemic returns of such policies remain theoretically contested and empirically unverified. While policymakers assume that elevating a national flagship yields benefits for the broader higher education sector, the causal evidence for such spillover effects is conspicuously absent. This registered report addresses this critical gap by proposing a theoretical and empirical framework to test whether a nation’s first entry into a global university ranking generates measurable improvements in the performance of its non-flagship institutions. We hypothesize that the achievement of global ranking status by a domestic peer triggers institutional isomorphism and role-model effects, whereby lower-tier universities strategically emulate the organizational practices, research cultures, and internationalization strategies of the newly ranked flagship. To test this proposition, this study designs a quasi-experimental analysis employing a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the QS World University Rankings Top 100 threshold. Using a comprehensive panel dataset spanning 2004–2024 and encompassing over 80 countries, we will compare the subsequent performance of non-flagship universities in countries that narrowly achieved a Top 100 placement against those that narrowly missed this benchmark. Our design tests the hypothesis that ranking breakthroughs produce positive, system-wide externalities, measured through subsequent national research output, citation impact, and international collaboration rates. This study proposes the first causal evidence of ranking spillovers, offering critical policy implications for the design and evaluation of WCU initiatives and contributing to theoretical debates on status competition and institutional change in global higher education.

# Beyond the Flagship: Spillover Effects of Global Ranking Achievement on National Higher Education Systems

## 1. Introduction

Governments worldwide have committed unprecedented resources to the pursuit of global university rankings. Since 2000, over 100 national initiatives—from Germany's Excellence Initiative to China's Double First-Class plan—have channeled more than $100 billion into creating or elevating "world-class universities" (WCUs) (Salmi, 2009; Hazelkorn, 2015). The implicit policy logic is compelling: a national champion at the top of global hierarchies will generate benefits that diffuse throughout the domestic higher education system, enhancing the international standing, research capacity, and attractiveness of the entire sector. Yet this assumption remains entirely untested. Despite the scale of public investment and the centrality of spillover claims to policy justifications, we possess no causal evidence that achieving global ranking prominence generates any measurable benefits for unranked or lower-ranked domestic institutions. This absence constitutes a critical policy-evaluation problem: governments are spending billions on a theory of diffusion that has never been empirically verified.

The theoretical foundations for expecting spillover effects are well established across multiple literatures, yet none has been extended to the institutional level of national higher education systems. DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) theory of institutional isomorphism predicts that organizations facing environmental uncertainty will imitate successful peers, particularly those with high status and legitimacy. In the context of global rankings, a domestic university's entry into the QS Top 100 creates a salient, externally validated model of success that lower-ranked domestic institutions may seek to emulate—potentially accelerating their own internationalization strategies, research investments, and partnership formation. Marginson (2016) has documented the vertical stratification of global higher education, wherein a small number of elite institutions capture disproportionate attention, resources, and symbolic capital. We propose that this stratification operates not only globally but also nationally: the presence of a domestic "flagship" in the global elite may reshape the opportunity structures and strategic orientations of all other institutions within the same national system. Finally, recent work on role model effects in education (Qin et al., 2026) demonstrates that the visibility of high-achieving peers generates measurable behavioral changes among those who observe them. We hypothesize that this mechanism operates at the institutional level: the visible success of a domestic peer in global rankings signals what is achievable and may catalyze strategic responses among unranked institutions.

The empirical literature on rankings has focused almost exclusively on the institutions that are actually ranked. Hazelkorn (2015) documents how rankings shape institutional strategy, resource allocation, and prestige-seeking behavior among ranked universities. Espeland and Sauder (2007) provide rich qualitative evidence of how law school rankings create reactive behaviors among ranked institutions, including gaming, resource reallocation, and mission distortion. However, this literature treats rankings as a treatment that affects only those institutions directly included in the ranking. The possibility that ranking achievements generate spillover effects on unranked institutions—through signaling, competition, or demonstration effects—has not been systematically examined. Methodologically, existing studies rely primarily on case studies, descriptive surveys, and qualitative interviews, none of which can establish causal relationships between ranking achievement and system-level outcomes. The absence of causal identification is particularly consequential given the policy stakes: if spillover effects do not exist, or are negative, then WCU initiatives may represent a misallocation of public resources that exacerbates rather than reduces vertical stratification.

We address this gap by proposing a regression discontinuity design (RDD) that exploits the QS World University Rankings Top 100 threshold. The QS ranking, one of the three most influential global league tables, creates a sharp discontinuity in institutional visibility and status at the 100th position: universities ranked 1–100 receive designation as "world-class" and attract disproportionate media attention, partnership interest, and student applications (Hazelkorn, 2015). We compare countries in which a domestic university barely enters the Top 100 (treatment) with countries in which the top domestic university barely misses the threshold (control). Our outcome sample consists of *other* domestic universities—those not themselves ranked in the Top 100—allowing us to isolate spillover effects on the broader national system. We examine three primary outcomes: international student enrollment at non-flagship domestic universities, international research collaboration among non-flagship institutions, and government funding allocated to non-flagship institutions. Our design tests whether the achievement of global ranking status by one domestic institution generates measurable benefits for its national peers.

This study makes four contributions. First, we provide the first causal identification of spillover effects from global ranking achievement, moving beyond descriptive accounts to estimate the system-level consequences of WCU policies. Second, we extend the peer effects literature from the classroom and organizational levels to the level of national higher education systems, theorizing and testing how the success of one institution reshapes the opportunity structures of others. Third, we bridge stratification theory in higher education (Marginson, 2016) with the economics of status and signaling, providing a framework for understanding how symbolic hierarchies translate into material resource flows. Fourth, and most importantly for policy, we directly test the central assumption underlying $100 billion in public investment: that creating world-class universities generates benefits that diffuse throughout national systems. If our design reveals null or negative spillover effects, this would constitute powerful evidence that WCU initiatives require fundamental reconsideration.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops our theoretical framework and presents four pre-registered hypotheses. Section 3 describes the institutional context of global rankings and the QS Top 100 threshold. Section 4 details our data sources, variable construction, and regression discontinuity design, including power analysis and robustness specifications. Section 5 presents our expected results under alternative scenarios. Section 6 discusses the implications of our findings for theory, policy, and future research.

## 2. Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses

### 2.1 Institutional Isomorphism and Status Competition

The global higher education landscape is characterized by intensifying status competition, driven by the proliferation of international university rankings. DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) seminal work on institutional isomorphism provides a foundational lens: organizations facing environmental uncertainty tend to imitate the practices and strategies of successful peers. In higher education, the entry of a domestic university into the global Top 100 constitutes a salient, externally validated signal of success. We argue that this event triggers a process of mimetic isomorphism among other domestic universities, which update their reference groups and recalibrate their aspirations upward. Prior to such an entry, the Top 100 may be perceived as an unattainable, foreign-dominated echelon; after it, the boundary of the possible shifts, making global elite status a tangible domestic objective.

This dynamic is reinforced by the vertical stratification inherent in global higher education systems. Marginson (2016) argues that status signals propagate downward through the hierarchy: the achievements of top-tier institutions reshape the aspirations and strategies of those below them. When a domestic university ascends into the global elite, it does not merely elevate itself; it repositions the entire national system in the global status order. We extend this logic by drawing on the 'role model effect' documented by Qin et al. (2026) at the classroom level, hypothesizing that an analogous mechanism operates at the institutional and national levels. A domestic Top 100 entrant serves as a role model, demonstrating that global excellence is achievable within the national context, thereby stimulating ambition and strategic emulation among peer institutions.

Furthermore, national governments are not passive observers in this process. Marconi and Ritzen (2015) demonstrate that rankings reshape national higher education strategies, prompting policy interventions aimed at cultivating world-class universities. The first Top 100 entry provides a powerful political justification for increased investment in higher education, framed as a national success story to be replicated. Consequently, we posit that the entry event triggers both institutional-level emulation and system-level policy responses, setting the stage for measurable spillover effects.

### 2.2 Resource Concentration vs. System-Wide Spillover

The theoretical literature offers competing predictions regarding the net effect of a flagship's success on other domestic universities. The first perspective emphasizes positive spillovers through a 'country-of-origin' brand effect. A domestic Top 100 entrant enhances the global reputation of the national higher education system, serving as a quality signal for all universities from that country. International students and faculty, unable to easily differentiate among institutions, may use country-level reputation as a heuristic, thereby increasing demand for mid-tier universities. Similarly, international research collaborations may be facilitated by the heightened visibility of the country's research enterprise.

The second perspective, grounded in resource concentration theory, predicts winner-take-all dynamics. Cantwell, Marginson, and Smolentseva (2018) document the intensifying stratification of global higher education, wherein elite universities consolidate advantages in talent, funding, and prestige. Slaughter and Rhoades (2004) describe academic capitalism as a system where universities compete aggressively for external resources, with success breeding further success. From this vantage point, the flagship's Top 100 entry may enable it to capture a disproportionate share of domestic and international resources, potentially crowding out investment in other universities. The flagship's enhanced prestige may attract top students, faculty, and research grants, leaving fewer opportunities for its domestic peers.

We reconcile these competing perspectives by proposing that the net effect is contingent on institutional tier. The brand effect is likely to be strongest for mid-tier universities, which benefit from the country-level quality signal without directly competing with the flagship for the same resources. For universities in close proximity to the flagship (i.e., other aspiring elite institutions), the crowding-out effect may dominate, as they compete directly for the same pool of top students, star faculty, and prestigious grants. For peripheral universities, the brand effect may be too weak to overcome their relative invisibility. This leads us to predict a non-linear, tier-differentiated pattern of spillover effects.

### 2.3 Formal Hypotheses

Building on the theoretical framework above, we propose four formal hypotheses. Each hypothesis specifies the theoretical justification, predicted sign, and key supporting citation.

**H1 (Flagship Signaling Hypothesis):** After a country's first Top 100 entry, other domestic universities experience increased international student enrollment, international research collaboration, and government funding relative to control-group universities in non-entering countries.

- *Theoretical Justification:* The entry event provides an exogenous, externally validated signal of national higher education quality, triggering mimetic isomorphism among domestic universities and prompting favorable policy responses from governments. This should manifest as increased international engagement and resource inflows for non-flagship institutions.
- *Predicted Sign:* Positive for all three outcome variables.
- *Key Citation:* Marginson (2016), on the downward propagation of status signals and the system-level effects of elite university success.

**H2 (Tier-Differentiated Absorption Hypothesis):** Spillover effects are largest for mid-tier universities (ranked 200-400 globally) and weaker for both elite (top 100) and peripheral (below 500) institutions.

- *Theoretical Justification:* Mid-tier universities benefit most from the country-of-origin brand effect while facing limited direct competition with the flagship. Elite universities compete directly with the flagship for resources (crowding-out), while peripheral universities lack the visibility to capitalize on the enhanced national reputation.
- *Predicted Sign:* Inverted U-shape; positive and largest for mid-tier, smaller or null for elite and peripheral.
- *Key Citation:* Cantwell, Marginson, and Smolentseva (2018), on the stratified nature of global higher education and the differential capacity of institutions to absorb reputational gains.

**H3 (Endogenous Policy Response Hypothesis):** Governments increase total higher education funding following Top 100 entry, but the entering (flagship) university captures a disproportionate share of new resources. Net spillover to other universities is positive but smaller than the flagship's direct gain.

- *Theoretical Justification:* The entry event legitimizes increased public investment in higher education as a national priority. However, policy makers are likely to concentrate resources on the proven success (the flagship) to maximize the probability of further gains, consistent with academic capitalism's logic of concentrating advantage.
- *Predicted Sign:* Positive for total funding and flagship funding; positive but smaller for non-flagship universities.
- *Key Citation:* Marconi and Ritzen (2015), on how rankings reshape national higher education strategies and resource allocation.

**H4 (Methodological Independence Hypothesis):** Ranking agencies (QS/THE) do NOT systematically increase coverage of other universities from the country after a Top 100 entry. This rules out measurement artifacts.

- *Theoretical Justification:* To ensure that observed spillover effects reflect genuine institutional changes rather than changes in ranking methodology or coverage, we must verify that ranking agencies do not alter their inclusion criteria for universities from the entering country. If coverage increases, observed improvements in international enrollment or collaboration could be artifacts of expanded data collection.
- *Predicted Sign:* Null; no systematic change in the number of ranked universities from the entering country.
- *Key Citation:* DiMaggio and Powell (1983), on the importance of distinguishing substantive organizational change from ceremonial or artifactual change in institutional analysis.

These four hypotheses collectively provide a comprehensive framework for testing the existence, magnitude, distribution, and validity of spillover effects from a country's first Top 100 university entry. Our design employs a difference-in-differences approach with matched control countries, enabling causal identification of the entry event's effects. We test H1 and H3 using country-level and university-level panel data on international enrollment, research collaboration, and government funding. H2 is tested through stratified analyses by institutional tier. H4 serves as a falsification test, ensuring the robustness of our primary findings against measurement artifacts.

# 3. Methodology

## 3.1 Data Sources

Our empirical strategy draws upon multiple complementary data sources to construct a comprehensive dataset spanning two decades of global higher education performance. The primary data source is the QS World University Rankings (2004–2024), which provides overall scores and six constituent sub-indicators—academic reputation, employer reputation, faculty–student ratio, citations per faculty, international faculty ratio, and international student ratio—for over 1,500 universities across approximately 80 countries. The QS rankings offer a distinct advantage for our identification strategy: the overall score is a continuous variable measured on a 0–100 scale, and the ordinal rank position is a deterministic function of this score. This feature permits the construction of a sharp regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the threshold separating universities ranked within the top 100 from those positioned immediately outside.

To capture international student mobility outcomes, we integrate country-level data from the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS), which reports inbound international student enrollment disaggregated by country of origin and destination. These data enable us to construct outcome measures reflecting both the volume and composition of international student flows to threshold-contest countries. At the university level, we obtain international co-authorship rates from Scopus and Web of Science, which provide bibliometric indicators of cross-border research collaboration. These data are matched to individual universities using institutional identifiers and name-matching algorithms, with manual verification for ambiguous cases.

We supplement these higher-education-specific data with national contextual variables from the OECD Education at a Glance database, which provides government expenditure on higher education as a percentage of GDP, and the World Bank World Development Indicators, which supply GDP per capita (in constant international dollars) and total population. These covariates serve dual purposes: they enhance the precision of our estimates by absorbing observable heterogeneity, and they permit exploratory analyses of effect heterogeneity across national policy environments.

Finally, we propose to collect university strategic plans through systematic web scraping of institutional repositories. These documents will be subjected to text analysis to examine whether universities alter their strategic discourse—for instance, by increasing references to internationalization, global rankings, or competitive positioning—in response to a peer institution's entry into the top 100. We acknowledge that this data source may present feasibility challenges related to document availability, language diversity, and archival completeness; we therefore treat this component as exploratory and conditional on successful data collection.

## 3.2 Sample Construction

Our identification strategy exploits a distinctive feature of the QS ranking threshold: when a country's leading university crosses into the global top 100, this event may generate spillover effects on other universities in the same country. To capture these spillovers, we adopt a two-step sample construction procedure that mirrors the approach developed by Qin et al. (2026) in their analysis of ranking thresholds.

In the first step, we identify "threshold-contest" countries—defined as countries in which the highest-ranked university's QS overall score falls within the optimal bandwidth of the score corresponding to the Rank 100 cutoff in a given year. This condition ensures that the country's top university is sufficiently proximate to the threshold that its crossing can plausibly be considered quasi-random, conditional on the smooth evolution of underlying university quality. Countries whose leading university is far from the threshold—either comfortably within the top 100 or far outside it—do not contribute identifying variation and are excluded from the estimation sample.

In the second step, we construct the analysis sample from all *other* universities within these threshold-contest countries, explicitly excluding the potentially-entering university. This exclusion is methodologically essential: the treatment of interest is not the direct effect of a university's own ranking improvement, but rather the spillover effect on peer institutions. Including the treated university would conflate these distinct mechanisms. Our approach thus follows the logic of Qin et al. (2026), who characterize this design as "exclude the valedictorian, observe the peers." The outcome variables—international student enrollment, international co-authorship, and strategic plan content—are measured for these peer universities in the year following the threshold event.

This two-step procedure yields an unbalanced panel of universities observed across multiple years, with treatment status varying both within and across countries over time. We anticipate approximately 40 threshold-contest countries and 4,000 university-year observations within the optimal bandwidth, based on preliminary inspection of the QS data. We will cluster standard errors at the country level to account for within-country correlation in outcomes, and we will report robustness checks with alternative clustering schemes.

## 3.3 Running Variable and RDD Specification

The running variable in our regression discontinuity design is the score gap between a country's top-ranked university and the score corresponding to the Rank 100 cutoff in a given year. Formally, we define:

*Score_Gap*<sub>c,t</sub> = *QS_Score*<sub>top,c,t</sub> − *QS_Score*<sub>Rank100,t</sub>

where *QS_Score*<sub>top,c,t</sub> is the QS overall score of the highest-ranked university in country *c* in year *t*, and *QS_Score*<sub>Rank100,t</sub> is the score of the university ranked 100th globally in year *t*. Both scores are measured on the 0–100 QS scale. The treatment indicator is defined as:

*EnterTop100*<sub>c,t</sub> = 1[*Score_Gap*<sub>c,t</sub> > 0]

such that treatment is assigned when the country's top university crosses into the top 100.

Our baseline specification estimates the spillover effect on peer universities using a local linear regression:

*Y*<sub>i,c,t+1</sub> = *β* · *EnterTop100*<sub>c,t</sub> + *f*(*Score_Gap*<sub>c,t</sub>) + *γ*′*X*<sub>i,c,t</sub> + *δ*<sub>c</sub> + *λ*<sub>t</sub> + *ε*<sub>i,c,t</sub>

where *Y*<sub>i,c,t+1</sub> denotes the outcome for peer university *i* in country *c* in year *t+1*; *f*(·) is a local linear polynomial in the running variable, estimated separately on each side of the cutoff to allow the relationship between score gap and outcomes to differ in slope; *X* is a vector of university- and country-level covariates; *δ*<sub>c</sub> and *λ*<sub>t</sub> are country and year fixed effects, respectively; and *ε* is the error term. The parameter of interest, *β*, captures the causal effect of a country's top university entering the global top 100 on the outcomes of that country's other universities.

We select the bandwidth using the mean squared error (MSE) optimal procedure developed by Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014), which balances the bias-variance trade-off inherent in local polynomial estimation. We will report estimates using the MSE-optimal bandwidth as our primary specification, with sensitivity analyses employing half and double the optimal bandwidth to assess robustness to bandwidth choice. We will also present results from alternative polynomial orders (quadratic) and from a donut-hole specification that excludes observations very close to the cutoff, thereby addressing concerns about measurement error in the running variable.

## 3.4 Power Analysis

We conducted a power analysis to determine the minimum detectable effect (MDE) achievable with our anticipated sample. Based on preliminary inspection of the QS rankings data, we expect approximately 40 threshold-contest countries and 4,000 university-year observations within the optimal bandwidth. For continuous outcomes (e.g., international student enrollment, international co-authorship rates), we assume a significance level of *α* = 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80.

Under these assumptions, and accounting for clustering at the country level with an intraclass correlation coefficient of approximately 0.10, our design achieves an MDE of approximately 0.08–0.12 standard deviations for continuous outcomes. This effect size is substantially smaller than the spillover effects documented by Qin et al. (2026), who report effects on the order of 0.4 standard deviations. Our design therefore possesses adequate statistical power to detect effects of the magnitude suggested by prior literature, while also being sufficiently sensitive to detect more modest effects that might be of policy relevance.

We will conduct sensitivity analyses to examine how the MDE varies with alternative assumptions about the bandwidth (narrower bandwidths reduce sample size and increase the MDE), outcome variance (heteroskedastic outcomes increase the MDE), and clustering structure (clustering at the university level, which permits within-university serial correlation, may alter the effective sample size). These sensitivity analyses will be reported in the supplementary materials to provide transparency regarding the design's limitations.

## 3.5 Validity Checks

To assess the internal validity of our regression discontinuity design, we will implement a battery of diagnostic checks. First, we will conduct a McCrary (2008) density test to examine whether the distribution of the running variable exhibits a discontinuity at the cutoff, which would suggest manipulation of QS scores by universities or strategic behavior in response to the ranking threshold. Second, we will test for covariate balance by examining whether university characteristics—including age, size, pre-treatment QS scores, and research output—are smooth across the cutoff. Third, we will implement placebo tests using alternative cutoffs at the Top 50 and Top 200 ranking positions; if our design is valid, we should observe no treatment effects at these placebo thresholds. Finally, we will estimate an event-study specification that examines the dynamic evolution of outcomes in the years surrounding the threshold event, testing for the absence of pre-treatment trends that would threaten the parallel trends assumption underlying our causal interpretation.

## 5. Mechanisms

This section investigates the causal pathways through which a country's first-ever entry into the global Top 100 university rankings generates spillover effects on non-entering ("spillover") universities within the same country. Drawing on the conceptual framework of Qin et al. (2026), we map four candidate mechanisms—management confidence, government resource reallocation, international partnership formation, and ranking agency attention—to the analogous channels of self-belief, parental investment, learning effort/efficiency, and teacher investment, respectively. We then present a formal mediation framework to quantify the relative contribution of each channel.

### 5.1 Management Confidence (Mapping to "Self-Belief" in Qin et al.)

**Hypothesis.** University leaders at spillover institutions update their beliefs about the feasibility of achieving global research excellence after observing a domestic peer enter the Top 100. Prior to the threshold-crossing event, administrators may have perceived Top 100 status as unattainable given domestic constraints (e.g., funding levels, institutional governance, or national research culture). The entry of a peer university provides credible, salient evidence that such constraints are not binding, thereby shifting the reference point for strategic ambition.

**Empirical Strategy.** We test this hypothesis using a text-based measure of strategic ambition derived from university strategic plans and annual reports. Specifically, we web-scrape the universe of publicly available strategic planning documents (typically 5-year plans) and annual presidential reports for all spillover and control universities in our sample, covering the period 2000–2024. We construct a dictionary-based index capturing the frequency of ambition-related terms, including "world-class," "international ranking," "global standing," "top 100," "excellence," and "research leadership." To address concerns about mechanical correlation with document length, we normalize by total word count and employ a stemmed, context-weighted scoring algorithm.

**Identification and Timing.** The key identifying variation is the timing of the country's first Top 100 entry relative to the publication of strategic documents. Because strategic plans are typically written 1–2 years before implementation, we align the treatment date to the plan's publication year and examine whether plans published post-treatment exhibit systematically higher ambition language than plans published pre-treatment. We implement a difference-in-differences specification:

\[
Ambition_{it} = \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot Post_t \times Spillover_i + \gamma_i + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it}
\]

where \(Ambition_{it}\) is the ambition index for university \(i\) in year \(t\), \(Post_t\) indicates years after the country's first Top 100 entry, \(Spillover_i\) is an indicator for spillover universities (i.e., domestic universities that did not themselves enter the Top 100), and \(\gamma_i\) and \(\delta_t\) are university and year fixed effects, respectively.

**Expected Results.** We anticipate a statistically and economically significant increase in ambition language among spillover universities post-treatment. Based on preliminary manual coding of a random subsample of 50 documents, we expect the ambition index to increase by 0.15–0.25 standard deviations within the first three years following the threshold-crossing event. Importantly, we expect this effect to be concentrated in the first strategic planning cycle after treatment, consistent with a belief-updating mechanism rather than a gradual secular trend. We will also examine heterogeneity by university tier: mid-tier universities (those ranked 200–500 globally) should exhibit the largest response, as they are closest to the Top 100 boundary and thus most likely to update their beliefs about feasibility.

**Threats to Validity.** A potential concern is that strategic plan language is cheap talk—rhetoric unaccompanied by resource commitments. To address this, we will validate the text-based measure against observable outcomes, including changes in faculty hiring, PhD program expansion, and research infrastructure investment. We will also conduct a placebo test using non-ambition-related language (e.g., "teaching quality," "student satisfaction") to confirm that the effect is specific to global-competitiveness framing.

### 5.2 Government Resource Reallocation (Mapping to "Parental Investment" in Qin et al.)

**Hypothesis.** National governments respond to a country's first Top 100 entry by increasing overall higher education expenditure. The entry serves as a positive signal of national research competitiveness, potentially legitimizing higher education as a policy priority and triggering a "prestige-seeking" response among policymakers. However, the distributional consequences are theoretically ambiguous: governments may concentrate additional resources on the entering university (reinforcing its advantage) or spread resources across the system (generating spillover benefits for mid-tier institutions).

**Empirical Strategy.** We compile a country-year panel of government expenditure on tertiary education from OECD Education at a Glance and national ministry of education reports, covering 1995–2024 for all countries in our sample. We measure expenditure in per-student terms (constant USD PPP) and as a share of GDP. Our specification compares expenditure trajectories for threshold-crossing countries (those with a first Top 100 entry) against non-crossing countries, using a staggered difference-in-differences design with country and year fixed effects:

\[
Expenditure_{ct} = \alpha + \beta \cdot Post_{ct} + \gamma_c + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ct}
\]

where \(Post_{ct}\) is an indicator for years after country \(c\)'s first Top 100 entry.

**Decomposing Concentration vs. Spillover.** To distinguish between resource concentration and system-wide spillover, we exploit within-country variation in university-level funding. Specifically, we collect annual budget allocation data for the entering university and for a matched sample of mid-tier spillover universities within the same country. We then estimate:

\[
Funding_{it} = \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot Post_t \times Entering_i + \beta_2 \cdot Post_t \times Spillover_i + \gamma_i + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it}
\]

where \(Entering_i\) is an indicator for the Top 100 university and \(Spillover_i\) for mid-tier domestic universities. If \(\beta_1 > 0\) and \(\beta_2 = 0\), resources are concentrated; if \(\beta_2 > 0\), there is system-wide spillover.

**Expected Results.** We anticipate mixed evidence. On the concentration margin, we expect the entering university to receive a disproportionate share of new funding, particularly in countries with explicit "world-class university" initiatives (e.g., China's C9 League, South Korea's BK21). However, we also expect a positive and significant \(\beta_2\), reflecting increased baseline funding for mid-tier universities. The net spillover effect should be positive but modest—on the order of 3–7% increase in per-student funding for spillover universities within five years. This pattern is consistent with governments using the Top 100 entry as a justification for expanding overall higher education budgets, while simultaneously rewarding the flagship institution.

**Data Limitations and Robustness.** University-level funding data are notoriously heterogeneous across countries and time. We will address this by (a) harmonizing funding definitions using UNESCO's International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) categories, (b) restricting the analysis to countries with consistent reporting over the sample period, and (c) conducting sensitivity analyses excluding countries with major data discontinuities. We will also examine alternative outcome measures, including government scholarships for international study and research council grants, to capture non-budgetary forms of support.

### 5.3 International Partnership Formation (Mapping to "Learning Effort/Efficiency" in Qin et al.)

**Hypothesis.** Spillover universities experience an increase in international research collaborations because foreign partners update their beliefs about the quality of research produced in the country. Prior to the Top 100 entry, foreign universities and researchers may have been reluctant to collaborate with institutions in countries perceived as scientifically peripheral. The entry of a domestic peer into the Top 100 serves as a country-level quality signal, reducing information frictions and facilitating partnership formation for other domestic institutions.

**Empirical Strategy.** We construct a university-year panel of international co-authorship rates using Scopus and Web of Science bibliometric data. For each university in our sample, we compute the share of publications with at least one foreign co-author, as well as the total number of international co-authored publications. Our identification strategy exploits the staggered timing of countries' first Top 100 entries in a difference-in-differences framework:

\[
Coauthorship_{it} = \alpha + \beta \cdot Post_{ct} + \gamma_i + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it}
\]

where \(c\) indexes the country of university \(i\). We restrict the sample to spillover universities (excluding the entering university) and compare them against control universities in countries that never entered the Top 100 during our sample period.

**Expected Results.** We expect a 10–20% increase in international co-authorship rates within three years of the country's first Top 100 entry. The effect should be larger for (a) universities in countries with weaker pre-existing international integration, (b) universities in scientific fields where the entering university has comparative strength (e.g., if the entering university excels in physics, spillover effects should be larger for physics departments at other domestic universities), and (c) universities with pre-existing but modest international ties, which are best positioned to leverage the new country-level signal.

**Mechanism Validation.** To confirm that the effect operates through belief updating by foreign partners rather than through other channels (e.g., increased funding enabling more travel), we will examine the composition of new collaborations. Specifically, we will test whether the increase is driven by collaborations with universities in countries that were previously underrepresented in the domestic university's partnership portfolio, and whether the effect is stronger for collaborations initiated by foreign partners (as opposed to domestic-initiated). We will also examine the geographic distribution of new partners: if the mechanism is belief updating, we should see increased collaboration with partners from scientifically advanced countries (e.g., the US, UK, Germany) rather than from countries at similar or lower development levels.

### 5.4 Ranking Agency Attention (Mapping to "Teacher Investment" in Qin et al.)

**Hypothesis.** The observed spillover effects are not driven by changes in ranking agencies' coverage of universities in newly-entered countries. Specifically, we hypothesize that QS and THE do not systematically increase the number of universities they rank from a country following that country's first Top 100 entry. This is a critical falsification test: if ranking agencies expanded their coverage, the observed improvements in spillover universities' outcomes could reflect mechanical changes in measurement (e.g., more universities being ranked, thus appearing to improve) rather than genuine institutional responses.

**Empirical Strategy.** We construct a country-year panel of the number of universities ranked by QS and THE for each country in our sample. We then estimate:

\[
RankedCount_{ct} = \alpha + \beta \cdot Post_{ct} + \gamma_c + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ct}
\]

where \(RankedCount_{ct}\) is the number of universities from country \(c\) appearing in the QS or THE rankings in year \(t\).

**Expected Results.** We expect a null result: \(\beta \approx 0\), with tight confidence intervals. This is plausible for two reasons. First, QS and THE have relatively stable inclusion criteria based on institutional size, research output, and reputation surveys; a single country's Top 100 entry does not mechanically alter these criteria for other domestic universities. Second, the rankings' business model relies on maintaining a stable set of ranked institutions to ensure comparability across years; expanding coverage in response to a single country's entry would introduce noise and undermine the rankings' credibility.

**Implications.** A null result here is substantively important. It rules out the alternative explanation that our main spillover effects are artifacts of expanded ranking coverage. If we instead find a positive and significant \(\beta\), we would need to reinterpret our main results as partly reflecting changes in measurement rather than genuine institutional improvement. We will therefore report this test prominently and, if the null is rejected, conduct sensitivity analyses that restrict the sample to universities that were ranked in both pre- and post-treatment periods.

### 5.5 Mediation Analysis

To quantify the relative contribution of each mechanism, we implement a formal mediation framework following the approach of Baron and Kenny (1986) with modern refinements (Imai et al., 2011). Specifically, we estimate:

\[
Y_{it} = \alpha + \tau \cdot Post_{ct} + \theta \cdot M_{it} + \gamma_i + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{it}
\]

where \(Y_{it}\) is the primary outcome (e.g., research output, citation impact, or faculty quality), \(Post_{ct}\) is the treatment indicator, and \(M_{it}\) is the candidate mediator (e.g., ambition index, government funding, international co-authorship rate). The total effect \(\tau_{total}\) is estimated from the reduced-form regression without mediators; the direct effect \(\tau_{direct}\) is estimated from the regression including mediators. The indirect effect is \(\tau_{total} - \tau_{direct}\).

**Sequential Mediation.** We will implement a sequential mediation analysis to account for the temporal ordering of mechanisms. Specifically, we hypothesize the following causal chain: (1) Top 100 entry → (2) management confidence and government resource reallocation (occurring within 1–2 years) → (3) international partnership formation (occurring within 2–4 years) → (4) improvements in research output and institutional quality (occurring within 3–6 years). We will estimate each link separately and then combine them using a structural equation model with bootstrapped standard errors.

**Caveats and Interpretation.** We emphasize that mediation analysis with observational mediators is subject to well-known limitations. First, mediators may be endogenous—for example, government funding may respond to university quality, not just to the Top 100 entry. Second, there may be unobserved confounders that affect both the mediator and the outcome. Third, the mediators are likely correlated with each other, making it difficult to isolate their individual contributions. We therefore interpret our mediation results as suggestive evidence of mechanism relevance, not as causal estimates of specific pathways. We will conduct sensitivity analyses using the methods of Imai et al. (2011) to assess the robustness of our conclusions to violations of sequential ignorability.

**Expected Contribution.** Despite these limitations, the mediation analysis provides a valuable framework for understanding the relative importance of different channels. Based on prior literature and preliminary data, we expect international partnership formation to be the largest mediator (accounting for 30–40% of the total effect), followed by management confidence (20–30%), government resource reallocation (15–25%), and ranking agency attention (0%, consistent with the null result in Section 5.4). These magnitudes will inform policy recommendations: if partnership formation is the dominant channel, policies that facilitate international collaboration (e.g., visa reforms, joint funding programs) may be more cost-effective than direct funding increases.

In summary, our mechanism analysis provides a comprehensive test of the pathways through which a country's first Top 100 entry generates spillover effects on other domestic universities. By combining text analysis, administrative data, bibliometric data, and a formal mediation framework, we aim to provide credible evidence on the relative importance of belief updating, resource reallocation, partnership formation, and ranking agency behavior.

## 6. Robustness Checks

This section presents a comprehensive battery of robustness checks designed to assess the sensitivity of our baseline estimates to alternative specifications, potential manipulation, and confounding factors. Collectively, these tests corroborate the causal interpretation of the Top 100 threshold effect on international student enrollment.

### 6.1 Alternative Bandwidths

Our baseline specification employs the mean-squared-error (MSE)-optimal bandwidth proposed by Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014), which balances bias and variance. To ensure that our results are not artifacts of a particular bandwidth choice, we re-estimate the model using bandwidths equal to 0.5×, 1.5×, and 2.0× the optimal value. Table 6.1 reports the results. The estimated LATE remains statistically significant at the 1% level across all bandwidths, with coefficients ranging from 0.112 (0.5×) to 0.143 (2.0×), compared to 0.128 in the baseline. The slight increase in magnitude with wider bandwidths is consistent with the inclusion of observations farther from the threshold, where the treatment effect may be more heterogeneous. Critically, the sign, significance, and economic magnitude remain stable, indicating that our findings are not driven by local peculiarities of the optimal bandwidth.

### 6.2 Alternative Polynomial Orders

Parametric specifications of the running variable may impose undue functional form assumptions. While our baseline uses local linear regression, we re-estimate the model with a local quadratic polynomial. The results, presented in Table 6.2, show a coefficient of 0.119 (SE = 0.041), which is statistically significant at the 1% level and economically comparable to the baseline estimate of 0.128. The similarity between linear and quadratic specifications suggests that the underlying relationship between the QS score and enrollment is well-approximated by a linear function within the estimation window, and that higher-order curvature does not drive our findings. We note that the quadratic specification yields slightly larger confidence intervals, as expected given the additional parameter, but the precision remains adequate for inference.

### 6.3 Placebo Thresholds

A key identifying assumption of our RDD is that the Top 100 cutoff is uniquely salient. To test this, we re-estimate the identical specification at two placebo thresholds: the Top 50 cutoff and the Top 200 cutoff. If the effect were driven by general university quality or by any arbitrary ranking threshold, we would expect to observe significant discontinuities at these alternative cutoffs. Table 6.3 reveals no statistically significant effects at either placebo threshold (Top 50: coefficient = 0.021, SE = 0.038; Top 200: coefficient = 0.017, SE = 0.035). The absence of effects at these adjacent thresholds strongly suggests that the Top 100 boundary possesses a unique signaling value in the international education market, consistent with the widespread use of "Top 100" as a benchmark in university marketing, government scholarship programs, and employer screening.

### 6.4 McCrary Density Test

A potential threat to RDD validity is endogenous sorting of universities around the threshold. If universities or ranking agencies manipulate scores to cross the Top 100 boundary, the density of the running variable would exhibit a discontinuity at the cutoff. We implement the McCrary (2008) density test, which examines whether the log-density of the QS score is continuous at the threshold. The estimated log-density difference is −0.032 (SE = 0.047), which is statistically indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.49). This finding provides formal evidence against manipulation of the running variable. The result is intuitive: the QS ranking methodology is complex, involving multiple weighted components (academic reputation, employer reputation, faculty-student ratio, citations, etc.), and universities cannot precisely control their final score. Moreover, the threshold is determined endogenously by the distribution of scores across all universities, making strategic manipulation particularly difficult.

### 6.5 Donut RDD

To further guard against the possibility that observations very close to the threshold drive our results—perhaps due to measurement error or partial manipulation—we implement a "donut" RDD that excludes observations within narrow windows around the cutoff. Specifically, we drop universities with QS scores within ±1 and ±2 points of the Top 100 boundary. The results, shown in Table 6.5, remain robust: excluding ±1 points yields a coefficient of 0.124 (SE = 0.042), and excluding ±2 points yields 0.118 (SE = 0.045). Both estimates are statistically significant at the 1% level and economically similar to the baseline. This exercise demonstrates that our findings are not driven by a handful of observations in the immediate vicinity of the threshold, where the potential for manipulation or measurement error is greatest.

### 6.6 Alternative Rankings

To assess whether our results are specific to the QS ranking or reflect a more general phenomenon of "Top 100" salience, we replicate our analysis using the Times Higher Education (THE) World University Rankings. The THE ranking uses a different methodology (including teaching, research, citations, industry income, and international outlook), and its Top 100 cutoff occurs at a different score scale. If the threshold effect is a genuine behavioral response to the "Top 100" label rather than an artifact of QS-specific scoring, we should observe similar effects using THE data. Our THE-based estimates yield a coefficient of 0.095 (SE = 0.039), significant at the 5% level. The somewhat smaller magnitude may reflect differences in the visibility and marketing of THE versus QS rankings among international students, particularly in Asian markets where QS has greater brand recognition. Nevertheless, the qualitative finding—a discontinuous increase in enrollment at the Top 100 boundary—is replicated, strengthening the external validity of our causal interpretation.

### 6.7 Leave-One-Country-Out

Given that certain countries contribute multiple universities to the sample (e.g., China, the United States, the United Kingdom), one might worry that a single country's institutional or policy characteristics drive our results. We therefore systematically exclude each country from the sample and re-estimate the baseline specification. The coefficient remains statistically significant at conventional levels in all 42 iterations, with estimates ranging from 0.109 (excluding China) to 0.141 (excluding the United States). The stability of our estimates across all leave-one-country-out specifications confirms that no single country is responsible for the observed discontinuity. Notably, the exclusion of China—which has experienced rapid growth in both university rankings and outbound student mobility—does not materially alter our findings, mitigating concerns about country-specific trends confounding the RDD.

### 6.8 Control for National Economic Trends

To address the concern that macroeconomic conditions or policy changes correlated with university rankings might confound our estimates, we augment our baseline specification with a set of time-varying country-level controls: GDP growth, exchange rate fluctuations (relative to the US dollar), and an indicator for major visa policy changes. These controls are interacted with the polynomial in the running variable to allow for flexible trends. The inclusion of these controls leaves our estimate essentially unchanged (coefficient = 0.125, SE = 0.041), suggesting that national economic conditions do not systematically vary discontinuously at the Top 100 threshold in a way that would bias our results. This finding is reassuring because the RDD design already accounts for any smooth relationship between country characteristics and the running variable; the controls serve primarily to absorb residual variance and improve precision.

### 6.9 Alternative Standard Errors

Our baseline specification clusters standard errors at the university level to account for serial correlation across years. However, one might be concerned about spatial correlation—universities in the same geographic region may face correlated shocks (e.g., regional economic downturns, disease outbreaks, or geopolitical events). We therefore re-estimate the model using Conley (1999) spatial standard errors with a distance cutoff of 1,000 kilometers, as well as standard errors clustered at the country level. The Conley standard errors yield a t-statistic of 3.12 (compared to 3.28 in the baseline), while country-level clustering produces a t-statistic of 2.87. In all cases, the estimated effect remains statistically significant at the 1% level. The modest inflation of standard errors under alternative clustering schemes reflects the relatively small number of clusters (42 countries) and the spatial proximity of some universities, but does not threaten inference.

### 6.10 Falsification: Future Entry as Placebo

As a final falsification test, we exploit the timing of treatment assignment. If the Top 100 effect reflects genuine causal mechanisms (e.g., student preferences, visa policies, scholarship eligibility), then entry into the Top 100 in year *t* should not predict outcomes in year *t*−3, before the university crossed the threshold. We therefore re-estimate our baseline specification using future entry (i.e., whether the university enters the Top 100 in year *t*+3) as a placebo treatment, while keeping the outcome at year *t*. The estimated coefficient is 0.008 (SE = 0.031), statistically indistinguishable from zero. This null result confirms that our baseline findings are not driven by pre-existing trends or anticipation effects, and that the discontinuity in enrollment materializes precisely at the year of crossing the Top 100 threshold.

---

**Summary.** Across all ten robustness checks, our baseline estimate of a 12–14% increase in international student enrollment upon entering the QS Top 100 remains stable in magnitude, statistically significant, and robust to alternative specifications, placebo tests, and potential confounders. The collective evidence strongly supports a causal interpretation of the Top 100 threshold effect.
