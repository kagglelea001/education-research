# Beyond the Flagship: Spillover Effects of Global Ranking Achievement on National Higher Education Systems

## 1. Introduction

Governments worldwide have committed unprecedented resources to the pursuit of global university rankings. Since 2000, over 100 national initiatives—from Germany's Excellence Initiative to China's Double First-Class plan—have channeled more than $100 billion into creating or elevating "world-class universities" (WCUs) (Salmi, 2009; Hazelkorn, 2015). The implicit policy logic is compelling: a national champion at the top of global hierarchies will generate benefits that diffuse throughout the domestic higher education system, enhancing the international standing, research capacity, and attractiveness of the entire sector. Yet this assumption remains entirely untested. Despite the scale of public investment and the centrality of spillover claims to policy justifications, we possess no causal evidence that achieving global ranking prominence generates any measurable benefits for unranked or lower-ranked domestic institutions. This absence constitutes a critical policy-evaluation problem: governments are spending billions on a theory of diffusion that has never been empirically verified.

The theoretical foundations for expecting spillover effects are well established across multiple literatures, yet none has been extended to the institutional level of national higher education systems. DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) theory of institutional isomorphism predicts that organizations facing environmental uncertainty will imitate successful peers, particularly those with high status and legitimacy. In the context of global rankings, a domestic university's entry into the QS Top 100 creates a salient, externally validated model of success that lower-ranked domestic institutions may seek to emulate—potentially accelerating their own internationalization strategies, research investments, and partnership formation. Marginson (2016) has documented the vertical stratification of global higher education, wherein a small number of elite institutions capture disproportionate attention, resources, and symbolic capital. We propose that this stratification operates not only globally but also nationally: the presence of a domestic "flagship" in the global elite may reshape the opportunity structures and strategic orientations of all other institutions within the same national system. Finally, recent work on role model effects in education (Qin et al., 2026) demonstrates that the visibility of high-achieving peers generates measurable behavioral changes among those who observe them. We hypothesize that this mechanism operates at the institutional level: the visible success of a domestic peer in global rankings signals what is achievable and may catalyze strategic responses among unranked institutions.

The empirical literature on rankings has focused almost exclusively on the institutions that are actually ranked. Hazelkorn (2015) documents how rankings shape institutional strategy, resource allocation, and prestige-seeking behavior among ranked universities. Espeland and Sauder (2007) provide rich qualitative evidence of how law school rankings create reactive behaviors among ranked institutions, including gaming, resource reallocation, and mission distortion. However, this literature treats rankings as a treatment that affects only those institutions directly included in the ranking. The possibility that ranking achievements generate spillover effects on unranked institutions—through signaling, competition, or demonstration effects—has not been systematically examined. Methodologically, existing studies rely primarily on case studies, descriptive surveys, and qualitative interviews, none of which can establish causal relationships between ranking achievement and system-level outcomes. The absence of causal identification is particularly consequential given the policy stakes: if spillover effects do not exist, or are negative, then WCU initiatives may represent a misallocation of public resources that exacerbates rather than reduces vertical stratification.

We address this gap by proposing a regression discontinuity design (RDD) that exploits the QS World University Rankings Top 100 threshold. The QS ranking, one of the three most influential global league tables, creates a sharp discontinuity in institutional visibility and status at the 100th position: universities ranked 1–100 receive designation as "world-class" and attract disproportionate media attention, partnership interest, and student applications (Hazelkorn, 2015). We compare countries in which a domestic university barely enters the Top 100 (treatment) with countries in which the top domestic university barely misses the threshold (control). Our outcome sample consists of *other* domestic universities—those not themselves ranked in the Top 100—allowing us to isolate spillover effects on the broader national system. We examine three primary outcomes: international student enrollment at non-flagship domestic universities, international research collaboration among non-flagship institutions, and government funding allocated to non-flagship institutions. Our design tests whether the achievement of global ranking status by one domestic institution generates measurable benefits for its national peers.

This study makes four contributions. First, we provide the first causal identification of spillover effects from global ranking achievement, moving beyond descriptive accounts to estimate the system-level consequences of WCU policies. Second, we extend the peer effects literature from the classroom and organizational levels to the level of national higher education systems, theorizing and testing how the success of one institution reshapes the opportunity structures of others. Third, we bridge stratification theory in higher education (Marginson, 2016) with the economics of status and signaling, providing a framework for understanding how symbolic hierarchies translate into material resource flows. Fourth, and most importantly for policy, we directly test the central assumption underlying $100 billion in public investment: that creating world-class universities generates benefits that diffuse throughout national systems. If our design reveals null or negative spillover effects, this would constitute powerful evidence that WCU initiatives require fundamental reconsideration.
