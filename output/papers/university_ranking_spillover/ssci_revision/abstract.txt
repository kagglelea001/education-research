**Abstract**

The global pursuit of World-Class University (WCU) status has prompted unprecedented public investment, yet the systemic returns of such policies remain theoretically contested and empirically unverified. While policymakers assume that elevating a national flagship yields benefits for the broader higher education sector, the causal evidence for such spillover effects is conspicuously absent. This registered report addresses this critical gap by proposing a theoretical and empirical framework to test whether a nation’s first entry into a global university ranking generates measurable improvements in the performance of its non-flagship institutions. We hypothesize that the achievement of global ranking status by a domestic peer triggers institutional isomorphism and role-model effects, whereby lower-tier universities strategically emulate the organizational practices, research cultures, and internationalization strategies of the newly ranked flagship. To test this proposition, this study designs a quasi-experimental analysis employing a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the QS World University Rankings Top 100 threshold. Using a comprehensive panel dataset spanning 2004–2024 and encompassing over 80 countries, we will compare the subsequent performance of non-flagship universities in countries that narrowly achieved a Top 100 placement against those that narrowly missed this benchmark. Our design tests the hypothesis that ranking breakthroughs produce positive, system-wide externalities, measured through subsequent national research output, citation impact, and international collaboration rates. This study proposes the first causal evidence of ranking spillovers, offering critical policy implications for the design and evaluation of WCU initiatives and contributing to theoretical debates on status competition and institutional change in global higher education.
