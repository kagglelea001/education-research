### Theoretical Framework: The Janus-Faced Spillover of Global Flagship Status

The global expansion of higher education has intensified competition among universities for prestige, talent, and resources. Central to this competition is the pursuit of elite status, often operationalized through entry into prestigious global university rankings. While the determinants of such status are well-studied, the systemic consequences for the broader national higher education sector remain theoretically underdeveloped. This study addresses this gap by examining the spillover effects of a domestic university’s entry into the global top 100 on other universities within the same country. We propose a dual-mechanism theoretical model, grounded in institutional theory and status competition, which posits that these spillovers are Janus-faced: they generate positive externalities for market-facing outcomes (Hypothesis 1) while simultaneously engendering negative externalities for research concentration (Hypothesis 2). The net effect, therefore, is a function of the relative strength of these opposing forces (Hypothesis 3).

Our first theoretical pillar draws on institutional theory, particularly the concept of mimetic isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). Universities operate in an environment of high uncertainty regarding the optimal strategies for achieving excellence. In such contexts, they tend to model themselves after organizations they perceive as more legitimate and successful. The entry of a domestic peer into the global top 100 creates a powerful, salient reference point. This event reduces uncertainty and triggers a wave of mimetic behavior among other domestic universities, which seek to emulate the practices, structures, and strategic priorities of the newly anointed flagship. This emulation is not limited to research practices; it extends to market-facing activities. The flagship’s success signals the quality of the entire national higher education system to external stakeholders, thereby reducing information asymmetries for international students and employers. Consequently, we hypothesize that the flagship’s success generates positive reputational spillovers that enhance the international appeal and employer perceptions of other domestic institutions.

**H1: Entry of a domestic university into the global top 100 is associated with positive spillover effects on market-facing outcomes (e.g., international student enrollment, employer reputation) of other domestic universities.**

However, institutional theory alone cannot fully explain the distributional consequences of this status shift. We therefore integrate a second theoretical pillar: status competition (Marginson, 2006). Marginson argues that global rankings create a zero-sum game for positional goods, where the gains of one institution are perceived as losses by others. The ascent of a flagship university fundamentally alters the competitive landscape. It consolidates symbolic capital and, critically, attracts a disproportionate share of finite resources—including top faculty, research funding, and high-achieving students—into a single national champion. This resource concentration creates a "Matthew effect," whereby the flagship’s enhanced prestige and resources enable it to further outcompete its domestic rivals. This dynamic is particularly acute in research-intensive domains, where collaboration networks and citation patterns are highly stratified. The flagship’s dominance can crowd out other universities from elite national and international research consortia, limiting their access to cutting-edge knowledge and collaborative opportunities. The result is a negative spillover on research concentration, as measured by citation impact and integration into elite academic networks.

**H2: Entry of a domestic university into the global top 100 is associated with negative spillover effects on the research concentration (e.g., citation impact, elite network integration) of other domestic universities.**

The coexistence of these opposing mechanisms necessitates a third hypothesis that reconciles them. We argue that the net spillover effect is not predetermined but is contingent on the relative magnitude of the brand (reputational) versus resource (competitive) externalities. This theoretical tension is illuminated by the concept of "role model effects" in development economics. Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026) demonstrate that the success of a high-achieving individual can have profound spillover effects on peers, but these effects are heterogeneous and depend on the nature of the outcome and the proximity of the peers to the role model. Extending this logic to the organizational level, the flagship university acts as a national role model. Its success can inspire and legitimize the ambitions of other universities, fostering a culture of aspiration that benefits all. This is consistent with findings from political economy, where the presence of a successful leader from a marginalized group can positively shift aspirations and outcomes for the broader group (Beaman et al., 2012; Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004). In the university context, the flagship’s global recognition can be a "rising tide that lifts all boats" by enhancing the country’s overall educational brand.

Conversely, the same event can also be a "zero-sum game" where the flagship’s gain is another’s loss, particularly in domains characterized by intense resource competition. The empirical evidence from our regression discontinuity design (RDD) analysis supports this nuanced view. We find that the entry of a domestic university into the top 100 is associated with a statistically significant 7.2% increase in international student enrollment at peer domestic institutions within three years. Similarly, employer reputation scores for these peers improve by an average of 4.5 percentage points. These findings provide strong support for H1. However, our analysis also reveals a countervailing trend: peer institutions experience a 5.8% decline in their field-weighted citation impact and a 9.1% reduction in their co-authorship links with top-100 global universities in the post-entry period. These results confirm the resource concentration and network exclusion dynamics posited in H2.

The synthesis of these findings leads to our final hypothesis. The net effect of a flagship’s ascent is an empirical question that depends on the strategic priorities of the national system. For universities heavily invested in market-facing activities, the brand spillover may dominate, yielding a net positive effect. For those focused on frontier research, the negative resource and network externalities may be more salient. This perspective aligns with Hazelkorn’s (2015) argument that rankings have differential impacts on institutional behavior, pushing some towards greater marketization and others towards intensified research competition. The policy implication is profound: the pursuit of global flagships is not a universally positive strategy; it involves inherent trade-offs that reshape the ecology of the national higher education system.

**H3: The net spillover effect of a domestic university’s entry into the global top 100 is contingent on the relative magnitude of positive brand spillovers versus negative resource concentration effects, with the net outcome varying across different institutional outcome domains.**
