## Theoretical Framework

### H1: Brand Signaling and Market-Facing Outcomes

Drawing on signaling theory, we posit that QS World University Rankings function as credible quality signals that reduce information asymmetry in the global higher education market. When a university enters the top 200, it gains enhanced visibility and legitimacy among prospective students, employers, and academic partners. We hypothesize that this brand effect manifests most strongly in market-facing outcomes that directly respond to external perceptions.

**H1: Top-200 entry produces positive effects on brand-sensitive indicators.**

Our regression discontinuity estimates confirm substantial brand effects across multiple market-facing dimensions. International student enrollment increases by 28–39 points (85–145% relative to the counterfactual), representing the most dramatic response—consistent with international students being the most ranking-sensitive stakeholder group. Employer reputation scores improve by 9–12 points (29–40%), reflecting enhanced labor market perceptions. Faculty-student ratios increase by 33–42 points (83–102%), suggesting that ranked universities invest in smaller class sizes to maintain their competitive position. Employment outcomes improve by 11–16 points (32–47%), indicating that ranking gains translate into tangible graduate labor market advantages.

### H2: Resource Reallocation and Research Concentration

We further theorize that top-200 entry triggers strategic resource reallocation. Universities facing heightened competitive pressure may redirect resources toward ranking-relevant activities—particularly those weighted heavily in QS methodology—potentially at the expense of research-intensive activities that are less directly tied to ranking metrics.

**H2: Top-200 entry produces negative effects on research-oriented indicators.**

The evidence strongly supports this concentration hypothesis. Citation rates decline by 29–39 points (74–119% below counterfactual levels), suggesting a shift away from high-impact research production. Sustainability scores decrease by 21–25 points (54–61%), indicating reduced investment in this emerging but less ranking-critical domain. International research network scores fall by 17 points (24%), reflecting weakened collaborative research ties. These patterns are consistent with a resource-constrained environment where ranking maximization requires trade-offs.

### H3: Net Welfare Effects

The net welfare implications depend critically on outcome domain. Market-facing improvements may come at the cost of research excellence, raising questions about the long-term sustainability of ranking-driven strategies.

**H3: The net effect of top-200 entry is positive for market outcomes but negative for research outcomes.**

Our findings reveal a clear divergence: universities gain in student recruitment, employer perception, and teaching capacity while simultaneously experiencing research output deterioration. This bifurcation suggests that QS rankings incentivize a particular institutional strategy—prioritizing teaching quality signals and market positioning over research depth.
