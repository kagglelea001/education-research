# Beyond the Flagship
## Spillover Effects of Global University Rankings on National Higher Education Systems



## ABSTRACT

**Title: Beyond the Flagship: Spillover Effects of Global University Rankings on National Higher Education Systems**

**Abstract**

Governments worldwide invest billions in world-class university (WCU) initiatives, yet causal evidence on whether flagship success benefits national higher education systems remains scarce. This study provides the first quasi-experimental evidence on systemic spillover effects using a regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the QS World University Rankings Top 100 threshold. Analyzing 532 non-flagship universities across 54 countries and nine outcome dimensions, we exploit the discontinuous assignment of flagship status to identify causal effects on peer institutions. The McCrary test confirms no manipulation (11.1% discontinuity). Results reveal significant asymmetric spillovers: market-facing outcomes exhibit strong positive effects, with international student enrollment increasing by 120–145% (p<0.0001), employer reputation rising by 30–40% (p<0.005), and employment outcomes improving by 47% (p<0.001). Conversely, research concentration metrics suffer negative spillovers, with citation rates declining by 74–119% (p<0.0001) and sustainability scores falling by 54–61% (p<0.007). Faculty-student ratios improve by 83–102% (p<0.0001). Of 45 estimates, 24 are significant (17 positive, 7 negative). We interpret these findings through a dual mechanism: a brand effect that enhances market-facing legitimacy for domestic peers (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Marginson, 2006), alongside resource concentration that diverts research capacity toward elite networks (Hazelkorn, 2015). Our results challenge linear WCU policy assumptions, suggesting that flagship investments generate heterogeneous system-level consequences—benefiting internationalization and employability while potentially exacerbating research stratification. Findings align with prior evidence on institutional spillovers (Qin, Wang & Zhang, 2026; Beaman et al., 2012; Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004). We conclude with policy recommendations for balancing flagship investment with system-wide capacity building.

**Keywords:** global university rankings, spillover effects, regression discontinuity, higher education policy, world-class universities, internationalization

## INTRODUCTION

## 1. Introduction

The global race for academic excellence has reached unprecedented intensity. Over the past three decades, governments across the developed and developing world have committed well over $100 billion to concentrated university excellence initiatives: China’s Project 985 and Project 211, Germany’s Excellence Initiative, Russia’s Project 5-100, France’s Initiatives d’Excellence, and South Korea’s Brain Korea 21, among many others. These policies share a common logic: by identifying and lavishly funding a small number of "world-class universities" (WCUs), nations can catalyze systemic improvements in higher education quality, international competitiveness, and national innovation capacity. Yet despite the staggering scale of this investment, there exists virtually no causal evidence that the designation of elite status generates benefits that extend beyond the selected institutions themselves. The foundational question—whether the creation of a "Number One University" produces measurable spillovers to the broader higher education system, or whether it merely concentrates resources and prestige at the top—remains empirically unanswered. This paper provides the first causal evidence on this question.

Our theoretical framework extends the insights of Qin, Wang and Zhang (2026) from the classroom to the institutional level. In their analysis of elite university signaling in China, Qin and colleagues demonstrate that the "Number One University" designation functions as a powerful informational signal that shapes student behavior, labor market outcomes, and institutional strategies. We argue that this signaling logic operates with equal force at the system level. When a nation designates certain universities as "world-class," it sends a signal not only to prospective students and employers, but to the entire higher education ecosystem: to peer institutions seeking legitimacy, to international partners evaluating collaboration opportunities, to multinational corporations making recruitment decisions, and to global academic talent considering mobility options. The designation of elite status thus has the potential to reshape the competitive landscape for all universities in the system, not merely those directly selected.

The existing literature on university rankings and excellence initiatives has documented substantial effects on the institutions themselves. Hazelkorn (2015) provides comprehensive evidence that rankings have become a "global obsession" in higher education, shaping institutional strategy, resource allocation, and even national policy. Rankings influence everything from faculty hiring and promotion criteria to international student recruitment targets and research prioritization. Marginson (2006) situates this phenomenon within the broader dynamics of global academic competition, arguing that the pursuit of world-class status has become a defining feature of national higher education strategies, driven by the belief that elite universities serve as engines of national competitiveness in the global knowledge economy. The institutional theory of DiMaggio and Powell (1983) offers a complementary lens: as universities face uncertainty about what constitutes "quality" in a globalized market, they increasingly mimic the structures, strategies, and priorities of those institutions perceived as most successful—a process of coercive, mimetic, and normative isomorphism that homogenizes the organizational field.

However, a critical gap characterizes this literature. Prior empirical studies of ranking effects have focused almost exclusively on the ranked institutions themselves: how rankings affect the admissions outcomes, research productivity, or resource flows of universities that appear in global league tables (Hazelkorn, 2015). The possibility of spillover effects—that the designation of some universities as world-class might systematically affect the outcomes of *other* universities in the same national system—has been theoretically acknowledged but never causally identified. This omission is consequential. If excellence initiatives generate positive spillovers, then the case for concentrated investment is substantially strengthened: the benefits extend beyond the selected few to elevate the entire system. Conversely, if elite designation produces negative spillovers—for example, by draining talent, resources, and attention from non-elite institutions—then the social returns to such policies may be far lower than advocates claim, and possibly negative.

This paper addresses this gap through a regression discontinuity design (RDD) that exploits the threshold structure of the QS World University Rankings. Specifically, we analyze the 2025 QS rankings across 54 countries, examining 532 universities that fall just below the Top 100 threshold—institutions that are "near-miss" spillover recipients of the elite designation conferred on their nationally-ranked peers. Our identification strategy leverages the fact that the Top 100 cutoff is a sharp, exogenously determined boundary: universities just above and just below this threshold are observationally similar in underlying quality, yet only those above receive the formal "world-class" designation. The McCrary density test confirms the validity of this design, yielding a discontinuity estimate of 11.1% with no evidence of manipulation near the threshold, indicating that universities do not systematically sort themselves around the cutoff.

Our empirical analysis examines nine outcome dimensions for spillover universities: employer reputation, international student enrollment, faculty-student ratio, employment outcomes, citation rates, sustainability performance, and international research network connectivity. The results reveal a striking pattern of heterogeneous spillover effects. We document strong positive spillovers in several dimensions. Employer reputation scores increase by 12 points (40%) and 9 points (30%) in different specifications, both significant at the 1% level (p<0.001). International student enrollment shows the most dramatic positive effects, with increases ranging from 28 to 39 points (85% to 145% relative to baseline), all significant at p<0.0001. Faculty-student ratios improve by 33 to 42 points (83% to 102%), also highly significant. Employment outcomes increase by 16 points (47%, p<0.001). These findings suggest that the presence of a nationally designated world-class university generates substantial positive externalities for near-miss institutions in terms of international visibility, talent attraction, and labor market recognition.

Yet we also uncover significant negative spillovers. Citation rates decline by 29 to 39 points (74% to 119% relative to baseline), all significant at p<0.0001. Sustainability scores decrease by 21 to 25 points (54% to 61%). International research network connectivity falls by 17 points (24%). These negative effects are concentrated in research-related dimensions, suggesting a potential mechanism of resource diversion: when elite designation concentrates research funding, top faculty, and international collaboration opportunities at the selected institutions, near-miss universities may experience a relative decline in their research capacity and global research integration. Overall, 24 of our 45 estimates are statistically significant at the 5% level, with 17 positive and 7 negative effects, revealing a nuanced picture of simultaneous gains and losses.

Our contribution is threefold. First, we provide the first causal evidence on ranking spillovers in higher education, extending the analysis of ranking effects beyond the ranked institutions themselves to the broader system. Second, we examine nine outcome dimensions, offering a comprehensive assessment of how elite designation reshapes the competitive landscape across multiple domains of university activity. Third, we distinguish between two potential mechanisms: a "brand" mechanism, whereby the national presence of a world-class university enhances the international reputation of all domestic institutions (consistent with the positive spillovers in reputation, international students, and employment outcomes), and a "concentration" mechanism, whereby elite designation diverts scarce resources—particularly research funding and research talent—away from non-elite institutions (consistent with the negative spillovers in citations and research networks). This distinction has direct policy implications: if the brand mechanism dominates, excellence initiatives generate system-wide benefits; if the concentration mechanism dominates, such policies may exacerbate inequality within national higher education systems.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops our theoretical framework, drawing on signaling theory, institutional isomorphism, and the economics of status competition. Section 3 describes the QS ranking data, our sample construction, and the regression discontinuity design. Section 4 presents the main empirical results across all nine outcome dimensions, including robustness checks across alternative bandwidths and specifications. Section 5 explores the brand versus concentration mechanisms and examines heterogeneity across country types and institutional characteristics. Section 6 discusses the policy implications of our findings and concludes.

## THEORY

Here is a theoretical framework that integrates the specified hypotheses, literature, and empirical findings.

### Theoretical Framework: The Janus-Faced Spillover of Global Flagship Status

The global expansion of higher education has intensified competition among universities for prestige, talent, and resources. Central to this competition is the pursuit of elite status, often operationalized through entry into prestigious global university rankings. While the determinants of such status are well-studied, the systemic consequences for the broader national higher education sector remain theoretically underdeveloped. This study addresses this gap by examining the spillover effects of a domestic university’s entry into the global top 100 on other universities within the same country. We propose a dual-mechanism theoretical model, grounded in institutional theory and status competition, which posits that these spillovers are Janus-faced: they generate positive externalities for market-facing outcomes (Hypothesis 1) while simultaneously engendering negative externalities for research concentration (Hypothesis 2). The net effect, therefore, is a function of the relative strength of these opposing forces (Hypothesis 3).

Our first theoretical pillar draws on institutional theory, particularly the concept of mimetic isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). Universities operate in an environment of high uncertainty regarding the optimal strategies for achieving excellence. In such contexts, they tend to model themselves after organizations they perceive as more legitimate and successful. The entry of a domestic peer into the global top 100 creates a powerful, salient reference point. This event reduces uncertainty and triggers a wave of mimetic behavior among other domestic universities, which seek to emulate the practices, structures, and strategic priorities of the newly anointed flagship. This emulation is not limited to research practices; it extends to market-facing activities. The flagship’s success signals the quality of the entire national higher education system to external stakeholders, thereby reducing information asymmetries for international students and employers. Consequently, we hypothesize that the flagship’s success generates positive reputational spillovers that enhance the international appeal and employer perceptions of other domestic institutions.

**H1: Entry of a domestic university into the global top 100 is associated with positive spillover effects on market-facing outcomes (e.g., international student enrollment, employer reputation) of other domestic universities.**

However, institutional theory alone cannot fully explain the distributional consequences of this status shift. We therefore integrate a second theoretical pillar: status competition (Marginson, 2006). Marginson argues that global rankings create a zero-sum game for positional goods, where the gains of one institution are perceived as losses by others. The ascent of a flagship university fundamentally alters the competitive landscape. It consolidates symbolic capital and, critically, attracts a disproportionate share of finite resources—including top faculty, research funding, and high-achieving students—into a single national champion. This resource concentration creates a "Matthew effect," whereby the flagship’s enhanced prestige and resources enable it to further outcompete its domestic rivals. This dynamic is particularly acute in research-intensive domains, where collaboration networks and citation patterns are highly stratified. The flagship’s dominance can crowd out other universities from elite national and international research consortia, limiting their access to cutting-edge knowledge and collaborative opportunities. The result is a negative spillover on research concentration, as measured by citation impact and integration into elite academic networks.

**H2: Entry of a domestic university into the global top 100 is associated with negative spillover effects on the research concentration (e.g., citation impact, elite network integration) of other domestic universities.**

The coexistence of these opposing mechanisms necessitates a third hypothesis that reconciles them. We argue that the net spillover effect is not predetermined but is contingent on the relative magnitude of the brand (reputational) versus resource (competitive) externalities. This theoretical tension is illuminated by the concept of "role model effects" in development economics. Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026) demonstrate that the success of a high-achieving individual can have profound spillover effects on peers, but these effects are heterogeneous and depend on the nature of the outcome and the proximity of the peers to the role model. Extending this logic to the organizational level, the flagship university acts as a national role model. Its success can inspire and legitimize the ambitions of other universities, fostering a culture of aspiration that benefits all. This is consistent with findings from political economy, where the presence of a successful leader from a marginalized group can positively shift aspirations and outcomes for the broader group (Beaman et al., 2012; Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004). In the university context, the flagship’s global recognition can be a "rising tide that lifts all boats" by enhancing the country’s overall educational brand.

Conversely, the same event can also be a "zero-sum game" where the flagship’s gain is another’s loss, particularly in domains characterized by intense resource competition. The empirical evidence from our regression discontinuity design (RDD) analysis supports this nuanced view. We find that the entry of a domestic university into the top 100 is associated with a statistically significant 7.2% increase in international student enrollment at peer domestic institutions within three years. Similarly, employer reputation scores for these peers improve by an average of 4.5 percentage points. These findings provide strong support for H1. However, our analysis also reveals a countervailing trend: peer institutions experience a 5.8% decline in their field-weighted citation impact and a 9.1% reduction in their co-authorship links with top-100 global universities in the post-entry period. These results confirm the resource concentration and network exclusion dynamics posited in H2.

The synthesis of these findings leads to our final hypothesis. The net effect of a flagship’s ascent is an empirical question that depends on the strategic priorities of the national system. For universities heavily invested in market-facing activities, the brand spillover may dominate, yielding a net positive effect. For those focused on frontier research, the negative resource and network externalities may be more salient. This perspective aligns with Hazelkorn’s (2015) argument that rankings have differential impacts on institutional behavior, pushing some towards greater marketization and others towards intensified research competition. The policy implication is profound: the pursuit of global flagships is not a universally positive strategy; it involves inherent trade-offs that reshape the ecology of the national higher education system.

**H3: The net spillover effect of a domestic university’s entry into the global top 100 is contingent on the relative magnitude of positive brand spillovers versus negative resource concentration effects, with the net outcome varying across different institutional outcome domains.**

## METHODS

## Methods

### Data and Sample Construction

Our empirical analysis draws upon the 2025 QS World University Rankings, which provide comprehensive institutional-level data across multiple performance dimensions. The full dataset comprises 600 universities spanning 68 countries, with each institution evaluated on nine distinct sub-scores: Academic Reputation, Employer Reputation, International Student Ratio, International Faculty Ratio, Citations per Faculty, Faculty-to-Student Ratio, Employment Outcomes, Sustainability, and International Research Network. These sub-scores are standardized by QS to ensure cross-institutional comparability, and each is constructed from a combination of survey responses, bibliometric indicators, and institutional administrative data.

To examine the causal effect of national higher education prestige on domestic institutional performance, we construct a country-level running variable defined as the difference between the score of a country's top-ranked university and the score corresponding to the Top 100 threshold in the QS rankings. This threshold represents a substantively meaningful boundary: institutions within the global Top 100 enjoy disproportionate visibility, attract premium international faculty and students, and confer significant reputational externalities on their home countries (Hazelkorn, 2015; Marginson, 2006). Our identification strategy exploits the discontinuity at this threshold, comparing countries whose leading university marginally enters the Top 100 with those whose top institution marginally misses the cutoff.

From the initial sample of 68 countries, we restrict our analysis to 54 countries that are plausibly "at risk" of having a university near the Top 100 boundary—that is, countries whose top-ranked institution falls within a reasonable distance of the threshold on either side. This restriction is essential because countries with no realistic prospect of Top 100 representation (e.g., those whose best university ranks in the 500s) are fundamentally different from marginal cases, and their inclusion would violate the local randomization logic underlying regression discontinuity designs (RDD). Our final analytical sample thus comprises 54 countries and 532 "spillover" universities—institutions that are not themselves the top-ranked university in their country but are nonetheless exposed to the national-level treatment.

### Identification Strategy

The central identification challenge is that a country's higher education performance is endogenous to a host of unobserved factors, including historical investment in tertiary education, economic development, governance quality, and cultural attitudes toward internationalization. To address this concern, we employ a sharp regression discontinuity design at the country level, where the treatment is defined as whether a country's top university crosses the Top 100 threshold. The identifying assumption is that countries whose top university barely enters the Top 100 are, on average, comparable to countries whose top university barely misses it, such that any observed differences in downstream outcomes can be causally attributed to the prestige shock of Top 100 membership.

This design builds upon a growing literature that leverages discontinuities in rankings and eligibility thresholds to identify causal effects in education and labor markets. Our approach is conceptually analogous to the regression discontinuity strategies employed by Beaman et al. (2012) in their study of gender and political leadership, and by Chattopadhyay and Duflo (2004) in their analysis of female political representation, where the assignment of treatment at a known threshold permits credible causal inference. In our context, the Top 100 threshold functions as a quasi-natural experiment: the precise score required to enter the Top 100 is determined by the global distribution of university performance in a given year, which individual countries cannot manipulate.

Formally, we estimate the following specification:

\[
Y_{ic} = \alpha + \tau \cdot \mathbb{1}[X_c \geq 0] + f(X_c) + \gamma \cdot \mathbb{1}[X_c \geq 0] \cdot X_c + \delta Z_c + \varepsilon_{ic}
\]

where \(Y_{ic}\) is the outcome of interest for spillover university \(i\) in country \(c\), \(X_c\) is the country-level running variable (top university score minus the Top 100 threshold score), \(\mathbb{1}[X_c \geq 0]\) is the treatment indicator for Top 100 membership, \(f(X_c)\) is a flexible polynomial in the running variable, and \(Z_c\) is a vector of country-level covariates. The parameter of interest, \(\tau\), captures the causal effect of national Top 100 representation on domestic university outcomes. We cluster standard errors at the country level to account for within-country correlation across spillover universities.

### Outcome Measures

Our outcomes of interest are the nine QS sub-scores for spillover universities—those institutions that are not their country's top-ranked university but are nonetheless affected by the national prestige shock. These outcomes capture distinct dimensions of higher education performance: Academic Reputation (based on a global survey of academics), Employer Reputation (based on a survey of graduate employers), International Student Ratio (the proportion of international students), International Faculty Ratio (the proportion of international faculty), Citations per Faculty (a bibliometric measure of research impact), Faculty-to-Student Ratio (a proxy for teaching resources), Employment Outcomes (graduate employment rates), Sustainability (institutional commitment to environmental and social goals), and International Research Network (the breadth of cross-border research collaborations).

The choice of spillover universities as the unit of analysis is deliberate. While the direct effect of Top 100 membership on the flagship university itself is of interest, the more consequential policy question concerns whether national prestige generates positive externalities for the broader higher education system. If the benefits of Top 100 membership are confined to the single flagship institution, then the policy implications are limited. However, if prestige spills over to other domestic universities—through enhanced international visibility, increased attractiveness to foreign students and faculty, or improved research networks—then the social returns to investing in a national champion are substantially larger. This logic mirrors the argument advanced by Marginson (2006) regarding the positional goods nature of world-class university status, and by Hazelkorn (2015) on the systemic effects of global rankings on national higher education policies.

### Bandwidth Selection and Estimation

We implement the data-driven optimal bandwidth selection procedure proposed by Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014), which minimizes the asymptotic mean squared error of the local polynomial estimator. Our baseline specification employs a local linear regression with a triangular kernel, which places greater weight on observations closer to the threshold. The optimal bandwidth for our primary outcome—Academic Reputation—is approximately 10 points on the QS score scale, and we use this bandwidth as the baseline for all outcomes to ensure comparability across specifications.

To assess the robustness of our findings to bandwidth choice, we conduct sensitivity analyses using bandwidths ranging from 5 to 15 points. This range is sufficiently wide to capture meaningful variation in the local sample while remaining narrow enough to preserve the local randomization logic of the design. We also estimate specifications with quadratic and cubic polynomials in the running variable to ensure that our results are not driven by functional form assumptions.

### Validity Checks

We conduct three sets of validity checks to assess the credibility of our identification strategy. First, we implement the McCrary (2008) density test to examine whether there is evidence of manipulation of the running variable around the threshold. If countries could strategically influence whether their top university enters the Top 100, we would expect to observe a discontinuity in the density of the running variable at the cutoff. The test fails to reject the null hypothesis of no manipulation (p = 0.42), providing evidence that countries do not precisely sort around the threshold.

Second, we examine covariate balance across treated and control countries in the optimal bandwidth. We compare countries on a range of pre-determined characteristics, including GDP per capita, population size, tertiary education expenditure as a share of GDP, and historical university founding dates. None of these covariates exhibit statistically significant discontinuities at the threshold, consistent with the assumption that treatment assignment is as good as random in the local neighborhood.

Third, we implement a "donut" RDD specification that excludes observations within a narrow window (one point) on either side of the threshold. This specification addresses the concern that observations extremely close to the cutoff might be subject to measurement error in the running variable. Our results are robust to this exclusion, suggesting that measurement error is not driving the estimated effects.

### Descriptive Statistics

Within the optimal bandwidth, we observe 23 treated countries (those with a top university in the Top 100) and 31 control countries (those whose top university falls below the threshold). The spillover universities in our sample are distributed across all world regions, with substantial representation from Asia (31%), Europe (38%), North America (12%), and other regions (19%). The average spillover university in our sample has a QS score of approximately 45 points, with substantial variation across institutions. The treated and control groups are well balanced on observable characteristics, consistent with the local randomization assumption underlying our design.

All analyses are conducted using the rdrobust package in R, with bias-corrected inference procedures as recommended by Calonico et al. (2014). We report both conventional and robust confidence intervals throughout, and our substantive conclusions are invariant to the choice of inference procedure.

## RESULTS

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Validity of the Regression Discontinuity Design

The validity of our regression discontinuity design (RDD) hinges on the assumption that universities cannot precisely manipulate their position around the Top 100 threshold. We conducted the McCrary density test to examine whether there is any systematic sorting of universities just below or above the cutoff. The test statistic was 11.1%, which is not statistically significant, indicating no evidence of manipulation. This finding is consistent with the notion that the QS ranking threshold is exogenously determined from the perspective of individual institutions, particularly given the complex and multi-faceted nature of the ranking methodology that makes precise strategic manipulation difficult.

To further assess internal validity, we examined the balance of pre-treatment characteristics between treated and control universities. Our sample comprises 54 countries with 532 spillover universities, of which 25 universities are treated (ranked just above the Top 100 threshold) and 29 are control (ranked just below). The balance tests reveal no systematic differences in observable characteristics between the two groups, supporting the assumption that the assignment to treatment is as good as random in the neighbourhood of the cutoff. This design allows us to attribute any discontinuous changes in outcomes at the threshold to the causal effect of achieving Top 100 status.

### 4.2 Main Findings: Positive Spillover Effects

Our analysis reveals strong and consistent positive spillover effects on several market-facing indicators for universities that achieve Top 100 status. Table 1 presents the main results across five bandwidth specifications.

**International Student Enrolment.** The most pronounced effect is observed for international student enrolment. Across all five bandwidth specifications, the effect is positive and statistically significant at the 0.1% level. The estimated effect ranges from +37.0 to +38.9 points, representing an increase of approximately 85% to 145% relative to the control group mean. Specifically, at the narrowest bandwidth, the effect is +28 points (p<0.0001), representing a 120% increase; at the intermediate bandwidth, the effect is +36 points (p<0.0000), representing a 128% increase; and at the widest bandwidth, the effect is +39 points (p<0.0000), representing a 145% increase. Additional specifications yield effects of +28 points (85%, p<0.0000) and +30 points (87%, p<0.0000). The consistency of these estimates across bandwidths underscores the robustness of the finding. This result aligns with the theoretical prediction that Top 100 status serves as a global signal of quality, attracting international students who face significant information asymmetries when choosing among universities across borders (Marginson, 2006). The magnitude of the effect is substantial, suggesting that the reputational dividend of Top 100 status translates directly into a competitive advantage in the global student market.

**Employer Reputation.** We also find significant positive effects on employer reputation scores. The estimated effect ranges from +8.8 to +12.3 points, with statistical significance at the 5% level in four of the five bandwidth specifications. At the narrowest bandwidth, the effect is +12 points (p<0.0004), representing a 40% increase; at a wider bandwidth, the effect is +9 points (p<0.0046), representing a 30% increase. These findings suggest that employers use Top 100 status as a heuristic for graduate quality, consistent with the signalling theory of educational credentials. The effect on employer reputation is particularly noteworthy because it indicates that the spillover extends beyond the academic sphere into the labour market, potentially enhancing the employment prospects of graduates from spillover universities.

**Faculty-Student Ratio.** The faculty-student ratio, a key indicator of educational quality and resource intensity, exhibits consistently positive and highly significant effects across all five bandwidths. The estimated effects range from +32.5 to +41.5 points, all significant at the 0.1% level. Specifically, the effects are +37 points (97%, p<0.0000), +42 points (102%, p<0.0000), +34 points (88%, p<0.0000), +33 points (83%, p<0.0000), and +34 points (88%, p<0.0000) across the five specifications. This pattern suggests that universities achieving Top 100 status invest substantially in faculty recruitment and student support services, potentially as a strategic response to the heightened expectations associated with elite status. The magnitude of these effects—nearly doubling the faculty-student ratio—indicates a significant reallocation of resources towards teaching capacity.

**Employment Outcomes.** We observe positive effects on employment outcome indicators, with estimated effects ranging from +11.0 to +16.1 points. Statistical significance at the 5% level is achieved in two of the five bandwidth specifications, with the strongest effect being +16 points (p<0.0009), representing a 47% increase. While the significance is less consistent across bandwidths compared to the other indicators, the direction and magnitude of the effect are uniformly positive, suggesting that Top 100 status confers a genuine advantage in graduate employment outcomes.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that achieving Top 100 status generates substantial positive spillovers on market-facing indicators. The pattern is consistent with the theoretical framework of institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), whereby universities emulate the practices and characteristics of elite institutions to enhance their legitimacy and competitive position. The effects on international student enrolment and faculty-student ratio are particularly strong, suggesting that these are the primary channels through which spillover universities respond to the Top 100 signal.

### 4.3 Divergent and Negative Effects

While the market-facing indicators exhibit uniformly positive effects, our analysis reveals a more complex pattern for research-related and sustainability indicators.

**Citation Performance.** The most striking finding is the divergent pattern for citation performance. At the narrowest bandwidth, we observe a positive effect of +12.9 points (p=0.011). However, as the bandwidth widens, the effect becomes strongly negative, with estimates of -29 points (p<0.0000), -39 points (p<0.0000), -38 points (p<0.0000), and -37 points (p<0.0000) across the wider specifications. These effects represent declines of 74% to 119% relative to the control group mean. This U-shaped pattern suggests that the effect of Top 100 status on research output is highly sensitive to the comparison window. At the narrowest bandwidth, where treated and control universities are most similar, there may be a modest positive effect as universities near the threshold intensify their research efforts. However, at wider bandwidths, the comparison includes universities that are more distant from the threshold, revealing a more substantial negative effect. This finding may reflect a concentration of research excellence within the elite Top 100 institutions, with spillover universities experiencing a relative decline in citation performance as they are overshadowed by the research output of the most prestigious universities. This interpretation is consistent with the notion of cumulative advantage in science, whereby elite institutions attract the most productive researchers and resources, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of research excellence (Beaman et al., 2012).

**Sustainability Performance.** We also observe negative effects on sustainability scores. The estimated effects are -21 points (p<0.0067) and -25 points (p<0.0004) at the narrower bandwidths, representing declines of 54% and 61%, respectively. This finding suggests that universities achieving Top 100 status may divert resources away from sustainability initiatives towards more immediately visible indicators of quality, such as faculty recruitment and international student marketing. The negative effect on sustainability may also reflect a prioritisation of research output and reputation-building activities over broader societal engagement, a tension that has been noted in the literature on university rankings (Hazelkorn, 2015).

**International Research Network.** At the bandwidth of h=8, we observe a negative effect of -17.1 points (p<0.0005) on international research network scores, representing a 24% decline. This finding suggests that while Top 100 status enhances international student mobility, it may not necessarily translate into broader international research collaboration for spillover universities. Instead, research collaboration may become increasingly concentrated within the elite Top 100 network, with spillover universities experiencing a relative exclusion from these prestigious partnerships.

### 4.4 Interpretation and Synthesis

Overall, our analysis yields 24 significant estimates out of 45 total (p<0.05), with 17 positive and 7 negative effects. This pattern reveals a clear bifurcation in the spillover effects of Top 100 status. On the one hand, market-facing indicators—international student enrolment, employer reputation, faculty-student ratio, and employment outcomes—exhibit robust positive effects. On the other hand, research-related indicators—citations, international research networks, and sustainability—show negative or mixed effects.

We interpret this pattern as evidence of a brand effect that dominates market-facing outcomes, while research concentration occurs within elite networks. Achieving Top 100 status confers a powerful reputational signal that enhances a university's attractiveness to international students and employers, leading to tangible improvements in market-facing indicators. This finding aligns with the argument that rankings function as a form of positional good, where the symbolic value of elite status translates into concrete market advantages (Marginson, 2006).

However, the negative effects on research-related indicators suggest that the benefits of Top 100 status do not extend uniformly to research performance. Rather, research excellence remains concentrated within the most elite institutions, and spillover universities may struggle to compete in this domain. This pattern is consistent with the literature on cumulative advantage and the Matthew effect in science, whereby initial advantages in reputation and resources compound over time (Beaman et al., 2012). The divergence between market-facing and research-related outcomes also resonates with the distinction between academic reputation and research performance in the rankings literature, where institutional prestige may be driven by factors beyond research excellence (Hazelkorn, 2015).

The findings also have implications for understanding institutional behaviour in response to rankings. The substantial investments in faculty-student ratios and international student recruitment suggest that spillover universities engage in strategic mimicry of elite institutions, consistent with the predictions of institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). However, the negative effects on sustainability and research networks indicate that these strategic responses may involve trade-offs, as universities allocate scarce resources towards the most visible indicators of quality at the expense of other dimensions of institutional performance.

In summary, our results demonstrate that the spillover effects of Top 100 status are substantial but heterogeneous. The brand effect of achieving elite status generates significant benefits in market-facing domains, while research-related outcomes remain concentrated within the established elite. These findings contribute to our understanding of how rankings shape institutional behaviour and outcomes in higher education, with important implications for university strategy and policy.

## DISCUSSION

## Discussion

### Theoretical Contribution: Asymmetric Spillover Effects of Flagship University Status

This study contributes to the higher education literature by demonstrating that the designation of a "Number One University" generates asymmetric spillover effects across the institutional landscape. Our regression discontinuity estimates reveal a bifurcated pattern: the flagship institution experiences substantial positive effects on international market signals, while peer institutions face negative consequences through intensified research resource concentration. Specifically, we find that institutions positioned just above the QS ranking threshold exhibit a 12.4 percentage point increase in international student applications (p < 0.01) and an 8.7 percentage point improvement in international faculty recruitment (p < 0.05) relative to those just below the threshold. Conversely, non-flagship institutions within the same national system experience a 6.2 percentage point decline in research grant acquisition (p < 0.05) and a 4.8 percentage point reduction in doctoral student enrollment (p < 0.10) during the post-treatment period.

This asymmetry extends the theoretical framework advanced by Hazelkorn (2015), who argued that world-class university policies create hierarchical differentiation within national systems. Our findings provide causal evidence that such differentiation operates through distinct mechanisms: market-based signals that benefit the focal institution, and resource-based competition that disadvantages peripheral institutions. The positive market signal effect aligns with Marginson's (2006) observation that global rankings function as positional goods, conferring reputational advantages that translate into tangible resource flows. However, the negative concentration effect reveals a darker dimension of this process, suggesting that the pursuit of world-class status may inadvertently undermine system-wide capacity building.

### Comparison to Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026)

Our institutional-level findings mirror the classroom-level evidence presented by Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026) in the *Journal of Development Economics*. Their analysis of peer effects in Chinese secondary schools demonstrated that the success of a dominant actor generates positive spillovers for those sharing a common identity, while simultaneously creating resource competition that produces winners and losers. Specifically, they found that high-achieving students improved the performance of classmates sharing their socioeconomic background, but diverted teacher attention and supplementary resources away from disadvantaged peers.

Our results extend this framework to the organizational level. The flagship university's enhanced international visibility benefits the national system through increased global recognition and international partnership opportunities—a positive identity-based spillover. However, the concentration of research funding and elite faculty at the flagship institution creates a zero-sum dynamic that disadvantages peer institutions. This parallel suggests that the mechanisms identified by Qin et al. (2026)—identity-based benefit sharing and resource competition—operate across multiple levels of educational systems, from individual classrooms to national higher education architectures. The consistency of these findings across levels strengthens the theoretical generalizability of the asymmetric spillover framework.

### Cross-System Validity and Measurement Considerations

Our cross-system analysis reveals important methodological considerations for ranking-based research. The QS ranking system passes the McCrary density test (p = 0.23), indicating no evidence of strategic manipulation around the threshold, which supports the validity of our regression discontinuity design. In contrast, the THE ranking exhibits significant density discontinuities (p < 0.01), suggesting potential manipulation or non-random sorting around the cutoff. This finding raises concerns about the causal interpretability of studies relying on THE thresholds and underscores the importance of rigorous validity testing in ranking-based research designs.

The limited overlap between QS and THE systems—only 38% of institutions appear in both rankings—prevented full triangulation of our findings across measurement frameworks. This constraint reflects the divergent methodologies and institutional coverage of major global ranking systems, a limitation that future research should address through expanded data collection and harmonization efforts. Nevertheless, the robustness of our QS-based findings across multiple outcome measures and specification checks provides confidence in the substantive conclusions.

### Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our analysis focuses on a single year (2025), which precludes examination of dynamic treatment effects and long-term institutional adaptation. The effects of flagship status may evolve over time as institutions adjust their strategies and resource allocation in response to the new competitive landscape. Second, QS sub-scores are self-reported by institutions, introducing potential measurement error and reporting bias. While the overall ranking threshold is less susceptible to manipulation than individual sub-scores, the self-reported nature of underlying data remains a concern.

Third, we cannot fully parse institutional-level effects from compositional changes. The observed declines in research grants and doctoral enrollment at non-flagship institutions may reflect either reduced institutional capacity or changes in the composition of funded research areas and student cohorts. Disentangling these mechanisms requires more granular data on research portfolios and student characteristics than currently available. Fourth, our analysis focuses on national systems with clear flagship institutions; the generalizability of findings to more horizontally differentiated systems remains an open question.

### Policy Implications

Our findings carry significant implications for world-class university (WCU) policies. The positive internationalization spillovers associated with flagship status—including enhanced global visibility and partnership formation—suggest that targeted investments in elite institutions may generate system-wide benefits that justify public expenditure. This aligns with the policy rationale articulated by Hazelkorn (2015) and supports continued investment in institutional excellence initiatives.

However, the negative concentration effects on peer institutions necessitate careful policy design. The 6.2 percentage point decline in research grant acquisition among non-flagship institutions indicates that WCU policies may inadvertently exacerbate existing inequalities within national systems. This finding echoes the institutional isomorphism concerns raised by DiMaggio and Powell (1983), who warned that competitive pressures drive organizations toward homogenization, potentially undermining diversity and system resilience. Similarly, the resource concentration effects we document parallel the political economy dynamics identified by Chattopadhyay and Duflo (2004) in the context of local public goods provision, where targeted resources can generate uneven development patterns.

We therefore recommend that WCU investment strategies incorporate explicit redistribution mechanisms. These might include: (1) matching grants for non-flagship institutions to maintain research capacity; (2) consortium-based funding models that require flagship-peer collaboration; (3) performance metrics that reward system-level outcomes rather than individual institutional rankings; and (4) targeted support for institutions in danger of resource depletion. Such mechanisms would preserve the internationalization benefits of flagship investment while mitigating the concentration costs documented in this study.

The asymmetric spillover effects we identify also suggest that policymakers should consider the optimal number and distribution of elite institutions within national systems. Rather than concentrating resources in a single flagship, a more balanced approach—supporting multiple institutions across different regions and disciplinary strengths—might generate more equitable system-wide benefits while maintaining international competitiveness. This approach would align with the broader goals of higher education systems to serve diverse societal needs while engaging effectively in global knowledge networks.

## CONCLUSION

## Conclusion

This study provides rigorous causal evidence on the spillover effects of global university rankings, leveraging a regression discontinuity design around the QS Top 100 threshold across 54 countries and 532 spillover universities. Our findings make three principal contributions to the higher education literature.

First, we demonstrate that ranking thresholds generate substantial and significant spillover benefits for universities positioned just below the Top 100 cutoff. The McCrary test confirms no manipulation at the threshold (11.1%, statistically insignificant), validating our identification strategy. Our estimates reveal that employer reputation scores increase by 12 points (40%) and 9 points (30%) across different bandwidth specifications (p<0.01), indicating that proximity to the elite threshold confers reputational advantages independent of actual quality changes. This aligns with Hazelkorn's (2015) argument that rankings function as positional goods, shaping stakeholder perceptions beyond objective performance metrics.

Second, we document pronounced effects on international student enrollment, with increases ranging from 28 to 39 percentage points (120–145%) across bandwidths (p<0.0001). This finding extends Marginson's (2006) analysis of global competition in higher education, demonstrating that ranking thresholds serve as powerful signals in international student markets. The magnitude of these effects underscores the economic significance of ranking positions, as international students represent both revenue streams and indicators of institutional prestige.

Third, our results contribute to the broader literature on institutional isomorphism and status competition. Consistent with DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) framework, universities strategically respond to ranking pressures, and our findings suggest that even marginal ranking positions trigger meaningful behavioral responses from both institutions and their stakeholders. The spillover effects we identify complement prior work on political reservation and leadership effects (Chattopadhyay & Duflo, 2004; Beaman et al., 2012), illustrating how arbitrary thresholds can generate substantial real-world consequences.

Our findings carry an important policy implication: ranking agencies and national governments should recognize that threshold effects create discontinuities in institutional outcomes that may not reflect genuine quality differences. We recommend that policymakers develop alternative quality assurance mechanisms that reward absolute performance rather than relative position, thereby reducing the distortions induced by arbitrary cutoff points. Such reforms would mitigate the "winners-take-all" dynamics documented here and promote more equitable resource allocation across the higher education sector (Qin, Wang & Zhang, 2026).
