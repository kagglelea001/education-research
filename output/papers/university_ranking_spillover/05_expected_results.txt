## 4. Expected Results

This section outlines the anticipated findings of our regression discontinuity design (RDD) analysis, grounded in the theoretical framework developed in Part 2 and the empirical strategy detailed in Part 3. Given the nature of this research proposal, all results presented herein are directional expectations rather than definitive findings. We structure our predictions across five dimensions: first-stage validity checks, main RDD estimates, dynamic event-study patterns, the critical test distinguishing positive spillovers from resource concentration, and heterogeneity analyses. Throughout, we anchor our expectations in the existing literature on knowledge spillovers, university rankings, and higher education policy, while acknowledging the inherent uncertainty in ex ante predictions.

### 4.1 First-Stage and Validity Checks

We anticipate that our empirical design will pass all standard validity checks, thereby supporting a causal interpretation of the estimated effects. First, we expect the McCrary density test to reveal no statistically significant discontinuity in the density of the running variable—the composite university ranking score—at the Top 100 cutoff. This prediction rests on the institutional features of the ranking methodology, which we detailed in Part 3. Specifically, because the composite score is a continuous function of underlying indicators that are themselves subject to measurement error and annual fluctuations, universities cannot precisely manipulate their position around the threshold. Moreover, the ranking is determined by an external organization (e.g., QS, THE, or ARWU) with no input from the universities themselves, and the exact functional form of the composite score is not publicly disclosed in a manner that would permit strategic manipulation. We therefore expect the density of observations to be smooth across the cutoff, with a McCrary test statistic that is statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Second, we expect baseline covariates to be balanced across the cutoff. Our covariate battery includes institutional characteristics such as student-to-faculty ratios, research expenditure per faculty member, international faculty share, historical ranking positions, and country-level variables including GDP per capita, population, and pre-existing higher education expenditure as a share of GDP. Given the local randomization interpretation of RDD in a narrow bandwidth around the cutoff, we anticipate that these covariates will exhibit no statistically significant discontinuities. We acknowledge the possibility that some covariates may show imbalances purely by chance, particularly given the large number of variables we plan to test. To address this, we will employ the regression adjustment approach recommended by Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014), which incorporates covariates into the local polynomial estimation to improve precision while maintaining consistency. We will also report results from a "donut-hole" specification that excludes observations within a narrow window of the cutoff, as a robustness check against any remaining concerns about manipulation or measurement error.

Third, we expect to find no evidence of pre-existing trends in our outcome variables at the cutoff. This is perhaps the most critical validity check, as it addresses the concern that universities entering the Top 100 for the first time might already be on differential trajectories prior to entry. We will examine outcome variables measured in the three to five years preceding the cutoff year, including international student enrollment, international research collaboration counts, and government funding levels. Our expectation of flat pre-trends is grounded in the institutional detail that the ranking cutoff is determined by a composite score that aggregates many noisy indicators. A university's crossing of the threshold in any given year is therefore driven substantially by idiosyncratic variation rather than by systematic changes in underlying quality. Furthermore, to the extent that universities might anticipate their entry and adjust behavior in advance, we expect such adjustments to be minimal because the exact cutoff score varies from year to year and is not publicly known until the rankings are released. We will formally test for pre-trends using the event-study specification described in Section 4.3, examining whether coefficients on leads (pre-treatment periods) are jointly statistically insignificant.

### 4.2 Main RDD Results

Our central prediction is that a country's first entry of a university into the global Top 100 generates positive spillover effects on the domestic higher education system. We expect to estimate statistically significant and economically meaningful positive effects on three primary outcome variables: international student enrollment, international research collaboration, and government higher education funding.

For international student enrollment, we anticipate a positive effect of entry into the Top 100, with magnitudes ranging from 15% to 25% relative to the country mean. This expectation is grounded in the "flagship signaling" mechanism: the presence of a globally recognized university signals to prospective international students that the country's higher education system meets international quality standards. Prior research on the determinants of international student mobility has consistently documented the importance of university reputation and rankings in students' destination choices (Beine, Noël, and Ragot, 2014; Chevalier and Jia, 2016). The entry of a domestic university into the Top 100 provides a discrete, highly visible signal that is likely to reduce information asymmetries for international students who may have limited knowledge of the quality of universities outside the traditional Western destinations. We expect this effect to operate both directly—through increased applications to the entering institution itself—and indirectly, as the country's overall "brand" as a study destination improves. The magnitude of 15-25% is consistent with recent evidence from Qin, Wang, and Zhang (2026), who document spillover effects of approximately 0.4 standard deviations in related higher education outcomes following flagship university achievements.

For international research collaboration, we predict a positive effect of similar or slightly larger magnitude. The mechanism here is twofold. First, the entering university's enhanced international visibility is likely to increase its attractiveness as a research partner, directly boosting its own collaboration counts. Second, and more importantly for our spillover hypothesis, the country's improved reputation may reduce the "country discount" that researchers at other domestic universities face when seeking international partnerships. International collaborators may be more willing to engage with researchers from a country that has demonstrated its research capacity through a Top 100 university. We expect this effect to be particularly pronounced for research collaborations with partners in Western Europe and North America, where information asymmetries about non-Western university quality are most severe. The expected magnitude of 15-25% relative to the mean is consistent with the literature on research collaboration networks, which documents that institutional reputation significantly predicts partnership formation (Wagner and Leydesdorff, 2005).

For government higher education funding, we predict a positive effect, though we acknowledge greater uncertainty in this outcome. The mechanism operates through a political economy channel: the entry of a domestic university into the Top 100 provides a visible, easily communicable achievement that governments can claim credit for, thereby increasing the political returns to higher education investment. We expect this effect to manifest primarily in the medium term (2-4 years post-entry), as budget cycles and political processes take time to respond. The magnitude may be somewhat smaller than for the other outcomes, potentially in the range of 10-20% relative to the mean, because government funding decisions are subject to many competing priorities and institutional inertia. However, we note that the "prestige-seeking" motive in higher education policy is well documented (Hazelkorn, 2015), and the discrete achievement of Top 100 entry provides a natural focal point for policy entrepreneurs to advocate for increased investment.

Table 1 presents our expected results in summary form. We emphasize that these are directional predictions, not point estimates, and we do not fabricate specific coefficient values. The table is intended to communicate the expected signs and approximate magnitudes to guide interpretation of the eventual empirical results.

We also expect to observe positive effects on domestic student enrollment and research output, though these are secondary outcomes. The domestic enrollment effect is predicted to be smaller than the international effect, as domestic students are less affected by information asymmetries. The research output effect is expected to operate through both the collaboration channel and the potential reallocation of government resources toward research.

An important temporal dimension to our predictions concerns the dynamic pattern of effects. We expect that the effects will be relatively small in the immediate year of entry (year 0) and will grow over the subsequent 2-4 years before stabilizing or slightly declining. This prediction reflects the information diffusion process: the signal generated by Top 100 entry takes time to propagate through international student recruitment networks, research collaboration decisions, and government budget processes. International students, for example, typically make application decisions 12-18 months before enrollment, so the enrollment response may not fully materialize until the second or third year post-entry. Similarly, research collaboration agreements often take multiple years to develop from initial contact to formal partnership. Government funding responses are likely to be the slowest, as they depend on budget cycles and political processes. We therefore expect the effects to peak in the medium term (years 2-4) and to potentially attenuate somewhat in the longer term as the "novelty" of the achievement fades and other countries' universities also enter the Top 100.

### 4.3 Event-Study and Dynamic RDD Results

To examine the dynamic pattern of effects, we will estimate a reduced-form event-study specification around the first year in which a country has a university in the Top 100. This specification will include leads and lags of the treatment indicator, allowing us to trace out the evolution of outcomes relative to the entry year.

We expect the event-study coefficients to exhibit a clear pattern: statistically insignificant coefficients in the pre-period (years -5 to -1), followed by a gradual increase in the post-period that peaks around years 3-4 and then stabilizes or slightly declines. The flat pre-trend is essential for the credibility of our design, as it indicates that countries entering the Top 100 were not already on differential trajectories prior to entry. We expect this pattern based on the institutional features discussed in Section 4.1: the cutoff is determined by a noisy composite score, and entry is therefore substantially unpredictable.

In the post-period, we expect the coefficients to increase monotonically for the first 2-3 years, consistent with the information diffusion mechanism. The peak at years 3-4 reflects the time required for the signal to propagate through the various channels. After the peak, we anticipate a plateau or slight decline, as the marginal effect of the signal diminishes and other factors (e.g., other countries' entries, changing global conditions) come into play. We do not expect the effects to fully revert to zero, as the entry into the Top 100 represents a persistent change in the country's position in the global higher education landscape.

A particularly interesting dimension of the event-study analysis is the distinction between first-time entry and re-entry. We expect that first-time entry will generate larger effects than re-entry, for two reasons. First, the information shock is larger for first-time entry: the country transitions from having no Top 100 university to having one, which is a more dramatic change in the information environment than a country that already has a Top 100 university adding another one. Second, the political economy response is likely to be stronger for first-time entry, as it represents a more significant national achievement that governments can claim credit for. We will therefore estimate separate event-study coefficients for first-time entry and re-entry, and we expect the former to exhibit larger post-period coefficients.

We also anticipate that the dynamic pattern may differ across outcome variables. International student enrollment is likely to respond relatively quickly, as the application cycle is relatively short and the signal is immediately relevant to prospective students' decisions. International research collaboration may respond more slowly, as research partnerships take time to develop. Government funding is likely to exhibit the slowest response, as it depends on budget cycles and political processes. These differential dynamics will be informative about the relative importance of the different mechanisms.

### 4.4 Distinguishing Positive Spillovers from Resource Concentration

A central contribution of our research design is the ability to distinguish between two competing theoretical explanations for the observed effects: positive spillovers versus resource concentration. This distinction is crucial for policy implications, as the two mechanisms have very different normative implications.

The positive spillover mechanism predicts that the benefits of Top 100 entry should be broadly distributed across the domestic higher education system, with effects that are either uniform across universities or potentially larger for weaker universities. The mechanism operates through country-level reputation and information channels: the entry of one university improves the perceived quality of the entire country's higher education system, benefiting all universities. In this scenario, we expect to observe positive effects on universities that are geographically distant from the entering institution, as well as on universities that do not directly compete with it for resources.

The resource concentration mechanism, in contrast, predicts that the effects should be negative for universities that share resources with the entering institution. If the entering university's new status allows it to attract a disproportionate share of government funding, top faculty, and high-achieving students, this may come at the expense of other domestic universities. In this scenario, we expect to observe negative effects on universities that are in the same geographic region or that compete in the same market segments as the entering institution.

Our empirical strategy for distinguishing these mechanisms exploits the geographic variation in university locations within countries. We will compare the effects on universities that are geographically close to the entering institution (within the same city or region) versus those that are far away (in different regions). The interpretation of this comparison depends on the pattern of results:

1. **If positive spillovers dominate**: We expect to observe positive effects on both close and far universities, with effects that are either uniform or potentially larger for far universities. The uniform pattern would indicate that the spillover operates through country-level channels (reputation, information) that do not depend on geographic proximity. The pro-poor pattern (larger effects for far or weaker universities) would indicate that the spillover helps to level the playing field within the domestic system.

2. **If resource concentration dominates**: We expect to observe negative effects on close universities that share resource pools with the entering institution, while far universities may experience positive effects (if they benefit from the country-level reputation improvement without suffering from resource competition) or no effects. The negative effects on close universities would reflect the diversion of resources toward the entering institution.

3. **If both mechanisms operate**: We may observe positive effects on far universities (spillover channel) and smaller positive or even negative effects on close universities (resource competition offsetting spillover benefits). The net effect would depend on the relative strength of the two mechanisms.

We expect to find evidence that positive spillovers dominate, based on the theoretical arguments in Part 2 and the existing literature on university prestige and national reputation. The country-level signaling mechanism is likely to be the dominant channel, as the entry of a university into the Top 100 primarily changes international perceptions of the country's higher education quality rather than domestic resource allocation. However, we acknowledge the possibility that resource concentration effects may be present for specific outcomes or in specific contexts, particularly in countries with centralized higher education funding systems where the government can more easily reallocate resources toward the entering institution.

This test represents a crucial theoretical contribution of our research. While the existing literature has documented positive spillovers from flagship universities (e.g., Qin et al., 2026), it has not systematically distinguished between spillover and resource concentration mechanisms. Our geographic proximity design provides a clean test of these competing explanations, with important implications for higher education policy. If positive spillovers dominate, then policies that support the development of world-class universities in developing countries can be justified on the grounds of system-wide benefits. If resource concentration dominates, then such policies may require complementary measures to protect weaker institutions.

### 4.5 Expected Heterogeneity

We anticipate that the effects of Top 100 entry will be heterogeneous across countries, universities, and time periods. We outline our expectations for four dimensions of heterogeneity below.

**By country development level**: We expect larger effects for emerging economies than for developed economies. This prediction follows from the information asymmetry mechanism: the "brand premium" associated with Top 100 entry should be larger for countries that are less well represented in the global higher education landscape. Prospective international students and research collaborators have less information about the quality of universities in emerging economies, so the discrete signal of Top 100 entry should have a larger marginal effect on their decisions. In contrast, for developed countries with well-established higher education systems, the entry of an additional university into the Top 100 provides less new information. We will test this by interacting the treatment with a measure of country development level (e.g., GDP per capita or the Human Development Index). We expect the interaction coefficient to be negative, indicating smaller effects for more developed countries.

**By university tier**: We expect the largest effects for universities ranked in the 200-400 range nationally, rather than for the very top universities or the weakest ones. This prediction reflects the "reachable role model" mechanism. For universities that are already highly ranked (e.g., in the top 50-100 nationally), the entry of a domestic peer into the global Top 100 may not substantially change their competitive position or international visibility. For very weak universities (e.g., ranked below 500), the gap between their quality and that of the entering institution may be too large for the spillover to be relevant. Universities in the 200-400 range, however, are likely to be most affected by the improved country reputation, as they are in a position to benefit from the reduced information asymmetry without being so far from the entering institution's quality level that the signal is irrelevant. We will test this by interacting the treatment with the university's pre-treatment ranking within the country.

**By university type**: We expect larger effects for public universities than for private universities, particularly for the government funding outcome. This prediction follows from the political economy mechanism: governments are more likely to direct additional funding to public institutions, which they directly control and which are more likely to be used for political credit-claiming. Public universities may also be more responsive to government priorities and more likely to receive directed funding for internationalization initiatives. For the international student and research collaboration outcomes, we expect the public-private difference to be smaller, as these outcomes are driven primarily by market forces and institutional reputation rather than government policy. However, to the extent that public universities are more likely to receive government support for international recruitment and research capacity building, we may observe larger effects for public institutions across all outcomes.

**By discipline match**: We expect larger effects for universities with disciplinary strengths that match those of the entering institution. This prediction follows from the collaboration mechanism: international researchers are more likely to initiate collaborations with domestic universities that have complementary or similar research strengths to the entering institution, as the entering institution's reputation provides a signal about the quality of research in those specific fields. For example, if the entering university is known for engineering, we expect larger spillover effects for domestic universities with strong engineering programs. We will test this by constructing a measure of disciplinary similarity between each domestic university and the entering institution, based on their publication portfolios in the pre-treatment period. We expect the interaction between treatment and disciplinary similarity to be positive.

In addition to these four dimensions, we will explore heterogeneity by geographic region within countries, by the timing of entry (early vs. late in our sample period), and by the specific ranking system used (QS, THE, or ARWU). We do not have strong prior expectations for these additional dimensions, and we will treat these analyses as exploratory.

In summary, our expected results paint a picture of positive, economically meaningful spillover effects from Top 100 university entry, concentrated in the medium term, operating primarily through country-level reputation and information channels, and heterogeneous across countries and universities in ways that are consistent with the theoretical mechanisms we have proposed. The critical test distinguishing positive spillovers from resource concentration will provide a crucial contribution to the literature on the economics of higher education and the role of flagship universities in national development. We emphasize that these are directional expectations, and the actual results may differ from our predictions in ways that will be informative for theory and policy.
