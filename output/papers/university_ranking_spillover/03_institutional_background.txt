## 2. Institutional Background

### 2.1 The QS World University Rankings: Construction and Measurement Error

The QS World University Rankings (QS WUR), first published in 2004, have emerged as one of the most influential global university ranking systems, alongside the Times Higher Education (THE) and Shanghai Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU). The QS methodology combines six weighted indicators to produce a composite score for each institution: Academic Reputation (40%), Employer Reputation (10%), Faculty/Student Ratio (20%), Citations per Faculty (20%), International Faculty Ratio (5%), and International Student Ratio (5%). The two reputation components are derived from large-scale global surveys—QS solicits responses from over 100,000 academics and 50,000 employers annually—while the remaining indicators are constructed from institutional submissions and bibliometric data from Scopus.

Three features of this construction generate substantial measurement noise in the composite score, particularly near the Top 100 threshold. First, the reputation surveys introduce non-classical measurement error. Response rates vary systematically across regions and disciplines, and respondents often rank institutions based on historical prestige rather than contemporaneous quality (Marginson, 2014). This "halo effect" means that reputation scores exhibit serial correlation and mean-reversion dynamics that are poorly approximated by classical measurement error assumptions. Second, the Faculty/Student Ratio indicator is notoriously sensitive to how institutions define "faculty" and "students"—part-time appointments, research-only staff, and distance-learning enrollments are treated heterogeneously across institutions, creating non-comparability in the raw inputs. Third, the Citations per Faculty indicator uses a five-year citation window with field normalization, but the normalization procedure is opaque and has been criticized for favoring large, English-language institutions in biomedical fields (Saisana & D'Hombres, 2008). The composite score is thus a noisy signal of underlying institutional quality, with the noise component being particularly large for institutions clustered around the 90–110 rank range, where small perturbations in any single indicator can shift an institution across the threshold.

### 2.2 The Salience of the Top 100 Threshold

The Top 100 threshold possesses exceptional salience in global higher education policy discourse. Unlike other cutoffs (e.g., Top 50 or Top 200), the Top 100 has become a focal point for media coverage, institutional strategic planning, and—critically for identification—explicit government policy targets. A substantial body of policy documents reveals that national governments in both developed and emerging economies have codified Top 100 membership as a formal policy objective.

China's Double First-Class University Plan (2017) explicitly requires participating universities to achieve "world-class" status, with the Ministry of Education using QS and THE rankings as reference metrics for performance evaluation. Japan's Top Global University Project (2014) similarly designates 37 universities with the stated goal of placing "at least 10 Japanese universities in the Top 100" of global rankings. Russia's 5-100 Project (2013–2020) allocated substantial federal funding with the explicit objective of propelling at least five Russian universities into the Top 100 by 2020. Germany's Excellence Initiative (2005–2017) and its successor, the Excellence Strategy (2019–present), while not explicitly referencing QS, have been widely interpreted by university leadership as targeting Top 100 placement. Similar policy frameworks exist in France (Initiatives d'Excellence), South Korea (Brain Korea 21), and Saudi Arabia (Vision 2030).

This policy salience generates a sharp discontinuity in institutional incentives at the Top 100 boundary. Universities just below the threshold face strong pressure to improve their ranking position, often through strategic resource allocation, international recruitment drives, and targeted publication strategies. Universities just above the threshold face incentives to maintain their position. The policy relevance of the threshold thus creates a natural experiment: institutions on either side of the cutoff experience differential treatment in terms of government funding, media attention, and student demand, even though their underlying quality is locally continuous.

### 2.3 Methodological Changes as Exogenous Variation

The QS methodology has undergone substantial revisions since 2004, creating plausibly exogenous variation in scores near the threshold. The initial 2004–2009 period employed a relatively simple weighting scheme dominated by Academic Reputation (40%) and Faculty/Student Ratio (20%). In 2010, QS introduced the Citations per Faculty indicator (20%) and adjusted the reputation survey sampling frame. The 2015 revision modified the normalization procedure for citation counts, shifting from a raw count to a field-normalized measure. The most consequential changes occurred in 2023, when QS added three new indicators—Employment Outcomes (5%), Sustainability (5%), and International Research Network (5%)—while reducing the weights of Academic Reputation (from 40% to 30%) and Faculty/Student Ratio (from 20% to 15%).

These methodological changes generate two sources of exogenous variation. First, the reweighting of existing indicators alters the composite score without any change in institutional behavior. An institution that performed well on the newly emphasized indicators (e.g., Sustainability) experiences a mechanical upward shift in its score, potentially crossing the Top 100 threshold. Second, the addition of new indicators introduces entirely new dimensions of measurement that institutions could not have strategically targeted in advance. The 2023 addition of Sustainability—an indicator for which few institutions had developed systematic data collection—created substantial reordering of institutions near the threshold. From an identification perspective, these methodological changes provide a source of variation in treatment status that is orthogonal to institutional quality, analogous to a "natural experiment" in the policy evaluation literature.

### 2.4 The "Winner's Curse" and Theoretical Ambiguity

The literature on university rankings has identified a "winner's curse" phenomenon whereby ranking success generates both positive and negative externalities. On the positive side, Top 100 membership enhances institutional prestige, increases applications from high-ability students, and may improve faculty recruitment and retention (Bowman & Bastedo, 2009). This "brand effect" could generate positive spillovers to neighboring institutions through increased international visibility of the host country's higher education system, or through knowledge spillovers from enhanced research collaboration.

On the negative side, ranking success may concentrate resources and talent at the ranked institution, crowding out neighboring universities. This "resource concentration" channel operates through multiple mechanisms: high-ability students redirect their applications toward the ranked institution, government funding becomes increasingly concentrated in "world-class" universities, and faculty mobility increases toward the ranked institution. The net effect on neighboring institutions is theoretically ambiguous. If the brand effect dominates, we would observe positive spillovers; if resource concentration dominates, we would observe negative spillovers. This ambiguity motivates the empirical analysis: the sign and magnitude of spillover effects are ultimately an empirical question that our RDD design is well-suited to answer.

### 2.5 Institutional Features of National Higher Education Systems

The identification strategy exploits variation in the number of Top 100 universities across countries and over time. As of 2024, the United States has 27 Top 100 universities, the United Kingdom has 17, Australia has 9, China has 6, Germany has 4, and Japan has 3. The remaining Top 100 slots are distributed across approximately 20 other countries, with most having 1–2 institutions. This cross-country variation in the "density" of Top 100 institutions is central to our identification: countries with multiple Top 100 universities provide richer opportunities for spillover analysis, while countries with a single Top 100 institution allow us to examine the effects of a "monopoly" ranked institution.

The typical distance in QS scores between top institutions within a country varies substantially. In the United States, the gap between the 1st and 27th ranked institution exceeds 30 points, while in smaller systems (e.g., Switzerland, with 2 Top 100 institutions), the gap may be less than 5 points. This variation in score dispersion affects the likelihood that a given institution crosses the threshold in any particular year and the intensity of competition among domestic institutions. Countries also differ in their historical trajectory: some (e.g., China, Russia) have experienced rapid increases in Top 100 representation, while others (e.g., Japan) have seen declines. This temporal variation, combined with the QS methodology changes, provides multiple sources of exogenous variation that we exploit in the empirical design.
